1. Introduction to Statistics

Statistics is the branch of mathematics concerned with collecting, organizing, presenting, analyzing and interpreting data.

Whenever information is collected in the form of numbers, statistics can help us understand what those numbers mean.

Descriptive Statistics

Describes data using tables, graphs, averages and measures of spread.

Inferential Statistics

Uses information from a sample to make conclusions about a larger population.

Important: Statistics is not only about mean, median and mode. Understanding the distribution and spread of data is equally important.

2. Data and Types of Data

Data are facts, measurements or observations collected for analysis.

Qualitative Data

Qualitative data describe qualities or categories rather than numerical measurements.

Examples include gender, colour, occupation and type of food.

Quantitative Data

Quantitative data are numerical and can be counted or measured.

Examples include age, height, examination scores and number of students.

Discrete Data

Discrete data consist of separate countable values.

Example: number of children in a family.

Continuous Data

Continuous data can take any value within an interval.

Example: height, weight and temperature.

3. Frequency Distribution

Frequency tells us how many times a particular value occurs.

Example

Consider the data: 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5.

Value Frequency
2 1
3 2
4 3
5 2
Total frequency = 1 + 2 + 3 + 2 = 8.
Cumulative Frequency

Cumulative frequency is the running total of frequencies.

Cumulative frequency = previous cumulative frequency + current frequency

4. Grouped Data

When a data set contains many observations, the values can be grouped into class intervals.

Class Interval

A class interval is a range of values belonging to the same group.

For example:

10–19, 20–29, 30–39, 40–49
Class Width

The class width represents the size of the interval.

Class Boundaries

For continuous grouping, class boundaries remove gaps between classes.

For example, the class 10–19 can have boundaries:

9.5 and 19.5
Example

Suppose students' scores are grouped as:

Score Frequency
0–9 3
10–19 7
20–29 10
30–39 6

5. Arithmetic Mean

The arithmetic mean is commonly called the average. It is obtained by adding all observations and dividing by the number of observations.

Mean = Σx / n
Example 1: Ungrouped Data

Find the mean of 4, 6, 8, 10 and 12.

Sum = 4 + 6 + 8 + 10 + 12 = 40

Number of observations = 5

Mean = 40 / 5 = 8
Mean = 8
Mean of Frequency Data
Mean = Σfx / Σf

Here, x is the value and f is its frequency.

Example 2

Values: 2, 4, 6

Frequencies: 3, 2, 5

Calculate fx:

2 × 3 = 6

4 × 2 = 8

6 × 5 = 30

Therefore:

Σfx = 44
Σf = 10
Mean = 44 / 10 = 4.4

6. Assumed Mean Method

The assumed mean method provides a quicker way of calculating the mean, especially when the numbers are large.

Choose a convenient value as the assumed mean, usually near the middle of the data.

d = x − A
Mean = A + Σfd / Σf
Example

Suppose the values are 20, 30, 40 and 50 with frequencies 2, 3, 4 and 1.

Take assumed mean A = 40.

d = x − A

Calculate each deviation, multiply by its frequency and obtain Σfd.

Finally use:

Mean = A + Σfd / Σf
Step-Deviation Method

When class widths are equal, deviations can be divided by the common class width.

u = (x − A) / h
Mean = A + h(Σfu / Σf)

7. Median

The median is the middle value when observations are arranged in ascending or descending order.

Odd Number of Observations
Median position = (n + 1) / 2
Example

Find the median of: 3, 5, 7, 9, 11.

There are five observations.

Median position = (5 + 1) / 2 = 3
Median = 7
Even Number of Observations

When there is an even number of observations, take the average of the two middle values.

Median = (middle value 1 + middle value 2) / 2
Grouped Data Median
Median = L + [(N/2 − F) / f]h

Where L is the lower class boundary of the median class, N is the total frequency, F is the cumulative frequency before the median class, f is the frequency of the median class and h is the class width.

8. Mode

The mode is the value that occurs most frequently.

Example

2, 3, 3, 4, 5, 3, 6

Mode = 3
Grouped Data Mode
Mode = L + [(f₁ − f₀) / (2f₁ − f₀ − f₂)]h

The modal class is the class with the highest frequency.

9. Quartiles

Quartiles divide ordered data into four equal parts.

Q₁

First quartile. Approximately 25% of observations lie below it.

Q₂

Second quartile. It is the median.

Q₃

Third quartile. Approximately 75% of observations lie below it.

IQR

Interquartile range measures the spread of the middle 50% of the data.

IQR = Q₃ − Q₁
Example

Suppose Q₁ = 12 and Q₃ = 30.

IQR = 30 − 12 = 18

10. Percentiles

Percentiles divide ordered data into one hundred parts.

For example, being at the 80th percentile means that the observation is greater than or equal to approximately 80% of the observations in the relevant distribution.

Percentile position = P(n + 1) / 100

Percentiles are particularly useful when comparing examination performance and ranking observations.

11. Range and Measures of Spread

Measures of central tendency tell us where the data are centred. Measures of dispersion tell us how spread out the observations are.

Range = Maximum − Minimum
Example

Data: 5, 8, 12, 15, 20.

Range = 20 − 5 = 15

12. Variance

Variance measures how far observations tend to lie from the mean by using squared deviations.

First calculate the mean. Then subtract the mean from each observation. Square each deviation and find the appropriate average.

Population variance: σ² = Σ(x − μ)² / N
Sample variance: s² = Σ(x − x̄)² / (n − 1)
Example

Consider: 2, 4, 6.

Mean = 4.

Deviations: −2, 0, 2.

Squared deviations: 4, 0, 4.

Sum of squared deviations = 8.

Population variance = 8 / 3

13. Standard Deviation

Standard deviation is the positive square root of variance. It tells us approximately how widely observations are spread around the mean.

σ = √[Σ(x − μ)² / N]
s = √[Σ(x − x̄)² / (n − 1)]
Understanding Standard Deviation

A small standard deviation means observations tend to be close to the mean.

A large standard deviation means observations are more widely spread.

Coefficient of Variation
CV = (Standard Deviation / Mean) × 100%

The coefficient of variation is useful when comparing the relative spread of different data sets.

14. Statistical Graphs and Charts

Bar Chart

A bar chart represents categorical or discrete data using separate rectangular bars.

Histogram

A histogram represents grouped continuous data. Unlike a bar chart, adjacent histogram bars normally touch.

Frequency Polygon

A frequency polygon is formed by joining points representing class frequencies, usually at class midpoints.

Pie Chart

A pie chart represents data as sectors of a circle.

Sector angle = (frequency / total frequency) × 360°
Example

If 20 out of 100 students prefer football:

Angle = (20 / 100) × 360°
Angle = 72°
Ogive

An ogive is a cumulative frequency curve. It can be used to estimate medians, quartiles and percentiles from grouped data.

Scatter Diagram

A scatter diagram displays paired observations and helps us investigate relationships between two variables.

15. Probability and Statistics

Probability measures how likely an event is to occur.

P(E) = Number of favourable outcomes / Total number of possible outcomes
Example

A fair die has six possible outcomes. What is the probability of rolling a 4?

P(4) = 1 / 6
Complementary Events
P(A') = 1 − P(A)
Independent Events
P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)

16. Statistics Word Problems

Statistics questions are often presented as real-life situations rather than direct formulas. The important skill is identifying the information given and deciding which statistical method is required.

Word Problem 1 — Mean

The scores of five students are 45, 52, 61, 70 and 72. Find their average score.

Add the scores:

45 + 52 + 61 + 70 + 72 = 300

There are five students.

Mean = 300 / 5 = 60
The average score is 60.
Word Problem 2 — Missing Value

The mean of five numbers is 18. Four of the numbers are 12, 15, 20 and 21. Find the fifth number.

Total = Mean × Number of observations
Total = 18 × 5 = 90

Sum of the known values:

12 + 15 + 20 + 21 = 68
Missing value = 90 − 68 = 22
The fifth number is 22.
Word Problem 3 — Frequency Mean

The following table gives the number of books read by students and their frequencies.

Books (x) Frequency (f)
1 4
2 7
3 5
4 4

Calculate fx for each row and then use:

Mean = Σfx / Σf
Word Problem 4 — Comparing Data Sets

Two classes have the same mean examination score, but Class A has a much smaller standard deviation than Class B. What does this tell us?

The scores in Class A are more closely concentrated around the mean, while the scores in Class B are more widely spread.
Exam tip: Do not immediately substitute numbers into a formula. First identify what the question is asking for: mean, median, mode, quartile, variance, standard deviation, range, frequency, probability or another measure.
