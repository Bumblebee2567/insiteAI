Probability

Probability is the branch of mathematics used to measure uncertainty and determine how likely an event is to occur. It is used in games, statistics, science, finance, weather forecasting and many real-life situations.

Basics Sample Space Events Rules Complement Addition Multiplication Independence Conditional Venn Diagrams Tree Diagrams Counting Permutations Combinations Experimental Distributions Expected Value Word Problems

1. Probability Basics

Probability tells us how likely something is to happen. Every probability must lie between 0 and 1 inclusive.

0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1

Probability can be expressed as a fraction, decimal or percentage.

Impossible Event

An impossible event is an event that cannot happen.

P(impossible event) = 0

Certain Event

A certain event is an event that must happen.

P(certain event) = 1

Likely and Unlikely Events

An event with a probability close to 1 is very likely, while an event with a probability close to 0 is unlikely.

Example:

A fair coin has two possible outcomes: Head and Tail. Since both outcomes are equally likely:

P(Head) = 1/2 = 0.5 = 50%

2. Sample Space and Outcomes

The sample space is the complete set of all possible outcomes of an experiment.

Coin Toss

S = {H, T}

Rolling a Die

S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}

Two Coin Tosses

S = {HH, HT, TH, TT}

Notice that the order matters when describing separate stages of an experiment. HT means Head followed by Tail, while TH means Tail followed by Head.

Example:

A fair die is rolled. What is the probability of getting an even number?

The possible outcomes are:

{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}

The favourable outcomes are 2, 4 and 6.

P(Even) = 3/6 = 1/2

3. Events

An event is a collection of one or more outcomes from a sample space.

Simple Event

A simple event contains only one outcome.

Rolling a 6 on a die is a simple event.

Compound Event

A compound event contains two or more outcomes.

Rolling an even number gives {2, 4, 6}.

Mutually Exclusive Events

Two events are mutually exclusive when they cannot happen at the same time.

When rolling a die, getting a 2 and getting a 5 on the same roll are mutually exclusive.

Exhaustive Events

A collection of events is exhaustive if together they contain every possible outcome.

P(S) = 1

4. Basic Probability Rules

When all outcomes are equally likely, probability can be calculated by comparing favourable outcomes with the total number of outcomes.

P(A) = Number of favourable outcomes / Total number of possible outcomes

Probability of the Entire Sample Space

P(S) = 1

Probability Cannot Be Negative

P(A) ≥ 0

Probability Cannot Exceed One

P(A) ≤ 1
Example:

A bag contains 5 red balls and 3 blue balls. Find the probability of selecting a red ball.

Total = 5 + 3 = 8

P(Red) = 5/8

5. Complementary Probability

The complement of an event is the event that the original event does not occur.

If A is an event, its complement is commonly written as A' or Aᶜ.

P(A') = 1 - P(A)
P(A) + P(A') = 1
Example:

The probability that a student passes an examination is 0.75. Find the probability that the student fails.

P(Fail) = 1 - P(Pass)
P(Fail) = 1 - 0.75
P(Fail) = 0.25

At Least One

Problems involving "at least one" can often be solved more easily using the complement.

P(at least one) = 1 - P(none)

6. Addition Rule of Probability

The addition rule is used when a question involves one event OR another event.

Mutually Exclusive Events

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B)
Example:

Find the probability of rolling either a 2 or a 5 on a fair die.

P(2 or 5) = 1/6 + 1/6 = 2/6 = 1/3

Non-Mutually Exclusive Events

If two events can occur together, their overlap is counted twice when we simply add their probabilities. Therefore, the intersection must be subtracted once.

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A ∩ B)
Example:

In a class, 20 students study Mathematics, 15 study Physics, and 8 study both.

The number who study at least one of the two subjects is:

20 + 15 - 8 = 27

7. Multiplication Rule

The multiplication rule is used when a problem involves event A AND event B.

P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B|A)

The notation P(B|A) means "the probability of B given that A has already occurred."

Independent Case

If A and B are independent, the occurrence of A does not change the probability of B.

P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)
Example:

A fair coin is tossed twice. Find the probability of getting two Heads.

P(HH) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4

8. Independent and Dependent Events

Independent Events

Two events are independent when the occurrence of one does not affect the probability of the other.

P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B)
Example:

Tossing a coin and rolling a die are independent experiments. The result of the coin toss does not affect the result of the die.

Dependent Events

Two events are dependent when the first event changes the probability of the second event.

Example:

A bag contains 4 red balls and 6 blue balls. Two balls are selected without replacement.

After the first ball is removed, the total number of balls changes. Therefore, the second probability depends on the first selection.

9. Conditional Probability

Conditional probability is the probability that an event occurs when we already know that another event has occurred.

P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)

The vertical bar means "given that."

Example:

Suppose 30 students are surveyed. 18 play football, while 10 of those football players also play basketball.

If we already know that a selected student plays football, the conditional probability that the student also plays basketball is:

P(Basketball | Football) = 10/18 = 5/9

Conditional Probability and Multiplication

P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B|A)

10. Venn Diagrams

A Venn diagram represents sets or events using circles. It is useful for understanding unions, intersections and complements.

Union

The union contains everything belonging to A, B, or both.

A ∪ B

Intersection

The intersection contains the outcomes common to both A and B.

A ∩ B

Mutually Exclusive Events

If two events cannot occur together, their intersection is empty.

A ∩ B = ∅
Example:

If A represents students who study Mathematics and B represents students who study Physics, the overlap represents students who study both subjects.

11. Tree Diagrams

A tree diagram displays possible outcomes of a sequence of events. It is especially useful when an experiment happens in stages.

Important Rule

Multiply probabilities along a branch to find the probability of that particular sequence.

Probability of branch = Product of probabilities along the branch

Add probabilities of separate branches when they represent different ways of achieving the required outcome.

Example:

A coin is tossed twice. The possible branches are:

HH, HT, TH, TT

Each branch has probability:

1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4

With Replacement

If an item is returned after selection, the probabilities remain unchanged for the next selection.

Without Replacement

If an item is not returned, the probabilities may change after each selection.

12. Counting Principles

Counting principles help us determine how many possible outcomes exist without listing every outcome individually.

Fundamental Counting Principle

If one task can be completed in m ways and another task can be completed in n ways, both tasks can be completed in m × n ways.

Total number of ways = m × n
Example:

A student has 4 shirts and 3 pairs of trousers. How many different shirt-and-trouser combinations are possible?

4 × 3 = 12

13. Permutations

A permutation is an arrangement of objects where order matters.

Factorial

Factorial is written using the symbol !.

n! = n × (n - 1) × (n - 2) × ... × 2 × 1
0! = 1

Permutation Formula

ⁿPᵣ = n! / (n-r)!
Example:

In how many ways can 3 students be selected and arranged from 5 students?

⁵P₃ = 5! / (5-3)!
= 5! / 2!
= 60

When to Use Permutations

  • Arranging people in seats.
  • Creating passwords where order matters.
  • Choosing positions such as first, second and third.
  • Arranging letters or objects.

14. Combinations

A combination is a selection where order does not matter.

ⁿCᵣ = n! / [r!(n-r)!]
Example:

How many ways can 3 students be selected from a group of 5 students?

⁵C₃ = 5! / (3!2!)
= 10

Permutation vs Combination

Permutation → Order matters
Combination → Order does not matter
Simple way to remember:

Choosing 3 people for a committee is a combination. Arranging 3 people in first, second and third positions is a permutation.

15. Experimental and Theoretical Probability

Theoretical Probability

Theoretical probability is based on the possible outcomes of an experiment before the experiment is performed.

P(A) = Favourable outcomes / Total possible outcomes

Experimental Probability

Experimental probability is calculated from actual results obtained after performing an experiment.

Experimental Probability = Number of times event occurs / Number of trials
Example:

A coin is tossed 100 times and lands on Heads 57 times.

Experimental P(Heads) = 57/100 = 0.57

As the number of trials becomes very large, experimental probability tends to become closer to the theoretical probability.

16. Probability Distributions

A probability distribution shows the possible values of a random variable together with their probabilities.

Random Variable

A random variable assigns numerical values to outcomes of a random experiment.

Discrete Random Variable

A discrete random variable has countable possible values. For example, the number of heads obtained when a coin is tossed several times.

Continuous Random Variable

A continuous random variable can take any value within a range, such as height, weight, distance or time.

Total Probability

ΣP(X) = 1
Example:

Suppose a random variable X has three possible values:

P(X=1) = 0.2
P(X=2) = 0.5
P(X=3) = 0.3

The probabilities add to:

0.2 + 0.5 + 0.3 = 1

17. Expected Value

Expected value represents the long-run average result of a random experiment when the experiment is repeated many times.

E(X) = Σ[xP(X=x)]
Example:

A game gives ₦100 with probability 0.4 and ₦0 with probability 0.6.

E(X) = (100 × 0.4) + (0 × 0.6)
= 40

The expected value is ₦40.

Expected Value and Games

Expected value can be used to determine whether a game is favourable or unfavourable in the long run.

A positive expected gain may indicate a favourable game for the player, while a negative expected gain indicates an expected loss in the long run.

18. Probability Word Problems

Probability word problems require you to translate a real situation into mathematical events before choosing a formula.

Step 1: Identify the Experiment

Determine what is happening. Is something being tossed, rolled, selected, arranged or observed?

Step 2: Identify the Outcomes

Determine all the possible outcomes.

Step 3: Identify the Required Event

Carefully identify exactly what the question is asking you to find.

Step 4: Look for Important Words

  • AND often indicates multiplication.
  • OR often indicates addition.
  • NOT often indicates a complement.
  • GIVEN THAT indicates conditional probability.
  • WITHOUT REPLACEMENT usually indicates dependent events.
  • WITH REPLACEMENT often indicates independent selections.
  • AT LEAST can often be solved using a complement.
  • ARRANGE often suggests permutations.
  • SELECT often suggests combinations.

Worked Example

Question:

A bag contains 4 red balls and 6 blue balls. One ball is selected at random. Find the probability of selecting a red ball.

First find the total number of balls:

4 + 6 = 10

There are 4 favourable outcomes.

P(Red) = 4/10
= 2/5
= 0.4
= 40%

Another Example

Question:

A fair die is rolled. Find the probability of getting a number greater than 4.

The possible outcomes are:

{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}

Numbers greater than 4 are 5 and 6.

P(X > 4) = 2/6 = 1/3
