States of Matter

Matter exists in different physical states depending on the arrangement, movement and energy of its particles. This guide explains solids, liquids, gases and plasma, together with the changes between these states and the particle theory behind them.

1. Introduction to Matter

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space. The chair you sit on, the water you drink, the air you breathe and the screen you are looking at are all examples of matter.

Matter is made up of extremely small particles. Depending on the substance, these particles may be atoms, molecules or ions.

The physical state of a substance depends largely on the arrangement and movement of its particles and on the strength of the attractive forces between those particles.

Key idea: The particles themselves do not normally disappear when a substance changes state. Instead, their arrangement, separation and movement change.

The Four Major States of Matter

Solid

Has a definite shape and definite volume. Its particles are closely packed and mainly vibrate about fixed positions.

Liquid

Has a definite volume but takes the shape of its container. Its particles can move past one another.

Gas

Has no definite shape or volume. Its particles are widely separated and move rapidly in random directions.

Plasma

A high-energy state containing freely moving charged particles such as ions and electrons.

2. Particle Theory of Matter

The particle theory of matter is a model used to explain the behaviour and properties of substances.

Main Ideas

  • All matter is made of tiny particles.
  • There are spaces between particles.
  • Particles are constantly moving.
  • Particles attract one another.
  • The particles of a substance are not necessarily identical to the particles of another substance.
  • Increasing temperature generally increases the average kinetic energy of particles.

Why Particle Theory Matters

Particle theory helps explain why solids keep their shape, why liquids flow, why gases can be compressed, why perfume spreads through a room, and why heating can cause a substance to change state.

3. Solids

A solid is a state of matter with a definite shape and a definite volume.

The particles in a solid are packed closely together. Strong attractive forces hold them in relatively fixed positions.

Particle Movement

Solid particles are not completely motionless. They vibrate around their fixed positions. When the temperature increases, these vibrations generally become more energetic.

Properties of Solids

  • Definite shape
  • Definite volume
  • Very low compressibility
  • Particles closely packed
  • Strong attractive forces
  • Particles vibrate about fixed positions

Examples

Iron, copper, aluminium, salt crystals, ice, diamond, wood and many plastics are solids.

4. Liquids

A liquid has a definite volume but does not have a definite shape. It takes the shape of the container in which it is placed.

Liquid particles remain relatively close together, but they have enough freedom to move and slide past one another.

Properties of Liquids

  • Definite volume
  • No definite shape
  • Usually difficult to compress
  • Particles remain relatively close
  • Particles can move past each other
  • Liquids can flow

Examples

Water, ethanol, mercury, petrol and cooking oil are examples of liquids.

5. Gases

A gas has neither a definite shape nor a definite volume. It expands to fill whatever container is available.

Gas particles are much farther apart than particles in solids and liquids. They move rapidly in random directions.

Properties of Gases

  • No definite shape
  • No definite volume
  • Highly compressible
  • Particles widely separated
  • Particles move rapidly
  • Gas particles collide with one another and the container
Why can gases be compressed? There are relatively large spaces between gas particles. Applying pressure can reduce these spaces.

Examples

Oxygen, nitrogen, hydrogen, helium and carbon dioxide are common examples of gases.

6. Plasma

Plasma is often described as the fourth major state of matter. It forms when a gas receives enough energy for some atoms or molecules to become ionized.

Ionization involves removing electrons from atoms or molecules, producing positive ions and free electrons.

Properties of Plasma

  • Contains charged particles
  • Contains free electrons and ions
  • Can conduct electricity
  • Can respond strongly to magnetic fields
  • Usually exists at high energies

Examples of Plasma

  • The Sun
  • Other stars
  • Lightning
  • Electric arcs
  • Some fluorescent lamps
Important distinction: Plasma is not simply an extremely hot ordinary gas. It is an ionized state containing significant numbers of charged particles.

8. Particle Motion and Kinetic Energy

Kinetic energy is the energy associated with motion. Since particles are constantly moving, they possess kinetic energy.

KE = ½mv²

In a substance, temperature is related to the average kinetic energy of its particles. Increasing the temperature generally increases their average kinetic energy.

Remember: Temperature is related to the average kinetic energy of particles, while the total thermal energy of an object also depends on factors such as the amount of substance.

9. Temperature and Particle Motion

Temperature provides a measure related to the average kinetic energy of particles in a substance.

Temperature ↑ → Average kinetic energy ↑
Temperature ↓ → Average kinetic energy ↓

This relationship helps explain why heating a substance can eventually cause its particles to move enough to change the substance from one physical state to another.

10. Melting

Melting is the change of state from a solid to a liquid.

Solid → Liquid

When a solid is heated, energy is transferred to its particles. Their motion becomes increasingly energetic. At the melting point, the structure of the solid can begin to break down and the particles gain greater freedom of movement.

Melting point: The temperature at which a substance changes from solid to liquid at a specified pressure.

Example

Ice changes into liquid water when enough energy is supplied.

11. Freezing

Freezing is the reverse of melting. A liquid changes into a solid when sufficient energy is removed.

Liquid → Solid

As energy is removed, particle motion decreases and particles become arranged into a more ordered solid structure.

Example

Liquid water becomes ice when it is cooled sufficiently.

12. Evaporation

Evaporation is the gradual conversion of a liquid into a gas from the surface of the liquid.

Liquid → Gas

Why Does Evaporation Occur?

Not all particles in a liquid have exactly the same kinetic energy. Some particles at the surface have enough energy to escape the attractive forces holding them in the liquid.

Factors Affecting Evaporation

  • Higher temperature generally increases evaporation.
  • Larger surface area generally increases evaporation.
  • Moving air can increase evaporation.
  • Lower humidity generally favours faster evaporation.
  • The nature of the liquid affects its evaporation rate.

Everyday Example

Sweat evaporates from the skin. The escape of higher-energy water molecules can remove energy from the skin and produce a cooling effect.

13. Boiling

Boiling is the rapid vaporization of a liquid that occurs throughout the liquid when its vapour pressure equals the external pressure.

Liquid → Gas

Boiling Point

The boiling point of a liquid depends on the external pressure. At higher external pressure, a liquid generally requires a higher temperature to boil.

Boiling vs Evaporation

Boiling Evaporation
Occurs throughout the liquid. Occurs mainly at the surface.
Occurs at the boiling point for a given pressure. Can occur below the boiling point.
Usually rapid. Usually slower.
Bubbles can form throughout the liquid. Bulk bubbling is not required.

14. Condensation

Condensation is the change from a gas to a liquid.

Gas → Liquid

When gas particles lose energy, their average motion decreases. Attractive forces can then bring particles closer together, allowing the liquid state to form.

Example

Water vapour can condense into liquid droplets on the outside of a cold glass.

15. Sublimation

Sublimation is the direct change of a solid into a gas without passing through the liquid state under the given conditions.

Solid → Gas

Examples

  • Dry ice changing directly into carbon dioxide gas.
  • Solid iodine producing iodine vapour when heated.
  • Some mothball substances gradually entering the gas phase.

16. Deposition

Deposition is the reverse of sublimation. A gas changes directly into a solid without becoming a liquid first.

Gas → Solid

Example

Water vapour can form ice directly on a sufficiently cold surface, producing frost.

17. Heating and Cooling Curves

A heating curve shows how the temperature of a substance changes as energy is supplied.

Typical Heating Process

Solid → Melting → Liquid → Boiling → Gas

During a phase change, energy can continue to enter the substance without causing an increase in temperature. The energy is being used to change the arrangement and interactions of the particles.

Important: On an ideal heating curve for a pure substance at constant pressure, the temperature remains approximately constant during a phase change even though energy continues to be transferred.

Cooling Process

Gas → Condensation → Liquid → Freezing → Solid

18. Diffusion

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration as a result of random particle motion.

Higher concentration → Lower concentration

Diffusion continues until the particles become more evenly distributed.

Diffusion in Gases

Diffusion is generally rapid in gases because gas particles are far apart and move rapidly.

Diffusion in Liquids

Diffusion also occurs in liquids, but it is generally slower because liquid particles are closer together and their movement is more restricted.

Example

When perfume is sprayed in one part of a room, its molecules eventually spread through the air by diffusion.

19. Gas Pressure

Gas pressure is produced by collisions between moving gas particles and the walls of their container.

More frequent and/or more energetic collisions generally produce greater pressure.

Temperature and Pressure

If the volume of a sealed container remains constant, increasing the temperature makes the gas particles move faster. They collide with the walls more frequently and with greater energy.

Temperature ↑ → Particle speed ↑ → Pressure ↑

Volume and Pressure

If temperature remains constant, reducing the volume gives the particles less space to move and increases the frequency of collisions with the container walls.

Volume ↓ → Collision frequency ↑ → Pressure ↑

20. Everyday Applications

Ice

Ice is solid water. Heating it sufficiently causes it to melt into liquid water.

Cloud Formation

Water vapour can cool and condense into tiny liquid droplets that contribute to cloud formation.

Boiling Water

Heating water sufficiently causes it to undergo boiling, producing water vapour.

Lightning

Lightning produces extremely energetic ionized matter, creating plasma.

21. Word Problems and Practice

Problem 1 — Identifying a State

A substance has a fixed volume but takes the shape of its container. What state of matter is it?

Answer: Liquid.

Liquids have a definite volume but do not have a definite shape.

Problem 2 — Melting

A piece of ice is heated until it becomes liquid water. Identify the change of state.

Answer: Melting.

Solid → Liquid

Problem 3 — Condensation

Water vapour in the air comes into contact with a cold window and forms liquid droplets. What process occurred?

Answer: Condensation.

Gas → Liquid

Problem 4 — Evaporation

Why does water usually evaporate faster from a wide, shallow container than from a narrow, deep container?

Answer: The wide container provides a larger surface area, giving more liquid particles an opportunity to escape from the surface.

Problem 5 — Gas Pressure

A sealed container contains a gas. The container is heated while its volume remains constant. What happens to the pressure and why?

Answer: The pressure increases because the particles gain kinetic energy and collide with the walls more frequently and more energetically.

Problem 6 — Sublimation

A solid substance changes directly into a gas without first becoming a liquid. What is the process called?

Answer: Sublimation.

Solid → Gas

Problem 7 — Particle Theory

Explain why a gas is much easier to compress than a solid.

Answer: Gas particles are separated by relatively large spaces. When pressure is applied, these spaces can be reduced. Solid particles are already packed closely together, so there is much less empty space available.

Problem 8 — Heating Curve

During the melting of a pure substance, energy continues to be supplied but the temperature remains approximately constant. Explain why.

Answer: The supplied energy is being used mainly to overcome attractive forces and change the arrangement of the particles rather than increasing their average kinetic energy.

Problem 9 — Diffusion

A bottle of perfume is opened in one corner of a classroom. After some time, students far away can smell it. Explain this using particle theory.

Answer: Perfume molecules are constantly moving randomly. They diffuse through the air from regions where their concentration is higher toward regions where it is lower.

Problem 10 — Comparing States

Explain why a liquid can flow while a solid usually cannot.

Answer: The particles in a liquid can move past one another, allowing the liquid to flow. In a solid, particles are held in relatively fixed positions and mainly vibrate, preventing the substance from flowing in the same way.
