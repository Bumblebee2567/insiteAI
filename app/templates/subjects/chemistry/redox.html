Redox Reactions

A comprehensive high-school guide to oxidation and reduction, oxidation state rules, oxidizing and reducing agents, balancing redox half-equations, displacement reactions, and redox titrations.

Overview Definitions Oxidation States Rules Agents Half-Equations Balancing Method Types of Redox Displacement Examples Redox Titrations Applications Exam Tips

1. What Are Redox Reactions?

A Redox reaction (short for Reduction-Oxidation) is a chemical process in which electrons are transferred between chemical species. Oxidation and reduction always occur simultaneously; one substance cannot be oxidized without another being reduced.

Oxidation

The loss of electrons, loss of hydrogen, or gain of oxygen during a reaction.

Reduction

The gain of electrons, gain of hydrogen, or loss of oxygen during a reaction.

Fundamental Rule: The total number of electrons lost by the species being oxidized must equal the total number of electrons gained by the species being reduced.

2. Four Ways to Define Redox

Over time, chemistry has expanded the definition of redox reactions from simple oxygen gain to fundamental electron and oxidation state changes.

Basis Oxidation Reduction
Oxygen Transfer Gain of oxygen Loss of oxygen
Hydrogen Transfer Loss of hydrogen Gain of hydrogen
Electron Transfer Loss of electrons Gain of electrons
Oxidation State Increase in oxidation number Decrease in oxidation number
Memory Mnemonics:
• OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons).
• LEO says GER: Lose Electrons = Oxidation, Gain Electrons = Reduction.

3. Oxidation States (Oxidation Numbers)

An oxidation state (or oxidation number) is a integer assigned to an element in a molecule or ion that represents the hypothetical charge it would have if all bonds were 100% ionic.

Oxidation State = (Hypothetical charge on an atom in a compound)

Oxidation states are written with the sign before the number (e.g., +2 or -1), unlike ionic charges which are written with the sign after (e.g., 2+ or 1-).

4. Rules for Assigning Oxidation Numbers

1. Uncombined Elements: Any element in its uncombined state has an oxidation number of 0 (e.g., Na, Fe, O₂, Cl₂, P₄, S₈).
2. Monatomic Ions: The oxidation number equals the charge on the ion (e.g., Na⁺ = +1, Mg²⁺ = +2, Cl⁻ = -1, O²⁻ = -2).
3. Neutral Compounds: The sum of oxidation numbers of all atoms in a neutral compound must equal 0 (e.g., in H₂O: 2(+1) + (-2) = 0).
4. Polyatomic Ions: The sum of oxidation numbers must equal the charge of the ion (e.g., in SO₄²⁻: sum = -2).
5. Fluorine: Fluorine is the most electronegative element and always has an oxidation state of -1 in all compounds.
6. Hydrogen: Hydrogen is +1 when bonded to non-metals (e.g., HCl, H₂O) and -1 in metal hydrides (e.g., NaH, CaH₂).
7. Oxygen: Oxygen is usually -2 in compounds, except in peroxides like H₂O₂ (-1) and when bonded to fluorine in OF₂ (+2).
Exam Tip: Group 1 metals are always +1, Group 2 metals are always +2, and Aluminium is always +3 in compounds.

5. Oxidizing and Reducing Agents

Because redox reactions involve electron transfer, reactants play complimentary roles as electron acceptors or electron donors.

Oxidizing Agent (Oxidant)

Accepts electrons and oxidizes another species. The oxidizing agent itself gets reduced (its oxidation state decreases).

Reducing Agent (Reductant)

Donates electrons and reduces another species. The reducing agent itself gets oxidized (its oxidation state increases).

Common Laboratory Agents

Agent Type Common Chemical Color Change / Role
Oxidizing Agent Acidified KMnO₄ (Potassium Manganate VII) Purple (MnO₄⁻) → Colorless (Mn²⁺)
Oxidizing Agent Acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ (Potassium Dichromate VI) Orange (Cr₂O₇²⁻) → Green (Cr³⁺)
Reducing Agent KI (Potassium Iodide) Colorless (I⁻) → Brown (I₂)
Reducing Agent Reactive Metals (e.g., Zn, Mg) Solid metal dissolves to form cations

6. Half-Equations

A half-equation shows only the oxidation or reduction part of a redox reaction, highlighting the gain or loss of electrons.

Oxidation Half-Equation

Electrons appear on the product side (right side) as they are lost.

Oxidation of Zinc
Zn (s) → Zn²⁺ (aq) + 2e⁻

Reduction Half-Equation

Electrons appear on the reactant side (left side) as they are gained.

Reduction of Copper(II)
Cu²⁺ (aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu (s)

Overall Balanced Redox Equation

Combine both half-equations so that electrons cancel out completely:

Net Ionic Equation
Zn (s) + Cu²⁺ (aq) → Zn²⁺ (aq) + Cu (s)

7. Method for Balancing Complex Redox Equations

For complex reactions in acidic aqueous solutions (involving polyatomic ions like MnO₄⁻ or Cr₂O₇²⁻), follow the systematic Ion-Electron Method:

Step 1: Split the reaction into two half-reactions (oxidation and reduction).
Step 2: Balance all elements EXCEPT hydrogen and oxygen.
Step 3: Balance Oxygen atoms by adding H₂O molecules to the oxygen-deficient side.
Step 4: Balance Hydrogen atoms by adding H⁺ ions to the hydrogen-deficient side.
Step 5: Balance the net charge by adding electrons (e⁻) to the more positive side.
Step 6: Multiply half-reactions by integers so both have the same number of electrons.
Step 7: Add the half-reactions together and cancel identical species (H⁺, H₂O, e⁻) on both sides.

8. Major Types of Redox Reactions

Combustion

A fuel reacts rapidly with oxygen to release energy. Oxygen is reduced from 0 to -2.

Displacement

A more reactive element displaces a less reactive element from its compound.

Synthesis / Combination

Two elements combine directly to form a single compound (e.g., 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl).

Disproportionation

A single species is simultaneously oxidized and reduced in the same reaction.

9. Displacement Reactions

In metal displacement, a more electropositive metal donates electrons to the ions of a less reactive metal.

Metal Reactivity Series Trend

Metals high in the reactivity series (e.g., K, Na, Mg, Zn) lose electrons easily and act as strong reducing agents.

Iron displacing Copper
Fe (s) + CuSO₄ (aq) → FeSO₄ (aq) + Cu (s)

Halogen Displacement

More electronegative halogens displace less reactive halide ions from solution:

Chlorine displacing Bromide
Cl₂ (g) + 2KBr (aq) → 2KCl (aq) + Br₂ (aq)

Chlorine is reduced (0 → -1) and bromide is oxidized (-1 → 0).

10. Worked Examples

Example 1 — Determining Oxidation Numbers

Find the oxidation state of Chromium in K₂Cr₂O₇.

Step 1: Identify known oxidation numbers: Potassium (Group 1) = +1, Oxygen = -2.

Step 2: Set up algebraic equation (neutral compound sum = 0):

2(+1) + 2(Cr) + 7(-2) = 0
+2 + 2(Cr) - 14 = 0 ⇒ 2(Cr) = +12 ⇒ Cr = +6

Answer: Chromium is in the +6 oxidation state.

Example 2 — Disproportionation Reaction

Examine the decomposition of Hydrogen Peroxide: 2H₂O₂ (aq) → 2H₂O (l) + O₂ (g)

Oxidation state of O in H₂O₂: -1

Oxidation state of O in H₂O: -2 (Reduced: -1 → -2)

Oxidation state of O in O₂: 0 (Oxidized: -1 → 0)

Conclusion: Oxygen is both oxidized and reduced; this is a disproportionation reaction.

11. Redox Titrations

Redox titrations determine the concentration of an unknown oxidizing or reducing agent by reacting it with a standard solution of known concentration.

Potassium Manganate(VII) Titrations

Acidified KMnO₄ is a self-indicating oxidizing agent used to determine concentrations of Fe²⁺ or C₂O₄²⁻ ions.

Manganate Half-Equation
MnO₄⁻ (aq) + 8H⁺ (aq) + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ (aq) + 4H₂O (l)
End Point: The titration turns from colorless to a permanent faint pink when all reducing agent is consumed and one excess drop of MnO₄⁻ remains.

12. Real-World Applications

Batteries & Fuel Cells

Spontaneous redox reactions separate electron flow to create electric current.

Corrosion & Rusting

Iron oxidizes in the presence of water and oxygen: 4Fe + 3O₂ + 2xH₂O → 2Fe₂O₃·xH₂O.

Biological Energy

Respiration oxidizes glucose to CO₂; photosynthesis reduces CO₂ to carbohydrates.

Metallurgy

Extracting metals from ores by reduction (e.g., reducing iron ore with CO in a blast furnace).

13. Exam Checklist

  • Remember OIL RIG and LEO says GER for electron transfers.
  • Assign oxidation state of 0 to pure elements (O₂, H₂, Cu).
  • Check that charges and atoms are balanced on both sides of half-equations.
  • In acid solutions, balance O using H₂O and H using H⁺.
  • The species oxidized is the REDUCING agent; the species reduced is the OXIDIZING agent.
  • Roman numerals in chemical names denote oxidation states (e.g., Iron(III) Chloride = Fe³⁺).
  • Multiply half-reactions to balance electrons before adding them together.
Core Memory Line: Oxidation = Gain of O, Loss of H/e⁻, Increase in Oxidation State.
Reduction = Loss of O, Gain of H/e⁻, Decrease in Oxidation State.
