What Is Organic Chemistry?

Organic chemistry is the study of carbon compounds and their structures, properties and reactions. Carbon is especially important because it forms four covalent bonds and can bond strongly to other carbon atoms, allowing chains, branches and rings to form.

Key idea: Carbon's ability to form four covalent bonds and bond with itself gives rise to an enormous variety of compounds.

Covalent Bonding

Carbon normally forms strong covalent bonds by sharing electrons with other atoms.

Catenation

Carbon atoms can bond to other carbon atoms to form chains, branches and rings.

Huge Variety

Carbon compounds range from simple molecules such as methane to very complex biological and synthetic molecules.

02

Why Is Carbon So Important?

Carbon has four valence electrons and can therefore form four covalent bonds. It can also form single, double and triple bonds, giving organic molecules many possible structures.

Carbon valency = 4 Carbon commonly forms four covalent bonds.

Chains

Carbon atoms can join together to form long continuous carbon chains.

Branches

Carbon chains can branch, producing structural isomers with different arrangements.

Rings

Carbon atoms can connect back to one another to form cyclic structures.

Multiple Bonds

Carbon can form single, double and triple bonds with other carbon atoms.

03

Hydrocarbons

Hydrocarbons are organic compounds containing only carbon and hydrogen. They include alkanes, alkenes and alkynes.

Alkanes

Saturated hydrocarbons containing carbon-carbon single bonds.

CnH2n+2

Alkenes

Unsaturated hydrocarbons containing at least one carbon-carbon double bond.

CnH2n

Alkynes

Unsaturated hydrocarbons containing at least one carbon-carbon triple bond.

CnH2n-2

Homologous Series

A homologous series is a family of organic compounds with the same functional group and general formula, where successive members differ by a CH2 unit.

Important: Members of a homologous series have similar chemical properties, while physical properties such as boiling point generally change gradually through the series.

Major Organic Reactions

Organic reactions involve breaking existing bonds and forming new bonds. The reaction type depends strongly on the functional group and the reagents and conditions involved.

Combustion

Hydrocarbons react with oxygen. Complete combustion produces carbon dioxide and water.

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

Addition

Unsaturated compounds such as alkenes can add atoms or groups across their carbon-carbon double bond.

C₂H₄ + H₂ → C₂H₆

Substitution

One atom or group in an organic compound is replaced by another atom or group.

CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl

Elimination

Atoms or groups are removed from a molecule, often forming an additional carbon-carbon bond.

C₂H₅OH → C₂H₄ + H₂O

Oxidation

Organic molecules can undergo oxidation, commonly involving an increase in oxygen or a decrease in hydrogen.

Esterification

A carboxylic acid can react with an alcohol to form an ester and water.

Acid + Alcohol ⇌ Ester + Water

Alkenes

Alkenes are unsaturated hydrocarbons containing at least one carbon-carbon double bond.

CnH2n General formula for simple open-chain alkenes containing one carbon-carbon double bond.
Example: Ethene
CH2 = CH2

Addition Reactions

The double bond provides a site where atoms or groups can be added.

Bromine Test

Bromine solution can be used as a test for carbon-carbon unsaturation.

10

Alcohols

Alcohols contain the hydroxyl functional group, −OH, attached to a carbon atom.

Example: Ethanol
CH3CH2OH

Combustion

Alcohols can burn in oxygen to produce carbon dioxide and water when complete combustion occurs.

Dehydration

Alcohols can lose water under suitable conditions, producing an alkene.

Carboxylic Acids

Carboxylic acids contain the carboxyl functional group, −COOH. They exhibit acidic behaviour because the O−H bond can release a proton.

General Structure

Ester Formation

Carboxylic acids react with alcohols to produce esters and water under suitable conditions.
