1. Introduction to pH and Indicators

The pH of a solution tells us how acidic or alkaline the solution is. It is closely related to the concentration of hydrogen ions or, more accurately in aqueous chemistry, hydronium ions.

Indicators are substances that change colour depending on the acidity of a solution. They allow us to estimate whether a solution is acidic, neutral or alkaline.

Key idea: The lower the pH, the greater the hydronium-ion concentration. The higher the pH, the lower the hydronium-ion concentration, under the usual aqueous conditions.

pH

A logarithmic measure related to the activity of hydrogen ions in solution.

Indicator

A substance whose colour changes over a particular pH range.

Acidic

At 25°C, an aqueous solution with pH below 7 is generally classified as acidic.

Alkaline

At 25°C, an aqueous solution with pH above 7 is generally classified as alkaline.

2. The pH Scale

What Does pH 7 Mean?

At 25°C, pure water has approximately equal concentrations of H₃O⁺ and OH⁻ and is considered neutral, giving a pH close to 7.

pH = 7 → Neutral at 25°C

Why Is the Scale Logarithmic?

A change of one pH unit corresponds to a tenfold change in hydronium-ion concentration when the simple concentration form of the pH relationship is being used.

Example: A solution of pH 3 has approximately ten times the hydronium concentration of a solution of pH 4, under the same assumptions.

3. Hydrogen-Ion Concentration and pH

In introductory chemistry, pH can be calculated from the concentration of hydronium ions using:

pH = -log₁₀[H₃O⁺]

The brackets indicate concentration in mol/L in this simplified school-level treatment.

Example

A solution has [H₃O⁺] = 1.0 × 10⁻³ mol/L. Find its pH.

pH = -log₁₀(1.0 × 10⁻³)

pH = 3

Finding Concentration from pH

The pH equation can be rearranged to obtain hydronium-ion concentration:

[H₃O⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ

Find [H₃O⁺] when pH = 5.

[H₃O⁺] = 10⁻⁵

[H₃O⁺] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol/L

4. Hydroxide Ions and pOH

Alkaline solutions contain hydroxide ions, OH⁻. Their concentration can be expressed using pOH.

pOH = -log₁₀[OH⁻]

At 25°C, the ion-product constant for water leads to:

pH + pOH = 14

Example

A solution has pH = 11. Find its pOH.

pOH = 14 - pH

pOH = 14 - 11

pOH = 3

Finding Hydroxide Concentration

[OH⁻] = 10⁻ᵖᴼᴴ

5. Acid-Base Indicators

An acid-base indicator is usually a weak acid or weak base whose different forms have different colours.

The colour change occurs over a characteristic transition range, rather than at one exact pH.

HIn ⇌ H⁺ + In⁻

The two forms, HIn and In⁻, can have different colours. As the pH changes, the equilibrium shifts and the observed colour changes.

Why Indicators Are Useful

  • They can show whether a solution is acidic or alkaline.
  • They can provide an approximate pH range.
  • They can be used to detect endpoints in titrations.
  • Different indicators are suitable for different pH ranges.

6. Universal Indicator

Universal indicator is a mixture of several indicators designed to show different colours across a broad range of pH values.

Because the colour changes across a range, universal indicator can be compared with a colour chart to estimate the pH of a solution.

Approximate pH General Classification
0–3 Strongly acidic
4–6 Weakly acidic
7 Neutral at 25°C
8–10 Weakly alkaline
11–14 Strongly alkaline
Important: Universal indicator is useful for estimating pH, but a precise pH measurement requires an appropriate instrument such as a calibrated pH meter.

10. Strong and Weak Acids and Bases

Acid or base strength describes how extensively the substance ionises or reacts with water. It is different from concentration.

Strong Acids

Strong acids ionise extensively in water.

  • Hydrochloric acid — HCl
  • Nitric acid — HNO₃
  • Perchloric acid — HClO₄
  • Sulfuric acid — H₂SO₄, with its first ionisation being strong

Weak Acids

Weak acids ionise only partially and establish equilibrium with their ions.

  • Ethanoic acid — CH₃COOH
  • Carbonic acid — H₂CO₃
  • Hydrofluoric acid — HF

Strong Bases

  • Sodium hydroxide — NaOH
  • Potassium hydroxide — KOH
  • Barium hydroxide — Ba(OH)₂

Weak Bases

  • Ammonia — NH₃
  • Many amines
Do not confuse: Strength describes ionisation behaviour. Concentration describes how much solute is present in a given volume.

11. Acid Strength and pH

For solutions having the same concentration, a strong monoprotic acid generally produces a greater hydronium concentration than a weak acid.

Strong acid → extensive ionisation → greater [H₃O⁺] → lower pH

A weak acid establishes an equilibrium:

HA + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + A⁻

Only part of the acid molecules ionise at equilibrium.

12. Base Strength and pH

Strong bases produce hydroxide ions extensively in aqueous solution, while weak bases react with water only partially.

Strong base → greater [OH⁻] → lower pOH → higher pH

Weak Base Example

NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻

Ammonia does not simply dissociate like NaOH. It reacts with water and establishes an equilibrium.

13. pH Calculations

Calculating pH from [H₃O⁺]

pH = -log₁₀[H₃O⁺]

Calculate the pH of 0.001 mol/L HCl, assuming complete ionisation.

HCl is a strong monoprotic acid.

[H₃O⁺] ≈ 0.001 = 1.0 × 10⁻³ mol/L

pH = -log₁₀(1.0 × 10⁻³)

pH = 3

Calculating pH from pOH

pH = 14 - pOH

A solution has pOH = 4. Calculate its pH at 25°C.

pH = 14 - 4

pH = 10

Calculating pOH

pOH = -log₁₀[OH⁻]

14. Worked Examples

Example 1 — Comparing pH

Which is more acidic: a solution with pH 2 or one with pH 4?

The pH 2 solution is more acidic.

Difference in pH = 2 units.

Hydronium concentration differs by:

10² = 100

Therefore, under the simple concentration interpretation, the pH 2 solution has about 100 times the hydronium concentration of the pH 4 solution.

Example 2 — Finding [H₃O⁺]

Find the hydronium-ion concentration of a solution with pH = 6.

[H₃O⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ

[H₃O⁺] = 10⁻⁶

[H₃O⁺] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ mol/L

Example 3 — Finding pOH

A solution has [OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻² mol/L. Find its pOH and pH.

pOH = -log₁₀(1.0 × 10⁻²)

pOH = 2

pH = 14 - 2

pH = 12

Example 4 — Choosing an Indicator

Why might an indicator be chosen based on the expected equivalence-point pH of a titration?

The indicator should change colour over a pH range that includes the steep region around the equivalence point. This makes the observed endpoint a useful approximation to the stoichiometric equivalence point.

15. Word Problems

Problem 1 — pH Comparison

Two laboratory solutions have pH values of 3 and 5. How many times greater is the hydronium-ion concentration of the pH 3 solution?

Difference:

5 - 3 = 2

Concentration ratio:

10² = 100

The pH 3 solution has approximately 100 times the hydronium concentration.

Problem 2 — Indicator Selection

A student wants to determine whether an unknown solution is acidic or alkaline. Which type of indicator could provide useful information over a broad pH range?

Universal indicator would be suitable for estimating the pH over a broad range because it changes through several colours across the pH scale.

Problem 3 — Strong and Weak Acids

Two solutions contain acids at the same concentration. Solution A contains a strong acid and Solution B contains a weak acid. Which would normally have the lower pH?

Assuming comparable monoprotic acids and equal concentration, the strong acid would normally produce more hydronium ions.

Therefore:

Solution A would normally have the lower pH.

Problem 4 — Household Chemistry

A household cleaning product is strongly alkaline. Explain why it is important to handle it carefully.

Strongly alkaline substances can be corrosive and can damage skin, eyes and other materials. Appropriate safety procedures, protective equipment and the product's instructions should therefore be followed.

16. Common Mistakes

1. Thinking pH 6 Is Twice as Acidic as pH 3

pH is logarithmic. A three-unit difference corresponds to a factor of 10³ = 1000 in hydronium concentration under the simple concentration interpretation.

2. Confusing Strong and Concentrated

A strong acid ionises extensively. A concentrated solution contains a large amount of solute per unit volume.

3. Assuming Every Indicator Has the Same Colour Change

Different indicators have different transition ranges and colour changes.

4. Assuming pH 7 Is Always Neutral

In school-level aqueous chemistry, pH 7 is treated as neutral at 25°C. The neutral pH depends on temperature.

5. Forgetting the Negative Sign

pH = -log₁₀[H₃O⁺]

6. Using the Wrong Indicator in a Titration

An indicator should be selected according to the expected equivalence-point region of the reaction.

Best solving method: Identify whether the solution is acidic or alkaline, determine the relevant ion concentration, choose the correct pH or pOH relationship, substitute carefully, and check whether the final answer makes chemical sense.
