Gas Laws

Gas laws describe how the pressure, volume, temperature and amount of a gas are related. This guide explains the major gas laws, their formulas, conditions, units, calculations, applications and common word-problem techniques.

Introduction Gas Variables Temperature Boyle's Law Charles' Law Gay-Lussac's Law Avogadro's Law Combined Gas Law Ideal Gas Law Gas Constant Pressure Kinetic Theory Gas Law Relationships Examples Word Problems Common Mistakes

1. Introduction to Gas Laws

Gases behave differently from solids and liquids because their particles are relatively far apart and move freely in random directions.

Gas laws are mathematical relationships that describe what happens when one or more properties of a gas change.

The four most important measurable variables used in basic gas laws are pressure, volume, temperature and amount of gas.

Core idea: When one gas variable changes, another variable may also change. The particular relationship depends on which variables are held constant.

The Major Gas Laws

Boyle's Law

Relates pressure and volume when temperature and amount of gas remain constant.

Charles' Law

Relates volume and absolute temperature when pressure and amount remain constant.

Gay-Lussac's Law

Relates pressure and absolute temperature when volume and amount remain constant.

Avogadro's Law

Relates volume and amount of gas when pressure and temperature remain constant.

2. Variables in Gas Laws

Understanding the symbols is one of the most important steps in solving gas-law problems.

P Pressure
V Volume
T Temperature
n Amount in moles

Pressure

Pressure is related to the force exerted by gas particles as they collide with the walls of their container.

Common units include pascals (Pa), kilopascals (kPa), atmospheres (atm) and millimetres of mercury (mmHg).

Volume

Volume is the amount of space occupied by the gas. Common units include litres (L) and millilitres (mL).

Temperature

Gas-law calculations require absolute temperature when using relationships involving temperature.

Amount

The amount of gas is usually measured in moles and represented by n.

3. Temperature and the Kelvin Scale

A major rule in gas-law calculations is that temperature must normally be expressed in kelvin rather than degrees Celsius.

T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15

Example

Convert 27°C to Kelvin.

T = 27 + 273.15

T = 300.15 K
Exam warning: Do not directly substitute Celsius temperatures into Boyle, Charles, Gay-Lussac or combined gas-law formulas when absolute temperature is required.

4. Boyle's Law

Boyle's law describes the relationship between the pressure and volume of a fixed amount of gas at constant temperature.

P₁V₁ = P₂V₂

Pressure and volume are inversely proportional.

P ↑ → V ↓

P ↓ → V ↑

Meaning

If the volume of a gas is reduced while temperature and amount remain constant, the particles have less space and collide with the container walls more frequently. The pressure therefore increases.

Worked Example

A gas occupies 4.0 L at a pressure of 2.0 atm. What is its volume when the pressure becomes 4.0 atm?

P₁V₁ = P₂V₂

(2.0)(4.0) = (4.0)V₂

8.0 = 4.0V₂

V₂ = 2.0 L

5. Charles' Law

Charles' law describes the relationship between volume and absolute temperature when pressure and amount of gas remain constant.

V₁ / T₁ = V₂ / T₂

Volume and absolute temperature are directly proportional.

T ↑ → V ↑

T ↓ → V ↓

Worked Example

A gas occupies 2.0 L at 300 K. What volume will it occupy at 450 K if pressure remains constant?

V₁ / T₁ = V₂ / T₂

2.0 / 300 = V₂ / 450

V₂ = (2.0 × 450) / 300

V₂ = 3.0 L

6. Gay-Lussac's Law

Gay-Lussac's law describes the relationship between pressure and absolute temperature when volume and amount of gas remain constant.

P₁ / T₁ = P₂ / T₂

Pressure and absolute temperature are directly proportional.

T ↑ → P ↑

T ↓ → P ↓

Worked Example

A gas has a pressure of 100 kPa at 300 K. What pressure will it have at 450 K if the volume remains constant?

P₁ / T₁ = P₂ / T₂

100 / 300 = P₂ / 450

P₂ = (100 × 450) / 300

P₂ = 150 kPa

7. Avogadro's Law

Avogadro's law describes the relationship between the volume and amount of gas when temperature and pressure remain constant.

V₁ / n₁ = V₂ / n₂

Volume is directly proportional to the number of moles.

n ↑ → V ↑

n ↓ → V ↓

Worked Example

A gas occupies 6 L when it contains 2 mol. What volume will it occupy when it contains 5 mol, assuming temperature and pressure remain constant?

V₁ / n₁ = V₂ / n₂

6 / 2 = V₂ / 5

V₂ = (6 × 5) / 2

V₂ = 15 L

8. Combined Gas Law

The combined gas law combines the pressure-volume-temperature relationships for a fixed amount of gas.

P₁V₁ / T₁ = P₂V₂ / T₂

The amount of gas remains constant.

When Should You Use It?

Use the combined gas law when pressure, volume and temperature can all change between two states while the amount of gas remains constant.

Worked Example

A gas occupies 2.0 L at 100 kPa and 300 K. It is changed to 150 kPa and 450 K. Find its new volume.

P₁V₁ / T₁ = P₂V₂ / T₂

(100 × 2.0) / 300 = (150 × V₂) / 450

200 / 300 = 150V₂ / 450

V₂ = 2.0 L

Answer: 2.0 L

9. Ideal Gas Law

The ideal gas law connects pressure, volume, amount of gas and absolute temperature in one equation.

PV = nRT

Meaning of the Symbols

Symbol Meaning Typical SI Unit
P Pressure Pa
V Volume m³
n Amount of gas mol
R Gas constant 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹
T Absolute temperature K

Rearranging the Ideal Gas Equation

P = nRT / V
V = nRT / P
n = PV / RT
T = PV / nR

10. The Gas Constant

The value of R depends on the units used for pressure and volume.

R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹

Another commonly used value is approximately:

R = 0.0821 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹
Important: Always make sure your value of R matches the pressure and volume units in your calculation.

11. Gas Pressure and Particle Collisions

Gas pressure comes from collisions between gas particles and the walls of their container.

Increasing Temperature

When a gas in a rigid container is heated, its particles gain kinetic energy and move faster. Their collisions with the walls become more frequent and energetic.

Temperature ↑ → Particle kinetic energy ↑ → Pressure ↑

Reducing Volume

If the same amount of gas is compressed into a smaller volume, particles have less space and collide with the walls more often.

Volume ↓ → Collision frequency ↑ → Pressure ↑

12. Kinetic Molecular Theory

Kinetic molecular theory provides a model for explaining the behaviour of gases using the motion of their particles.

Main Assumptions

  • Gas particles are in constant random motion.
  • The individual volume of gas particles is considered negligible compared with the volume of the container in the idealized model.
  • Attractive and repulsive forces between particles are assumed to be negligible for an ideal gas.
  • Collisions are treated as elastic in the idealized model.
  • The average kinetic energy of particles depends on absolute temperature.
Key relationship: Higher absolute temperature means greater average kinetic energy of gas particles.

13. Gas-Law Relationships

Boyle's Law

At constant temperature, pressure increases as volume decreases. The relationship is inverse.

P ∝ 1/V

Charles' Law

At constant pressure, volume increases directly with absolute temperature.

V ∝ T

Gay-Lussac's Law

At constant volume, pressure increases directly with absolute temperature.

P ∝ T

Avogadro's Law

At constant pressure and temperature, volume increases directly with the number of moles.

V ∝ n

14. Worked Examples

Example 1 — Boyle's Law

A gas has a volume of 10 L at 1 atm. If the volume is reduced to 5 L at constant temperature, find the new pressure.

P₁V₁ = P₂V₂

(1)(10) = P₂(5)

P₂ = 2 atm

Example 2 — Charles' Law

A gas occupies 3 L at 300 K. What volume will it occupy at 600 K if pressure remains constant?

V₁ / T₁ = V₂ / T₂

3 / 300 = V₂ / 600

V₂ = 6 L

Example 3 — Gay-Lussac's Law

A sealed gas container has a pressure of 200 kPa at 300 K. What will its pressure be at 600 K?

P₁ / T₁ = P₂ / T₂

200 / 300 = P₂ / 600

P₂ = 400 kPa

Example 4 — Choosing the Correct Law

A gas is heated while its pressure remains constant. Its volume increases. Which gas law describes this situation?

Charles' Law.

Charles' law relates volume and absolute temperature at constant pressure.

15. Gas-Law Word Problems

Problem 1

A gas occupies 8 L at 2 atm. The pressure is increased to 4 atm while temperature remains constant. Find the new volume.

P₁V₁ = P₂V₂

(2)(8) = (4)V₂

V₂ = 4 L

Problem 2

A balloon has a volume of 2 L at 250 K. It is heated to 500 K while pressure remains constant. Find its new volume.

V₁ / T₁ = V₂ / T₂

2 / 250 = V₂ / 500

V₂ = 4 L

Problem 3

A gas has a pressure of 100 kPa at 300 K. It is heated to 600 K in a rigid container. Find the new pressure.

P₁ / T₁ = P₂ / T₂

100 / 300 = P₂ / 600

P₂ = 200 kPa

Problem 4

A gas has a volume of 5 L at 300 K and 1 atm. It is changed to 400 K and 2 atm. Find the new volume.

P₁V₁ / T₁ = P₂V₂ / T₂

(1 × 5) / 300 = (2 × V₂) / 400

V₂ = 3.33 L approximately.

Answer: 3.33 L

Problem 5

A gas occupies 10 L at 1 atm and 300 K. Calculate the number of moles using R = 0.0821 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹.

PV = nRT

n = PV / RT

n = (1 × 10) / (0.0821 × 300)

n ≈ 0.406 mol

Problem 6 — Conceptual

Why does a pressure cooker allow food to cook at a higher temperature than ordinary boiling water?

Increasing the pressure above the liquid increases its boiling temperature. Therefore, water inside a pressure cooker can reach a temperature above its normal boiling point at atmospheric pressure.

16. Common Gas-Law Mistakes

1. Using Celsius Instead of Kelvin

Temperature should normally be converted to kelvin before it is substituted into temperature-based gas-law equations.

2. Mixing Units

Make sure pressure, volume and the gas constant use compatible units.

3. Choosing the Wrong Law

Look carefully at which variables change and which variables remain constant.

4. Forgetting the Inverse Relationship

Boyle's law is inverse. Increasing pressure causes volume to decrease when temperature and amount remain constant.

5. Forgetting Moles

If the amount of gas changes, a simple two-state Boyle, Charles or Gay-Lussac calculation may no longer be sufficient. Consider Avogadro's law, the combined relationships or the ideal gas equation depending on the problem.

Best strategy: First write down P, V, T and n for the initial and final states. Then identify what remains constant. Finally choose the equation.
