Atomic Structure

1. What Is Atomic Structure?

Everything around you is made from matter. Your body, the air, water, your phone, the screen you are looking at, and even the stars are made from matter.

Matter is made from particles called atoms. An atom is the smallest particle that still represents a particular chemical element.

Think of an atom as an extremely tiny system. At its centre is a dense nucleus containing protons and neutrons. Around that nucleus is an electron cloud containing electrons.

Modern atomic theory is much more sophisticated than the simple picture of electrons moving around a nucleus like planets around the Sun. Electrons are described using probability, energy levels, orbitals and quantum mechanics.

The most important idea

The number of protons determines what element an atom is. Changing the number of protons changes the element itself.

2. The Story of the Atom

Atomic structure was not discovered in one experiment. It was built gradually as scientists found evidence that forced them to improve their models.

Democritus

Around the fifth century BCE, the Greek philosopher Democritus suggested that matter could be divided into extremely small particles that could not be divided forever. He called these hypothetical particles atomos, meaning indivisible.

John Dalton

In the early nineteenth century, Dalton developed a scientific atomic theory. He proposed that matter was composed of atoms and that atoms of different elements had different properties.

Dalton's original idea that all atoms of one element were identical in mass was later modified because scientists discovered isotopes.

J. J. Thomson

Thomson's cathode-ray experiments provided evidence for negatively charged particles later called electrons. This showed that atoms were not indivisible.

Ernest Rutherford

Rutherford's famous gold-foil experiment showed that the positive charge and most of an atom's mass are concentrated in a tiny nucleus. Most of the atom is empty space.

Niels Bohr

Bohr proposed that electrons could occupy specific allowed energy levels. His model helped explain the line spectrum of hydrogen.

James Chadwick

Chadwick discovered the neutron, explaining why nuclei could contain substantial mass without all of that mass coming from positively charged particles.

The Quantum Mechanical Model

Later work by scientists including de Broglie, Heisenberg and Schrödinger led to the modern quantum description of electrons. Electrons are not assigned simple circular paths. Instead, orbitals describe regions of high probability for finding electrons.

3. Inside an Atom

An atom contains three major subatomic particles: protons, neutrons and electrons.

Particle Charge Location Relative Mass
Proton +1 Nucleus About 1
Neutron 0 Nucleus About 1
Electron -1 Electron cloud Much smaller

The nucleus is extremely small compared with the whole atom, but it contains almost all of the atom's mass. The electrons occupy most of the atom's volume.

This distinction is important: mass is concentrated mainly in the nucleus, while the electron cloud largely determines the size and chemical behaviour of the atom.

4. The Three Fundamental Particles in Basic Chemistry

Protons

A proton has a positive electrical charge. Protons are found inside the nucleus.

The number of protons is called the atomic number. This number identifies the element.

Neutrons

Neutrons have no net electrical charge. They are also found in the nucleus.

Changing the number of neutrons does not change the element. Instead, it creates a different isotope of the same element.

Electrons

Electrons have a negative electrical charge and occupy regions around the nucleus.

Electrons are extremely important in chemistry because chemical bonding and many chemical reactions involve changes in electron arrangement.

5. The Nucleus

The nucleus is the compact central region of an atom. It contains protons and neutrons, collectively called nucleons.

Because protons are positively charged, they repel one another electrically. Yet nuclei can remain together because of the strong nuclear interaction acting over extremely short distances.

Remember

Nucleus = protons + neutrons. Electrons are outside the nucleus.

6. Atomic Number

The atomic number, represented by Z, is the number of protons in the nucleus.

Atomic number Z = number of protons

This is one of the most important facts in atomic structure. Every element has its own atomic number.

Example: Sodium

Sodium has atomic number 11.

Therefore, every sodium atom contains 11 protons.

Example: Oxygen

Oxygen has atomic number 8.

Therefore, every oxygen atom contains 8 protons.

7. Mass Number

The mass number, represented by A, is the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.

Mass number A = protons + neutrons

Therefore:

Finding neutrons neutrons = A - Z

Example

An atom has mass number 23 and atomic number 11. How many neutrons does it have?

Neutrons = 23 - 11

Neutrons = 12

8. Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

How to recognise isotopes

Same atomic number → same element. Different mass number → different isotope.

Example: Carbon

Carbon-12 has 6 protons and 6 neutrons.

Carbon-14 has 6 protons and 8 neutrons.

Both are carbon because both contain 6 protons.

Isotopic notation

AZX

X represents the element symbol, A is the mass number and Z is the atomic number.

Why isotopes matter

  • Some isotopes are stable.
  • Some isotopes are radioactive.
  • Isotopes are used in medicine.
  • They can be used as tracers.
  • They are important in archaeology and geology.
  • They are used in scientific measurements and research.

9. Ions

An ion is an atom or group of atoms with a net electrical charge. The charge occurs when the number of electrons is different from the number of protons.

Cations

A positively charged ion is called a cation. It forms when an atom loses electrons.

Example: Sodium ion

Neutral sodium has 11 protons and 11 electrons.

If sodium loses one electron, it has 11 protons and 10 electrons.

Its charge becomes +1, giving Na+.

Anions

A negatively charged ion is called an anion. It forms when an atom gains electrons.

Example: Chloride ion

Neutral chlorine has 17 protons and 17 electrons.

If chlorine gains one electron, it has 17 protons and 18 electrons.

Its charge becomes -1, giving Cl-.

Charge = protons - electrons

10. Relative Atomic Mass

The mass of an individual atom is extremely small, so chemists use the atomic mass unit, written as u or commonly amu.

The atomic mass unit is defined relative to carbon-12.

The value shown on the periodic table is usually an average atomic mass, reflecting the naturally occurring isotopes and their abundances.

Average atomic mass = Σ(isotope mass × fractional abundance)

Worked idea

Suppose an imaginary element has two isotopes:

Isotope A = 10 u and abundance = 70%

Isotope B = 11 u and abundance = 30%

Convert the percentages into fractions:

70% = 0.70

30% = 0.30

Average mass = (10 × 0.70) + (11 × 0.30)

Average mass = 7.0 + 3.3

Average mass = 10.3 u

11. The Bohr Model

Bohr proposed that electrons could occupy specific allowed energy levels around the nucleus.

The model is not the complete modern description of the atom, but it is extremely useful for introducing energy levels and explaining the hydrogen spectrum.

Ground state

The ground state is the lowest-energy state available to an atom.

Excited state

When an electron absorbs energy, it can move to a higher allowed energy state. This is called an excited state.

Returning to a lower level

When an electron falls from a higher energy level to a lower one, energy can be released as electromagnetic radiation.

The big idea

Electrons do not possess arbitrary energies in bound atoms. They occupy allowed energy states.

12. Electron Shells and Energy Levels

Electron shells represent principal energy levels. They are commonly labelled using numbers:

  • n = 1
  • n = 2
  • n = 3
  • n = 4
  • and so on.

In the simplified shell model, electrons farther from the nucleus generally occupy higher-energy levels.

Maximum electrons in a shell

A commonly taught rule gives the maximum number of electrons in principal shell n as:

Maximum electrons = 2n²

Example

For the second shell:

2(2²) = 8

So the second principal shell can contain up to 8 electrons.

Important qualification

The 2n² rule gives the maximum capacity of a principal shell. It should not be confused with the actual order in which orbitals fill in multi-electron atoms.

13. Orbitals

The modern atomic model does not describe an electron as travelling around the nucleus on a fixed circular track.

Instead, an orbital is a mathematical description of a region of space in which there is a high probability of finding an electron with particular quantum numbers.

s orbitals

An s orbital is approximately spherical in shape.

p orbitals

p orbitals have two-lobed shapes. There are three p orbitals in a p subshell.

d orbitals

d orbitals have more complicated shapes and orientations. A d subshell contains five orbitals.

f orbitals

f orbitals are even more complex. An f subshell contains seven orbitals.

Subshell Number of Orbitals Maximum Electrons
s 1 2
p 3 6
d 5 10
f 7 14

14. Electron Configuration

Electron configuration describes how the electrons of an atom are distributed among its orbitals.

The Aufbau principle

The Aufbau principle states that electrons occupy available orbitals in an order associated with increasing energy.

Pauli exclusion principle

No two electrons in the same atom can have exactly the same set of four quantum numbers.

Consequently, an orbital can contain a maximum of two electrons, and those two electrons must have opposite spins.

Hund's rule

When orbitals of equal energy are available, electrons occupy them singly before pairing.

Common filling order

1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p → 5s → 4d → 5p → 6s → 4f → 5d → 6p → 7s → 5f → 6d → 7p

Example: Oxygen

Oxygen has atomic number 8, so a neutral oxygen atom has eight electrons.

Its electron configuration is:

1s² 2s² 2p⁴

The superscripts add to 8:

2 + 2 + 4 = 8

Example: Sodium

Sodium has atomic number 11.

1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹

Sodium therefore has one electron in its outermost occupied s orbital.

15. Quantum Numbers

Quantum numbers provide a way of describing the state of an electron in an atom.

Principal quantum number (n)

The principal quantum number identifies the main energy level and is a positive integer such as 1, 2, 3 or 4.

Azimuthal quantum number (l)

This identifies the subshell type:

  • l = 0 → s
  • l = 1 → p
  • l = 2 → d
  • l = 3 → f

Magnetic quantum number (mₗ)

This describes the orientation of an orbital within a subshell.

Spin quantum number (mₛ)

Electron spin has two allowed projections, commonly represented as +1/2 and -1/2.

16. Valence Electrons

Valence electrons are the electrons involved most directly in many chemical bonding and reaction processes.

For main-group elements, the number and arrangement of outer electrons help explain patterns in chemical behaviour.

Example: Sodium

Sodium has the simplified shell arrangement 2, 8, 1.

It has one electron in its outer shell and commonly forms a +1 ion by losing that electron.

Example: Chlorine

Chlorine has the simplified shell arrangement 2, 8, 7.

It has seven outer-shell electrons and commonly gains one electron to form Cl⁻.

17. Atomic Emission Spectra

When atoms absorb energy, their electrons can move to higher energy states. When those electrons return to lower states, electromagnetic radiation can be emitted.

Because the allowed energy differences are discrete, atoms can produce characteristic spectral lines.

ΔE = hν

Here ΔE represents the energy difference, h is Planck's constant, and ν is the frequency of the radiation.

Since electromagnetic radiation also obeys:

c = λν

the wavelength can be related to the energy of the emitted photon.

Why spectra are useful

Different atoms have different allowed energy structures. Their spectral patterns can therefore act like fingerprints that help identify elements.

18. Atomic Structure and the Periodic Table

The periodic table is closely connected to atomic structure. Elements are arranged according to increasing atomic number.

The position of an element tells us useful information about its electron arrangement and chemical behaviour.

Periods

A period is a horizontal row of the periodic table. Elements in a period generally have electrons occupying the same principal energy level as the highest occupied shell, though the detailed orbital filling changes across the row.

Groups

A group is a vertical column. Elements in the same main-group family often have similar valence-electron patterns and therefore similar chemical properties.

Blocks

  • s-block
  • p-block
  • d-block
  • f-block

These blocks correspond to the type of subshell being filled by the differentiating electrons.

19. Atomic Structure and Periodic Trends

Atomic radius

Atomic radius generally decreases from left to right across a period because the nuclear charge increases while electrons are added to the same principal shell.

Atomic radius generally increases down a group because additional electron shells are occupied.

Ionization energy

Ionization energy is the energy required to remove an electron from an atom or ion in the appropriate gaseous state.

First ionization energy generally increases across a period and generally decreases down a group, although there are important exceptions.

Electronegativity

Electronegativity describes the tendency of an atom in a chemical bond to attract electron density toward itself.

It generally increases toward the upper-right region of the periodic table, with fluorine having the highest commonly cited Pauling electronegativity.

Shielding

Inner electrons partially shield outer electrons from the full attraction of the positively charged nucleus.

Effective nuclear charge

Effective nuclear charge describes the net attractive influence experienced by an electron after accounting for shielding effects.

Think of the periodic table as an electron map

Many periodic trends become easier when you stop memorising arrows and instead ask: How strongly is the nucleus attracting the electron, how much shielding exists, and which energy level is occupied?

20. Photoelectron Spectroscopy

Photoelectron spectroscopy provides experimental evidence about the energies of electrons in atoms.

In a simplified description, photons are used to eject electrons from an atom. Measuring the kinetic energy of the emitted electrons provides information about their binding energies.

Ebinding = Ephoton - Ekinetic

Different groups of electrons produce different signals, allowing scientists to infer occupied subshells and distinguish core electrons from more weakly bound electrons.

This is one example of how the modern atomic model is supported by experimental evidence rather than simply being a picture invented by scientists.

21. Atomic Symbolism and Calculations

A chemical symbol can be written with the mass number and atomic number around it.

AZX

Finding protons

Protons = atomic number.

Finding neutrons

Neutrons = mass number - atomic number.

Finding electrons in a neutral atom

Electrons = protons.

Finding electrons in an ion

For a positive ion, electrons have been lost.

For a negative ion, electrons have been gained.

Example: ²⁷₁₃Al³⁺

Atomic number = 13

Therefore protons = 13.

Mass number = 27.

Neutrons = 27 - 13 = 14.

The 3+ charge means the atom has lost three electrons.

Electrons = 13 - 3 = 10.

Answer: 13 protons, 14 neutrons and 10 electrons.

22. Rutherford's Gold-Foil Experiment

Rutherford's experiment is one of the most important experiments in the history of chemistry and physics.

Alpha particles were directed toward a very thin sheet of gold. If positive charge were spread uniformly through the atom, the particles should mostly pass through with only small deflections.

Most particles did pass through. However, some were deflected by large angles, and a very small number were scattered backward.

Rutherford's interpretation was that the atom must contain a very small, dense, positively charged nucleus.

Conclusion

Most of an atom is empty space, while nearly all of its mass and positive charge are concentrated in a tiny nucleus.

23. Why Atomic Structure Matters in Chemistry

Atomic structure is not just a chapter to memorise for an exam. It explains why chemistry behaves the way it does.

  • Electron arrangement helps explain chemical bonding.
  • Valence electrons help explain reactivity.
  • Atomic number identifies the element.
  • Isotopes explain differences in atomic mass.
  • Electron energy levels explain atomic spectra.
  • Nuclear structure explains radioactive isotopes.
  • Electron configurations help explain periodic trends.
  • Atomic structure provides the foundation for understanding molecules, compounds and chemical reactions.

24. Atomic Structure Word Problems

Question 1

An atom has 17 protons and 18 neutrons. Determine its atomic number and mass number.

Atomic number = number of protons = 17.

Mass number = protons + neutrons.

Mass number = 17 + 18 = 35.

Answer: atomic number = 17, mass number = 35.

Question 2

An ion contains 12 protons and 10 electrons. What is its charge?

Charge = protons - electrons.

Charge = 12 - 10 = +2.

The ion is 2+.

Question 3

An atom has atomic number 8 and mass number 18. How many protons, neutrons and electrons does a neutral atom contain?

Protons = 8.

Neutrons = 18 - 8 = 10.

Because the atom is neutral, electrons = 8.

Answer: 8 protons, 10 neutrons and 8 electrons.

Question 4

An element has two naturally occurring isotopes. Isotope A has mass 10 u and abundance 20%. Isotope B has mass 11 u and abundance 80%. Find the average atomic mass.

Average mass = (10 × 0.20) + (11 × 0.80)

= 2.0 + 8.8

Average atomic mass = 10.8 u.

25. Common Mistakes

  • Confusing atomic number with mass number.
  • Thinking neutrons determine the element.
  • Forgetting that ions have unequal numbers of protons and electrons.
  • Saying that isotopes have different numbers of protons.
  • Treating an orbital as a literal circular path.
  • Assuming the Bohr model is the complete modern atomic model.
  • Forgetting that the periodic-table atomic mass is generally an isotope-weighted average.
  • Mixing up ion charge and number of neutrons.
  • Forgetting that positive ions have lost electrons.
  • Forgetting that negative ions have gained electrons.

26. Exam Practice

Question 1

Define atomic number.

Answer: The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom.

Question 2

What are isotopes?

Answer: Atoms of the same element having the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

Question 3

An atom contains 19 protons and 20 neutrons. Find its mass number.

Answer: 19 + 20 = 39.

Question 4

Explain why an Na⁺ ion has fewer electrons than a neutral sodium atom.

Answer: Sodium forms Na⁺ by losing one electron. Its number of protons remains unchanged.

Question 5

Write the electron configuration of carbon.

Carbon has atomic number 6.

1s² 2s² 2p²

Question 6

Explain the main conclusion of Rutherford's gold-foil experiment.

Answer: The atom contains a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus and most of the atom is empty space.
