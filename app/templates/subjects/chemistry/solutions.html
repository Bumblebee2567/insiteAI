Solutions

Solutions are homogeneous mixtures in which one substance is uniformly distributed throughout another. This guide explains solubility, solute and solvent, concentration, molarity, dilution, solution preparation, saturation, solubility curves, calculations, practical applications and common mistakes.

1. Introduction to Solutions

A solution is a homogeneous mixture. This means that its composition is uniform throughout the mixture.

For example, when sodium chloride dissolves completely in water, the salt particles become distributed throughout the water. The resulting mixture is a salt solution.

Key idea: A solution contains a substance being dissolved and a substance that does the dissolving.

Solute

The substance that is dissolved in a solution.

Solvent

The substance that dissolves the solute.

Solution

The homogeneous mixture formed when the solute dissolves in the solvent.

Concentration

A measure of how much solute is present in a given amount of solution.

2. Solute and Solvent

Solute

The solute is the substance that gets dissolved. It is often present in a smaller amount than the solvent.

In salt water, sodium chloride is the solute.

Solvent

The solvent is the substance that dissolves the solute. Water is one of the most common solvents in chemistry.

Example

Identify the solute and solvent.

A student dissolves 10 g of sugar in 200 mL of water.

Solute: Sugar
Solvent: Water
Solution: Sugar solution

3. Types of Solutions

Solutions can exist in different physical states depending on the physical states of their solute and solvent.

Solution Example
Solid in liquid Salt dissolved in water
Liquid in liquid Ethanol mixed with water
Gas in liquid Carbon dioxide dissolved in soft drinks
Gas in gas Air
Solid in solid Alloys such as brass

4. Solubility

Solubility is the maximum amount of a substance that can dissolve in a particular amount of solvent under specified conditions of temperature and pressure.

A substance that dissolves easily is described as soluble, while a substance that does not dissolve significantly is described as insoluble or sparingly soluble.

Important distinction: Solubility describes how much can dissolve, while concentration describes how much is actually present in a particular solution.

Example

If 35 g of a salt can dissolve in 100 g of water at a certain temperature, that value represents the solubility of the salt under those conditions.

5. Factors Affecting Solubility

Temperature

For many solid solutes, increasing temperature increases solubility, although the exact behaviour depends on the substance.

Gases generally become less soluble in liquids as temperature increases.

Pressure

Pressure has a major effect on gases dissolved in liquids. Increasing pressure generally increases the solubility of a gas in a liquid.

Nature of the Solute and Solvent

Substances with similar intermolecular characteristics tend to dissolve more readily in one another.

Remember: "Like dissolves like" is a useful general rule for understanding why some substances dissolve better than others.

6. Unsaturated, Saturated and Supersaturated Solutions

Unsaturated Solution

An unsaturated solution contains less dissolved solute than the maximum amount that can dissolve under the given conditions.

Saturated Solution

A saturated solution contains the maximum amount of dissolved solute possible under the specified conditions.

Supersaturated Solution

A supersaturated solution contains more dissolved solute than would normally be stable under those conditions. Such solutions can be unstable and may crystallize when disturbed.

Type Description
Unsaturated More solute can still dissolve.
Saturated No more significant solute can dissolve under the conditions.
Supersaturated Contains an unusually large amount of dissolved solute and may be unstable.

7. Concentration

Concentration tells us how much solute is present relative to a specified amount of solution or solvent.

There are several ways of expressing concentration in chemistry. At high-school level, common forms include molarity, mass concentration and percentage concentration.

Why Concentration Matters

Two solutions can contain the same solute but have very different concentrations.

A solution containing 1 mole of sodium chloride in 1 litre of solution is more concentrated than one containing 0.1 mole in the same volume.

8. Molarity

Molarity is one of the most important concentration units used in chemistry.

It is defined as the number of moles of solute present per litre of solution.

C = n / V

Where:

  • C = concentration in mol/L
  • n = number of moles of solute
  • V = volume of solution in litres

Finding Moles

n = C × V

Finding Volume

V = n / C

Worked Example

Calculate the concentration of a solution containing 0.50 mol of sodium chloride in 2.0 L of solution.

C = n / V

C = 0.50 / 2.0

C = 0.25 mol/L

9. Mass Concentration

Mass concentration expresses the mass of solute present per unit volume of solution.

Mass concentration = mass of solute / volume of solution

A common unit is g/L.

Example

A solution contains 20 g of glucose in 2 L of solution. Calculate its mass concentration.

Mass concentration = 20 / 2

= 10 g/L

10. Dilution

Dilution is the process of reducing the concentration of a solution by adding more solvent.

During an ideal dilution, the amount of solute remains constant. What changes is the total volume of the solution.

C₁V₁ = C₂V₂

Where:

  • C₁ = initial concentration
  • V₁ = initial volume
  • C₂ = final concentration
  • V₂ = final volume
Key idea: Dilution does not destroy the solute. The same amount of solute is simply spread throughout a larger volume.

Worked Example

100 mL of a 2.0 mol/L solution is diluted to 500 mL. Find the new concentration.

C₁V₁ = C₂V₂

(2.0)(100) = C₂(500)

C₂ = 200 / 500

C₂ = 0.40 mol/L

11. Solution Preparation

Preparing a solution of known concentration is an important laboratory skill.

Preparing a Solution from a Solid

To prepare a solution with a known concentration, the required amount of solute is first calculated and accurately measured.

General Procedure

  1. Calculate the required number of moles.
  2. Convert the number of moles into mass using the molar mass.
  3. Accurately weigh the required solute.
  4. Dissolve the solute in a suitable amount of distilled water.
  5. Transfer the solution into a volumetric flask.
  6. Add distilled water until the solution reaches the required volume mark.
  7. Stopper the flask and mix thoroughly.

Mass Required for a Molar Solution

n = C × V
mass = n × molar mass

Worked Example

Calculate the mass of NaCl required to prepare 500 mL of a 0.20 mol/L NaCl solution.

Molar mass of NaCl ≈ 58.5 g/mol.

Convert volume:

500 mL = 0.500 L

n = C × V

n = 0.20 × 0.500

n = 0.100 mol

mass = n × molar mass

mass = 0.100 × 58.5

mass = 5.85 g

12. Solubility Curves

A solubility curve shows how the solubility of a substance changes with temperature.

The horizontal axis commonly represents temperature, while the vertical axis represents the amount of solute that can dissolve in a specified amount of solvent.

Reading a Solubility Curve

To determine the solubility at a particular temperature, locate the temperature on the horizontal axis and move vertically until you reach the substance's curve. Then read the corresponding solubility value.

Comparing Substances

A curve that lies higher at a particular temperature represents a substance with greater solubility at that temperature.

Important: Solubility curves describe maximum dissolving capacity under specified conditions. They are not automatically the same thing as concentration.

13. Worked Examples

Example 1 — Molarity

Find the concentration of a solution containing 0.75 mol of solute in 1.5 L of solution.

C = n / V

C = 0.75 / 1.5

C = 0.50 mol/L

Example 2 — Finding Moles

How many moles are present in 250 mL of a 0.40 mol/L solution?

V = 250 mL = 0.250 L

n = C × V

n = 0.40 × 0.250

n = 0.100 mol

Example 3 — Dilution

What volume of a 5.0 mol/L stock solution is required to prepare 250 mL of a 1.0 mol/L solution?

C₁V₁ = C₂V₂

(5.0)V₁ = (1.0)(250)

V₁ = 250 / 5.0

V₁ = 50 mL

Example 4 — Mass Concentration

A solution contains 15 g of solute in 500 mL of solution. Find its concentration in g/L.

500 mL = 0.500 L

Concentration = 15 / 0.500

= 30 g/L

14. Solution Word Problems

Problem 1

A student dissolves 5.0 g of potassium chloride in enough water to make 250 mL of solution. Calculate the mass concentration in g/L.

250 mL = 0.250 L

Mass concentration = 5.0 / 0.250

= 20 g/L

Problem 2

A laboratory has 100 mL of a 4.0 mol/L stock solution. It is diluted until its concentration becomes 1.0 mol/L. What is the final volume?

C₁V₁ = C₂V₂

(4.0)(100) = (1.0)V₂

V₂ = 400 mL

Problem 3

A student needs 500 mL of a 0.10 mol/L solution. How many moles of solute are required?

V = 500 mL = 0.500 L

n = C × V

n = 0.10 × 0.500

n = 0.050 mol

Problem 4 — Conceptual

Why does adding water to a salt solution decrease its concentration?

The amount of dissolved salt remains essentially the same, but the total volume of the solution increases. Therefore, the same amount of solute is distributed through a larger volume, reducing the concentration.

15. Common Mistakes

1. Confusing Solubility and Concentration

Solubility is the maximum amount that can dissolve under specified conditions, while concentration describes the amount actually present relative to a specified quantity of solution.

2. Forgetting to Convert Millilitres to Litres

When using molarity in mol/L, the volume must be expressed in litres.

1000 mL = 1 L

3. Using the Wrong Volume in Molarity

Molarity uses the volume of the entire solution, not simply the volume of solvent added.

4. Forgetting Dilution Principles

During dilution, the amount of solute remains constant even though the concentration decreases.

5. Incorrectly Calculating Moles

When mass is given, calculate moles using the molar mass before using the molarity equation.

n = mass / molar mass
Best approach: Write down what you know, convert all units, identify the quantity being asked for, choose the appropriate formula, then substitute carefully.
