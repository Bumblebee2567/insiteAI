1. What Is Reaction Rate?

The rate of a reaction describes how quickly reactants are converted into products. A fast reaction produces products rapidly, while a slow reaction takes more time.

The big idea

Reactions happen when particles collide successfully. The more successful collisions that occur each second, the faster the reaction.

Rate ↑

More successful collisions per second

2. Collision Theory

Collision theory explains that particles must collide before a reaction can occur. However, simply touching is not enough. A collision must have enough energy and the correct orientation.

Successful collision = sufficient energy + correct orientation

Effective Collision

Particles have enough energy and the correct orientation. Bonds can break and new bonds can form.

Ineffective Collision

Particles collide, but they do not have enough energy or the orientation is unsuitable.

3. Factors Affecting Reaction Rate

Several factors can change the frequency and success of collisions.

Temperature

Increasing temperature makes particles move faster and increases the proportion of particles with enough energy to react.

Concentration

A higher concentration means more particles occupy the same volume, increasing collision frequency.

Pressure

For gases, increasing pressure brings particles closer together and increases collision frequency.

Surface Area

Breaking a solid into smaller pieces exposes more surface for collisions, usually increasing the reaction rate.

Catalyst

A catalyst provides an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy.

Nature of Reactants

Different substances have different bonding and structural properties, so they can react at different rates.

4. Concentration and Gas Pressure

Liquid Solutions: Concentration

Increasing the concentration of reactants in a solution means there are more reactant particles packed into the same volume of liquid.

More Particles: Higher concentration = More reactant particles per unit volume.
Shorter Distance: Particles are closer together, making collisions more frequent.
Higher Frequency: The frequency of successful collisions increases, speeding up the reaction.

Gaseous Mixtures: Pressure

For gases, increasing the pressure (by compressing the gas into a smaller container) has the exact same physical effect as increasing concentration.

Higher Gas Pressure = More gas particles per unit volume = More frequent collisions

5. Temperature

Temperature is a direct measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a substance. Increasing the temperature has a highly significant, double-action effect on reaction rate.

1. Faster Movement: Particles gain kinetic energy and move faster. This leads to more frequent collisions per unit time.
2. Harder Collisions (Dominant Effect): Because particles move with much higher energy, a significantly larger fraction of collisions occur with energy greater than or equal to the activation energy (Ea).
Exam Tip: When explaining temperature, always mention that the main reason rate increases is because a larger fraction of colliding particles have energy >= Ea. The increase in collision speed/frequency is a minor contributor.

6. Surface Area of Solids

When a solid reacts with a liquid or gas, the reaction can only occur at the boundary interface where the solid surface meets the fluid. Particles trapped inside the bulk solid cannot react until the outer layers are stripped away.

Single Large Lump

Low surface-area-to-volume ratio. Fewer particles are exposed on the outer surface to collide with surrounding reactants.

Powdered Solid

High surface-area-to-volume ratio. Far more atoms are exposed simultaneously, leading to a much higher collision frequency.

Example Reaction: Acid and Calcium Carbonate (Marble)
CaCO3 (solid lump) + 2HCl (aq) → CaCl2 (aq) + H2O (l) + CO2 (g) [Slower Rate] CaCO3 (fine powder) + 2HCl (aq) → CaCl2 (aq) + H2O (l) + CO2 (g) [Much Faster Rate]

7. Nature of Reactants

Even under the exact same temperature, concentration, and surface area, different substances react at completely different rates. This is due to their chemical identities, bonding structures, and state of matter.

Bond Types (Covalent vs. Ionic): Reactions between simple ions in aqueous solution (e.g., Ag+ and Cl-) are almost instantaneous because no strong covalent bonds need to be broken. Reactions involving the breaking of strong covalent bonds (e.g., organic combustion) are much slower.
State of Matter: Gases and liquids react faster than solids because their particles are highly mobile and can mix thoroughly to maximize contact.
Inherent Reactivity Trends: Highly active metals (like Sodium or Potassium) react vigorously with water, while less active metals (like Magnesium or Zinc) react incredibly slowly or require steam to react.

8. Catalysts

A catalyst increases the reaction rate by providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy.

Without Catalyst

Higher activation energy → fewer particles have enough energy → slower reaction.

With Catalyst

Lower activation energy → more successful collisions → faster reaction.

Catalyst lowers Ea It does not change the overall energy change of the reaction.

9. Activation Energy

Even when particles collide, they need enough energy to reach the transition state. The minimum energy required is called activation energy, Ea.

a ΔH Reactants Products Transition state Energy Reaction progress
Ea = Energy of transition state - Energy of reactants

10. Experimental Methods to Measure Rates

To track a reaction rate experimentally, we must measure how a physical property changes over time. Three standard laboratory setups are used depending on the reactants:

Mass Loss Method

Place the reaction on a digital balance. If a gas (like CO2) escapes, the mass drops. Record the decreasing mass at regular time intervals.

Gas Syringe Method

Connect a sealed flask to a graduated gas syringe. Measure the volume of gas produced (like H2 or O2) at set intervals (e.g. every 15 seconds).

Disappearing Cross Method

For precipitation reactions (like sulfur formation). Put a flask over an 'X' on paper. Time how long it takes for the precipitate to block the cross.

11. Rate Graph Analysis

Plotting experimental data (like gas volume vs. time) reveals a distinct curve. The shape of this curve tells you exactly how the reaction rate evolves:

Steep Slope (Start): Steepest gradient at the beginning. Reactant concentrations are high, collisions are highly frequent, and the reaction is at its fastest.
Gentle Slope (Middle): The slope curves and flattens out. Reactants are being used up, concentration drops, collisions become less frequent, and the rate slows down.
Flat Line (End): The curve becomes completely horizontal. One or both reactants are fully depleted, collisions cease, and the reaction has stopped.
Calculating Gradient:
• To find the instantaneous rate at a specific time "t", draw a straight tangent line touching the curve at "t".
• Find the slope of that tangent: Gradient = (Change in y-axis) / (Change in x-axis).

12. Worked Examples

Example 1 — Temperature

Explain why increasing the temperature usually increases reaction rate.

Higher temperature gives particles more kinetic energy. They collide more frequently and a greater proportion of collisions have enough energy to overcome Ea.

Example 2 — Concentration

Why does increasing the concentration of a solution usually increase reaction rate?

There are more reactant particles in the same volume, so collisions occur more frequently.

Example 3 — Surface Area

Which reacts faster: a large lump of calcium carbonate or the same mass of powdered calcium carbonate?

The powdered calcium carbonate reacts faster because it has a much larger surface area available for collisions.
