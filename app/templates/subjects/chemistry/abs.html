1. Introduction to Acids, Bases and Salts

Acids and bases are two important groups of chemical substances. They behave differently because of the particles they produce when dissolved in water.

Acids are commonly associated with sour substances, while bases are commonly associated with alkaline substances. In chemistry, however, these everyday descriptions are replaced by precise definitions based on chemical behaviour.

When an acid reacts with a base, a neutralisation reaction can occur. One of the major products is usually a salt.

Big idea: Acids, bases and salts are connected. Understanding acids and bases makes it much easier to understand neutralisation and salt formation.

Acid

A substance that can donate hydrogen ions or increase the hydrogen-ion concentration of an aqueous solution, depending on the acid-base theory being used.

Base

A substance that can accept hydrogen ions or produce hydroxide ions in water, depending on the theory.

Salt

An ionic compound formed from the reaction of an acid with a base or through other suitable chemical reactions.

Neutralisation

A reaction in which acidic and basic properties are reduced by reaction between the two substances.

2. Acids

An acid is a substance that produces hydrogen ions in aqueous solution according to the Arrhenius definition.

Many acids contain hydrogen in their formula. Examples include hydrochloric acid, sulfuric acid, nitric acid and ethanoic acid.

Examples of Common Acids

Acid Formula Common Use
Hydrochloric acid HCl Laboratory work and industrial processes
Sulfuric acid H₂SO₄ Fertilisers, batteries and industry
Nitric acid HNO₃ Fertilisers and chemical manufacture
Ethanoic acid CH₃COOH Present in vinegar

Hydrogen Ions in Water

In water, a bare H⁺ ion does not exist as an isolated particle for any significant time. It associates with water molecules, commonly represented using the hydronium ion H₃O⁺.

H⁺ + H₂O → H₃O⁺

3. Bases

A base is a substance that can accept a proton according to the Brønsted-Lowry definition.

Under the Arrhenius definition, a base produces hydroxide ions in aqueous solution.

Examples of Bases

Base Formula
Sodium hydroxide NaOH
Potassium hydroxide KOH
Calcium hydroxide Ca(OH)₂
Ammonia NH₃

Alkalis

An alkali is a base that is soluble in water and produces hydroxide ions in aqueous solution.

Important: All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis.

4. Theories of Acids and Bases

Arrhenius Theory

According to Arrhenius, an acid increases the concentration of H⁺ in aqueous solution, while a base increases the concentration of OH⁻.

HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻
NaOH → Na⁺ + OH⁻

Brønsted-Lowry Theory

A Brønsted-Lowry acid is a proton donor, while a Brønsted-Lowry base is a proton acceptor.

NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻

In this reaction, water donates a proton and behaves as an acid, while ammonia accepts a proton and behaves as a base.

Lewis Theory

A Lewis acid accepts an electron pair, while a Lewis base donates an electron pair.

Remember: Arrhenius focuses on H⁺ and OH⁻ in water, Brønsted-Lowry focuses on proton transfer, while Lewis focuses on electron-pair transfer.

5. Properties of Acids and Bases

Properties of Acids

  • They have pH values below 7 at 25°C when aqueous.
  • They react with many metals to produce hydrogen gas.
  • They react with carbonates to produce carbon dioxide.
  • They can react with bases in neutralisation reactions.
  • Strong aqueous acids conduct electricity because they produce ions.

Properties of Bases

  • They have pH values above 7 at 25°C when aqueous.
  • Many bases feel slippery, although substances should never be tested by touching them.
  • They react with acids during neutralisation.
  • Alkalis conduct electricity in aqueous solution because they contain mobile ions.

6. Acid-Base Indicators

Indicators are substances that change colour depending on the acidity or alkalinity of a solution.

Indicator Acidic Colour Alkaline Colour
Litmus Red Blue
Methyl orange Red Yellow
Phenolphthalein Colourless Pink

Universal Indicator

Universal indicator contains a mixture of indicators and gives different colours over a broad pH range.

It can therefore give an approximate measurement of pH rather than simply indicating whether a substance is acidic or alkaline.

7. pH and pOH

The pH scale is used to express the acidity of an aqueous solution.

pH = -log₁₀[H⁺]

Here, [H⁺] represents the hydrogen-ion concentration in mol/L.

pOH

pOH = -log₁₀[OH⁻]

At 25°C, for aqueous solutions:

pH + pOH = 14

Understanding the Scale

pH Nature
Below 7 Acidic
7 Neutral at 25°C
Above 7 Alkaline

Worked Example

Find the pH of a solution with [H⁺] = 1.0 × 10⁻³ mol/L.

pH = -log₁₀[H⁺]

pH = -log₁₀(1.0 × 10⁻³)

pH = 3

8. Strong and Weak Acids and Bases

The terms strong and weak describe the extent to which an acid or base ionises in water. They do not simply mean concentrated and dilute.

Strong Acid

A strong acid ionises extensively in aqueous solution.

Examples include HCl, HNO₃ and H₂SO₄, although the details of their ionisation steps differ.

Weak Acid

A weak acid ionises only partially and establishes an equilibrium between molecules and ions.

CH₃COOH ⇌ H⁺ + CH₃COO⁻

Strong and Weak Bases

Strong bases dissociate extensively or react strongly with water, while weak bases establish equilibria and only partially produce their conjugate-acid/hydroxide products.

Very important: Strong does not mean concentrated, and weak does not mean dilute. A strong acid can be dilute, while a weak acid can be relatively concentrated.

9. Neutralisation

Neutralisation is the reaction between an acid and a base. In the simplest strong acid-strong base case, hydrogen ions react with hydroxide ions to form water.

H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O

Example

HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O

Hydrochloric acid reacts with sodium hydroxide to produce sodium chloride and water.

Another Example

H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O

The equation must be balanced because atoms and charge are conserved.

10. Reactions of Acids

Acid + Metal

Many acids react with reactive metals to form a salt and hydrogen gas.

Acid + Metal → Salt + Hydrogen
2HCl + Mg → MgCl₂ + H₂

Acid + Metal Oxide

Acid + Metal Oxide → Salt + Water
2HCl + CuO → CuCl₂ + H₂O

Acid + Carbonate

Acid + Carbonate → Salt + Water + Carbon Dioxide
2HCl + CaCO₃ → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂

Acid + Hydrogen Carbonate

HCl + NaHCO₃ → NaCl + H₂O + CO₂
Test for carbon dioxide: Carbon dioxide turns limewater milky or cloudy.

11. Salts

A salt is an ionic compound containing positive ions other than H⁺ and negative ions other than OH⁻.

Salts can be formed in several ways, including acid-base neutralisation, reactions between acids and metals, and reactions involving carbonates.

Examples

Salt Formula Possible Source
Sodium chloride NaCl HCl + NaOH
Copper(II) sulfate CuSO₄ H₂SO₄ + CuO
Calcium chloride CaCl₂ HCl + CaCO₃

Normal Salts

A normal salt is formed when all replaceable hydrogen ions of the acid have been replaced by metal ions or ammonium ions.

Acid Salts

Acid salts retain hydrogen that can potentially participate in further acid-base reactions. Examples include NaHCO₃ and NaHSO₄.

12. Preparation of Salts

Method 1 — Acid + Soluble Base

When both reactants are soluble and there is no obvious way to separate excess solid directly, titration can be used to obtain an accurately neutralised solution.

Method 2 — Acid + Insoluble Base

An insoluble base can be added to an acid until no more reacts. Excess solid can then be removed by filtration and the solution can be concentrated and crystallised.

Method 3 — Acid + Carbonate

An acid can react with a carbonate to produce a salt, water and carbon dioxide.

Method 4 — Precipitation

Two soluble solutions can react to form an insoluble salt. The solid product is called a precipitate.

AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl + NaNO₃

Silver chloride forms as an insoluble precipitate.

13. Solubility of Salts

Some salts dissolve readily in water while others have very low solubility.

Generally Soluble Important Exceptions
Nitrates Most nitrates are soluble.
Group 1 metal salts Generally soluble.
Ammonium salts Generally soluble.
Most chlorides AgCl and some other chlorides are poorly soluble.
Most sulfates BaSO₄ is insoluble; CaSO₄ and PbSO₄ have low solubility.

14. Acid-Base Titration

Titration is a laboratory method used to determine the concentration of an unknown solution by reacting it with a solution of known concentration.

Important Terms

  • Titrant: The solution added from the burette.
  • Analyte: The solution whose concentration is being determined.
  • Endpoint: The point indicated by the chosen indicator's colour change.
  • Equivalence point: The point at which the reacting quantities are present in the exact stoichiometric proportions required by the balanced equation.

Basic Titration Procedure

  1. Measure a known volume of the unknown solution.
  2. Place it in a suitable flask.
  3. Add a few drops of an appropriate indicator.
  4. Fill the burette with the standard solution.
  5. Record the initial burette reading.
  6. Add the titrant while swirling the flask.
  7. Slow down near the endpoint.
  8. Record the final burette reading.
  9. Calculate the titre.
  10. Use the balanced equation to calculate the unknown concentration.
Remember: The indicator should be selected so that its colour-change range is appropriate for the type of titration being performed.

15. Acid-Base Calculations

Many acid-base calculations use the relationship between concentration, volume and number of moles.

n = C × V

Volume must be in litres when concentration is expressed in mol/L.

For a 1:1 Reaction

For a reaction such as:

HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O

One mole of HCl reacts with one mole of NaOH. Therefore, at equivalence:

n(HCl) = n(NaOH)

Worked Example

25.0 mL of HCl is neutralised by 20.0 mL of 0.100 mol/L NaOH. Find the concentration of HCl.

Step 1:
Convert the NaOH volume:

20.0 mL = 0.0200 L

Step 2:
n(NaOH) = C × V

n = 0.100 × 0.0200

n = 0.00200 mol

Step 3:
The reaction ratio is 1:1, so:

n(HCl) = 0.00200 mol

Step 4:
HCl volume = 25.0 mL = 0.0250 L

C = n / V

C = 0.00200 / 0.0250

C(HCl) = 0.0800 mol/L

16. Worked Examples

Example 1 — Acid and Metal

Write the balanced equation for the reaction between magnesium and hydrochloric acid.

Magnesium forms Mg²⁺, while hydrochloric acid supplies H⁺.

Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂

Example 2 — Acid and Carbonate

What products form when hydrochloric acid reacts with calcium carbonate?

Acid + carbonate produces:

Salt + water + carbon dioxide

2HCl + CaCO₃ → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂

Example 3 — pH

Calculate the pH of a solution with hydrogen-ion concentration of 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol/L.

pH = -log₁₀[H⁺]

pH = -log₁₀(1.0 × 10⁻⁵)

pH = 5

Example 4 — Neutralisation

What are the products of sulfuric acid reacting completely with sodium hydroxide?

H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O

17. Word Problems

Problem 1

A student adds magnesium to hydrochloric acid. Bubbles are produced and a gas is released. Identify the gas and explain why it is formed.

The gas is hydrogen.

Magnesium displaces hydrogen ions from hydrochloric acid:

Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂

The hydrogen ions are reduced to hydrogen gas.

Problem 2

A student adds hydrochloric acid to marble chips. The chips gradually disappear and bubbles are produced. Explain what is happening.

Marble contains calcium carbonate. It reacts with hydrochloric acid to produce calcium chloride, water and carbon dioxide.

2HCl + CaCO₃ → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂

The bubbles are carbon dioxide gas.

Problem 3

A solution has a pH of 2 while another has a pH of 5. Which is more acidic?

The solution with pH 2 is more acidic.

The pH scale is logarithmic, so the difference represents a large difference in hydrogen-ion concentration.

Problem 4

A farmer applies lime to acidic soil. Why can this help the soil?

Lime contains basic substances that can react with acidic components in the soil. This reduces soil acidity and can bring the soil pH closer to a range suitable for particular crops.

18. Common Mistakes

1. Confusing Strong with Concentrated

Strong refers to the extent of ionisation, while concentration refers to the amount of solute present per unit volume.

2. Thinking Every Base Is an Alkali

An alkali is specifically a base that is soluble in water.

3. Forgetting to Balance Equations

Chemical equations must obey conservation of atoms and charge.

4. Forgetting Volume Conversion

When using molarity in mol/L, convert millilitres to litres.

1000 mL = 1 L

5. Confusing Endpoint and Equivalence Point

The endpoint is the observable indicator change used in a titration. The equivalence point is the stoichiometric point defined by the reaction.

6. Assuming pH Changes Linearly

The pH scale is logarithmic. A one-unit change in pH represents a tenfold change in hydrogen-ion concentration under the usual concentration-based interpretation.

Best approach: When solving an acid-base problem, first identify the substances, write and balance the chemical equation, convert units, determine the mole relationship, and only then perform the calculation.
