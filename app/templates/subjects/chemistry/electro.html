Electrochemistry

A detailed high-school guide to electrolysis, electrodes, ions, oxidation and reduction, electrolytic cells, electrochemical cells, electroplating, half-equations and quantitative electrolysis.

1. What Is Electrochemistry?

Electrochemistry studies the relationship between chemical reactions and electrical energy. Some chemical reactions produce electricity, while electricity can also be used to force chemical reactions.

Electrolysis

Electrical energy is used to drive a non-spontaneous chemical reaction.

Electrochemical Cells

A spontaneous redox reaction can be used to produce electrical energy.

Key idea: In an electrolyte, electrical charge is carried through the liquid or molten substance by moving ions.

2. Electrolytes and Non-Electrolytes

An electrolyte conducts electricity when molten or dissolved because it contains mobile ions.

Strong Electrolytes

Strong electrolytes produce a large concentration of ions in solution. Many soluble ionic compounds and strong acids are examples.

Weak Electrolytes

Weak electrolytes ionize only partially. Ethanoic acid is a familiar example.

Non-Electrolytes

Substances such as sugar dissolve as neutral molecules and therefore do not conduct electricity significantly.

Substance Main mobile particles Conducts?
Molten NaCl Na⁺ and Cl⁻ Yes
NaCl(aq) Na⁺ and Cl⁻ Yes
Solid NaCl Ions fixed in lattice No
Sugar solution Neutral molecules No significant conduction

3. Ions and Flow of Charge

Positive ions are called cations. Negative ions are called anions.

Cations

Positive ions that move toward the cathode during electrolysis.

Anions

Negative ions that move toward the anode during electrolysis.

Memory rule: Cations → Cathode. Anions → Anode.

4. Electrolysis

Electrolysis is the decomposition of an electrolyte by passing an electric current through it.

Electrical energy → chemical change

What Happens?

The electrolyte contains mobile ions.
A direct-current power supply creates a potential difference.
Positive ions migrate toward the cathode.
Negative ions migrate toward the anode.
Electron-transfer reactions occur at the electrode surfaces.
New substances are formed at the electrodes.

5. Electrodes

Electrodes are conducting materials through which current enters or leaves an electrolyte.

Electrode Electrolytic cell Reaction Attracted ions
Cathode Negative Reduction Cations
Anode Positive Oxidation Anions
Important: Oxidation always occurs at the anode and reduction always occurs at the cathode. The electrode signs depend on the cell type.

6. Electrolytic Cells

An electrolytic cell uses an external electrical source to force a chemical reaction that is not spontaneous under the conditions.

Power Supply

Provides electrical energy to drive the reaction.

Electrolyte

Contains mobile ions that carry charge through the system.

Cathode

Negative electrode where reduction occurs.

Anode

Positive electrode where oxidation occurs.

7. Half-Equations

A half-equation represents the electron transfer at one electrode. It must be balanced for atoms and charge.

Reduction at the Cathode

Copper deposition
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu

Cu²⁺ gains two electrons. Gain of electrons is reduction.

Oxidation at the Anode

Chloride oxidation
2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻

Cl⁻ loses electrons. Loss of electrons is oxidation.

OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain of electrons.

8. Electrolysis of Aqueous Solutions

Aqueous electrolysis is more complicated than molten electrolysis because water also participates in the chemistry and provides hydrogen- and hydroxide-containing species.

Example: Copper(II) Sulfate Solution

CuSO₄(aq) contains Cu²⁺ and SO₄²⁻, while water contributes to the electrode chemistry. With inert electrodes, Cu²⁺ is commonly reduced at the cathode.

Cathode
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu

At the inert anode, hydroxide-containing water chemistry commonly leads to oxygen formation.

Anode
4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻

9. Preferential Discharge

When several ions are present, they compete for discharge. Which ion is discharged depends on factors including electrode material, concentration and the relative ease of the electrode reaction.

At the Cathode

  • Less reactive metal ions are often reduced preferentially.
  • Very reactive metal ions such as Na⁺ and K⁺ are generally not deposited from aqueous solutions.
  • Water can instead be reduced to form hydrogen.

At the Anode

  • Halide ions such as Cl⁻, Br⁻ and I⁻ can be oxidized under suitable conditions.
  • Otherwise hydroxide/water chemistry commonly produces oxygen with inert electrodes.
Exam tip: List all ions present before deciding what forms at each electrode.

10. Worked Examples

Example 1 — Molten Sodium Chloride

What products form when molten NaCl is electrolysed?

Step 1: The ions are Na⁺ and Cl⁻.

Step 2: Na⁺ moves to the cathode.

Step 3: Cl⁻ moves to the anode.

Cathode
Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na
Anode
2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻

Products: sodium metal and chlorine gas.

Example 2 — Copper(II) Sulfate

Copper(II) sulfate solution is electrolysed using inert electrodes. What happens at the cathode?

Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu

Copper ions gain electrons and copper is deposited at the cathode.

Example 3 — Concentrated Brine

Concentrated sodium chloride solution contains Na⁺, Cl⁻ and water-derived species. Under suitable industrial conditions, chlorine forms at the anode and hydrogen forms at the cathode.

Cathode
2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻
Anode
2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻

The solution becomes enriched in sodium hydroxide.

11. Electroplating

Electroplating uses electrolysis to coat an object with a thin layer of another metal.

Uses

  • Improving appearance.
  • Protecting against corrosion.
  • Improving surface properties.
  • Reducing the amount of expensive metal required.

The object being coated is connected to the negative terminal and becomes the cathode. Metal ions in solution gain electrons and are deposited on it.

Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu

12. Electrochemical Cells

A galvanic or voltaic cell converts chemical energy from a spontaneous redox reaction into electrical energy.

Feature Electrolytic Cell Galvanic/Voltaic Cell
Energy conversion Electrical → chemical Chemical → electrical
Reaction Non-spontaneous Spontaneous
Anode Positive Negative
Cathode Negative Positive
Oxidation Anode Anode
Reduction Cathode Cathode
Remember: Oxidation is always at the anode and reduction is always at the cathode. Only the electrode signs change between cell types.

13. Redox Reactions

Electrochemistry is based on oxidation-reduction reactions.

Oxidation

Loss of electrons and an increase in oxidation state.

Reduction

Gain of electrons and a decrease in oxidation state.

14. Quantitative Electrolysis

The amount of material produced depends on the amount of electric charge passed through the cell.

Q = It

Q is charge in coulombs, I is current in amperes and t is time in seconds.

Faraday Constant

One mole of electrons carries approximately 96,500 coulombs of charge.

Q = nF
It = nF

Word Problem

A current of 2.0 A flows through an electrolytic cell for 30 minutes. Calculate the charge passed.

Convert time:

30 × 60 = 1800 s

Apply Q = It:

Q = 2.0 × 1800 = 3600 C

Therefore, the charge passed is 3600 C.

15. Applications of Electrochemistry

Electroplating

Coating objects with metals for protection or appearance.

Metal Extraction

Electrolysis is used to obtain highly reactive metals such as aluminium.

Metal Refining

Electrorefining produces very pure metals such as copper.

Chemical Manufacture

Electrolysis is used industrially to produce chlorine, hydrogen and sodium hydroxide.

16. Exam Checklist

  • Identify every ion present before predicting products.
  • Cations move toward the cathode.
  • Anions move toward the anode.
  • Oxidation occurs at the anode.
  • Reduction occurs at the cathode.
  • Balance half-equations for atoms and charge.
  • Distinguish molten from aqueous electrolysis.
  • Remember that water contributes to aqueous electrolysis.
  • Use Q = It and convert time to seconds.
  • Check electron ratios when doing quantitative questions.
