Chemical Formulae

1. What Is a Chemical Formula?

A chemical formula is a short way of representing a chemical substance. It tells us which elements are present and, in many cases, the ratio in which their atoms or ions are combined.

Instead of writing the full name of a substance every time, chemists use symbols and numbers to represent it.

Example: Water is represented by H₂O. This means that each water molecule contains two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom.

Why Are Chemical Formulae Important?

  • They identify chemical substances.
  • They show which elements are present.
  • They can show the ratio of atoms in a compound.
  • They are used in chemical equations.
  • They allow chemists to calculate relative formula masses.
  • They are used in mole calculations and quantitative chemistry.

2. Chemical Symbols

Before writing chemical formulae, you need to understand chemical symbols. Each element has its own symbol.

The first letter of an element symbol is always capitalized. If a second letter is present, it is lowercase.

Hydrogen

Symbol: H

Oxygen

Symbol: O

Sodium

Symbol: Na

Chlorine

Symbol: Cl

Calcium

Symbol: Ca

Iron

Symbol: Fe

Important: CO and Co do not mean the same thing. CO represents carbon monoxide, while Co represents cobalt.

3. How to Read a Chemical Formula

The small number written after an element symbol is called a subscript. It tells us how many atoms of that element are present.

H₂O

In H₂O, the subscript 2 belongs to hydrogen. Therefore there are two hydrogen atoms. Oxygen has no subscript, so there is one oxygen atom.

Example: CO₂

CO₂ contains one carbon atom and two oxygen atoms.

Example: CaCl₂

CaCl₂ contains one calcium atom and two chlorine atoms.

Parentheses in Formulae

Parentheses are used when a group of atoms occurs more than once.

Ca(OH)₂

The subscript 2 applies to the entire OH group. Therefore the compound contains one calcium atom, two oxygen atoms and two hydrogen atoms.

4. Valency and Writing Formulae

Valency describes the combining capacity of an atom or ion. Understanding valency helps us determine the correct formula of many ionic compounds.

Common Valencies

Element/Ion Symbol Common Valency
Sodium Na 1
Potassium K 1
Magnesium Mg 2
Calcium Ca 2
Aluminium Al 3
Oxygen O 2
Chlorine Cl 1

Example: Magnesium Chloride

Magnesium has valency 2 and chlorine has valency 1. Therefore two chlorine ions are required for every magnesium ion.

MgCl₂

Example: Aluminium Oxide

Aluminium has valency 3 while oxygen has valency 2. The smallest ratio that balances the charges is 2 aluminium ions to 3 oxide ions.

Al₂O₃

5. Ions and Chemical Formulae

An ion is an atom or group of atoms that has gained or lost electrons and therefore carries an electrical charge.

Positive Ions

Positive ions are called cations. They are formed when atoms lose electrons.

Sodium ion

Na⁺

Magnesium ion

Mg²⁺

Calcium ion

Ca²⁺

Aluminium ion

Al³⁺

Negative Ions

Negative ions are called anions. They are formed when atoms gain electrons.

Chloride

Cl⁻

Oxide

O²⁻

Nitride

N³⁻

Sulfide

S²⁻

Key idea: An ionic compound must have an overall electrical charge of zero.

6. How to Write Chemical Formulae

For ionic compounds, the total positive charge must balance the total negative charge.

Step 1: Write the Ions

For calcium chloride:

Ca²⁺    Cl⁻

Step 2: Balance the Charges

One Ca²⁺ ion needs two Cl⁻ ions.

Step 3: Write the Formula

CaCl₂

Worked Example: Aluminium Oxide

Aluminium ion = Al³⁺

Oxide ion = O²⁻

The lowest common total charge is 6.

2 × Al³⁺ = +6

3 × O²⁻ = −6

Formula = Al₂O₃

8. Relative Formula Mass (Mr)

Relative formula mass is the sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms represented in a chemical formula.

Mᵣ = sum of the relative atomic masses of all atoms

Example: Water

Relative atomic mass of hydrogen = 1.

Relative atomic mass of oxygen = 16.

Water contains two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom.

Mᵣ(H₂O) = (2 × 1) + 16
Mᵣ(H₂O) = 18

Example: CaCO₃

Mᵣ(CaCO₃) = 40 + 12 + (3 × 16)
Mᵣ(CaCO₃) = 100

9. Chemical Formulae and Moles

Chemical formulae are essential when calculating the number of moles of a substance.

n = m / M
Symbol Meaning
n Amount of substance in moles
m Mass in grams
M Molar mass in g mol⁻¹

Worked Example

Calculate the number of moles in 36 g of water.

Mᵣ of H₂O = 18.

n = 36 / 18
Number of moles = 2 mol

10. Percentage Composition

Percentage composition tells us what percentage of a compound's mass comes from a particular element.

Percentage by mass = (mass contributed by element / relative formula mass) × 100

Example: Percentage of Oxygen in Water

H₂O has a relative formula mass of 18. Oxygen contributes 16.

Percentage oxygen = (16 / 18) × 100
Percentage oxygen ≈ 88.9%

11. Empirical Formula

The empirical formula shows the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound.

Example: The molecular formula C₆H₁₂O₆ has the empirical formula CH₂O.

How to Find an Empirical Formula

  1. Write the mass or percentage of each element.
  2. Divide each value by its relative atomic mass.
  3. Find the simplest whole-number ratio.
  4. Write the empirical formula.

Worked Example

A compound contains 24 g of carbon and 4 g of hydrogen. Find its empirical formula.

Carbon:

24 / 12 = 2

Hydrogen:

4 / 1 = 4

Ratio = 2 : 4

Divide both by 2.

Simplest ratio = 1 : 2

Empirical formula = CH₂

12. Molecular Formula

The molecular formula shows the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule.

The molecular formula is always a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula.

Molecular formula = empirical formula × n

Where:

n = molecular mass / empirical formula mass

Worked Example

Suppose a compound has empirical formula CH₂ and a molecular mass of 42.

Empirical formula mass:

12 + (2 × 1) = 14

Therefore:

n = 42 / 14 = 3
Molecular formula = C₃H₆

13. Chemical Formulae in Equations

Chemical formulae are used to represent reactants and products in chemical equations.

For example, hydrogen reacts with oxygen to form water.

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Why Are Numbers Placed Before Formulae?

The large numbers in front of formulae are called coefficients. They are used to balance chemical equations.

Important: Never change the subscripts inside a chemical formula simply to balance an equation. Changing a subscript changes the substance. Balance equations by changing the coefficients.

Example

Unbalanced:

H₂ + O₂ → H₂O

Balanced:

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

14. Chemical Formulae Word Problems

Chemical formula questions are often presented as real chemical situations. The important skill is to identify what information the formula gives you and what quantity the question asks for.

Problem 1

A student has 90 g of water. Calculate the number of moles present.

Mᵣ of H₂O = 18.

n = m / M
n = 90 / 18
Answer = 5 mol

Problem 2

Calcium carbonate has the formula CaCO₃. Calculate its relative formula mass.

Mᵣ = 40 + 12 + (3 × 16)
Answer = 100

Problem 3

A compound contains carbon and hydrogen in the ratio 1 : 2. What is its empirical formula?

Empirical formula = CH₂

Problem 4

Aluminium reacts with oxygen to form aluminium oxide. Aluminium forms Al³⁺ ions and oxygen forms O²⁻ ions. Determine the formula of the compound.

The charges must balance:

2(Al³⁺) + 3(O²⁻)
Formula = Al₂O₃
