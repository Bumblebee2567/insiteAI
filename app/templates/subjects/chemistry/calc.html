The UPS-CHECK Strategy:

Stoichiometry, Moles, and Limiting Reactants

Stoichiometry forms the quantitative bedrock of chemistry, linking mass, moles, particles, and gas volumes.

Problem 1 (Combustion Analysis):

A 2.50 g sample of a hydrocarbon undergoes complete combustion to produce 7.92 g of carbon dioxide (CO2) and 1.62 g of water (H2O). Determine the empirical formula of the hydrocarbon.

Step-by-step Solution:
1. Calculate moles of carbon from CO2: Moles CO2 = 7.92 g / 44.01 g/mol = 0.180 mol. Therefore, moles of C = 0.180 mol.
2. Calculate moles of hydrogen from H2O: Moles H2O = 1.62 g / 18.02 g/mol = 0.0899 mol. Since each water molecule has 2 H atoms, moles of H = 2 × 0.0899 = 0.180 mol.
3. Find the mole ratio C : H = 0.180 : 0.180 = 1 : 1.
4. Empirical formula = CH (Benzene or acetylene family empirical unit).
Problem 2 (Limiting Reactant & Theoretical Yield):

If 15.0 g of aluminum reacts with 35.0 g of chlorine gas (Cl2) to form aluminum chloride (AlCl3), which reactant is limiting, and what mass of AlCl3 is produced?

Step-by-step Solution:
1. Balanced Equation: 2Al(s) + 3Cl2(g) → 2AlCl3(s)
2. Calculate moles available: Moles Al = 15.0 g / 26.98 g/mol = 0.556 mol. Moles Cl2 = 35.0 g / 70.90 g/mol = 0.494 mol.
3. Determine required ratio: For 0.494 mol Cl2, required Al = 0.494 × (2/3) = 0.329 mol. Since we have 0.556 mol Al available (which is more than 0.329 mol), Chlorine (Cl2) is the limiting reactant.
4. Calculate mass of AlCl3 produced: Moles AlCl3 = Moles Cl2 × (2/3) = 0.494 × (2/3) = 0.329 mol. Mass = 0.329 mol × 133.34 g/mol = 43.9 g of AlCl3.

Solution Concentration, Molarity, and Dilutions

Concentration calculations govern titrations, precipitation reactions, and solution preparations.

Problem 3 (Titration Stoichiometry):

25.0 cm3 of a sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution requires 21.45 cm3 of 0.125 mol/dm3 hydrochloric acid (HCl) for complete neutralization. What is the concentration of the NaOH solution in g/dm3?

Step-by-step Solution:
1. Balanced Equation: NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H2O(l) (1:1 mole ratio).
2. Calculate moles of HCl used: Moles = 0.125 mol/dm3 × (21.45 / 1000) dm3 = 2.681 × 10-3 mol.
3. Moles of NaOH neutralized = 2.681 × 10-3 mol in 25.0 cm3.
4. Concentration in mol/dm3 = (2.681 × 10-3 mol / 0.0250 dm3) = 0.10725 mol/dm3.
5. Convert to g/dm3 (Molar mass NaOH = 39.997 g/mol): 0.10725 × 39.997 = 4.29 g/dm3.
Problem 4 (Serial Dilution):

Calculate the final concentration of a solution prepared by taking 15.0 cm3 of 2.00 mol/dm3 stock nitric acid (HNO3) and diluting it in a volumetric flask to a total volume of 250.0 cm3.

Step-by-step Solution:
1. Use the dilution formula: C1V1 = C2V2
2. C2 = (C1 × V1) / V2 = (2.00 mol/dm3 × 15.0 cm3) / 250.0 cm3
3. C2 = 30.0 / 250.0 = 0.120 mol/dm3.

Thermochemistry and Enthalpy Changes

Evaluating heat flow, calorimeter constants, and Hess's Law cycles.

Problem 5 (Calorimetry & Enthalpy of Neutralization):

When 50.0 cm3 of 1.00 mol/dm3 HCl is mixed with 50.0 cm3 of 1.00 mol/dm3 NaOH in a polystyrene cup calorimeter, the temperature rises from 21.5 degrees Celsius to 28.3 degrees Celsius. Calculate the enthalpy change of neutralization per mole of water formed. (Assume density of solution = 1.00 g/cm3 and specific heat capacity c = 4.18 J/g·°C).

Step-by-step Solution:
1. Total mass of solution (m) = 50.0 + 50.0 = 100.0 g.
2. Temperature change (delta T) = 28.3 - 21.5 = 6.8 degrees Celsius.
3. Heat released (q) = m × c × delta T = 100.0 g × 4.18 J/g·°C × 6.8 degrees Celsius = 2842.4 J = 2.842 kJ.
4. Moles of water formed = 0.050 dm3 × 1.00 mol/dm3 = 0.0500 mol.
5. Enthalpy of neutralization (delta H) = -q / moles = -2.842 kJ / 0.0500 mol = -56.8 kJ/mol.

Reaction Kinetics and Rate Laws

Determining rate orders, rate constants (k), and half-lives from experimental initial rates data.

Problem 6 (Method of Initial Rates):

For the reaction A + B → Products, the following initial rate data was collected:
Experiment 1: [A] = 0.10 M, [B] = 0.10 M, Rate = 2.0 × 10-4 M/s
Experiment 2: [A] = 0.20 M, [B] = 0.10 M, Rate = 8.0 × 10-4 M/s
Experiment 3: [A] = 0.10 M, [B] = 0.30 M, Rate = 6.0 × 10-4 M/s
Determine the rate law and calculate the rate constant k.

Step-by-step Solution:
1. General Rate Law: Rate = k[A]m[B]n
2. Find order with respect to A (m): Comparing Exp 1 and 2, [A] doubles (0.10 to 0.20) while [B] is constant. Rate quadruples (2.0 × 10-4 to 8.0 × 10-4). Therefore, 2m = 4, so m = 2 (second order in A).
3. Find order with respect to B (n): Comparing Exp 1 and 3, [B] triples (0.10 to 0.30) while [A] is constant. Rate triples (2.0 × 10-4 to 6.0 × 10-4). Therefore, 3n = 3, so n = 1 (first order in B).
4. Rate Law: Rate = k[A]2[B].
5. Calculate rate constant k using Exp 1: 2.0 × 10-4 = k (0.10)2 (0.10) → k = 2.0 × 10-4 / 0.001 = 0.20 M-2s-1.

Chemical Equilibrium (Kc and Kp)

ICE tables (Initial, Change, Equilibrium) and equilibrium constant expressions.

Problem 7 (ICE Table Calculation):

1.00 mol of hydrogen and 1.00 mol of iodine are placed in a 2.00 dm3 sealed flask at 450 degrees Celsius and allowed to reach equilibrium: H2(g) + I2(g) ⇌ 2HI(g). At equilibrium, 1.56 mol of HI is present. Calculate the equilibrium constant Kc.

Step-by-step Solution:
1. Initial concentrations: [H2] = 1.00 / 2.00 = 0.50 M; [I2] = 1.00 / 2.00 = 0.50 M; [HI] = 0.
2. Equilibrium moles of HI = 1.56 mol → Equilibrium [HI] = 1.56 / 2.00 = 0.78 M.
3. Change in [HI] = +0.78 M. From stoichiometry, change in [H2] and [I2] = -0.78 / 2 = -0.39 M.
4. Equilibrium concentrations: [H2] = 0.50 - 0.39 = 0.11 M; [I2] = 0.50 - 0.39 = 0.11 M.
5. Calculate Kc: Kc = [HI]2 / ([H2][I2]) = (0.78)2 / (0.11 × 0.11) = 0.6084 / 0.0121 = 50.3.

Acids, Bases, pH, and Buffer Solutions

Calculations involving strong/weak acids, Ka, pKa, pH, and Henderson-Hasselbalch buffers.

Problem 8 (Weak Acid pH Calculation):

Calculate the pH of a 0.150 mol/dm3 solution of ethanoic acid (CH3COOH). Given that Ka = 1.74 × 10-5 mol/dm3.

Step-by-step Solution:
1. Equilibrium Expression: Ka = [H+][CH3COO-] / [CH3COOH] ≈ [H+]2 / C
2. Rearrange for [H+]: [H+] = square root of (Ka × C) = square root of (1.74 × 10-5 × 0.150) = square root of (2.61 × 10-6) = 1.615 × 10-3 M.
3. Calculate pH: pH = -log10[H+] = -log10(1.615 × 10-3) = 2.79.

Electrochemistry and Nernst Equation

Standard cell potentials (E degrees cell), Gibbs free energy, and Faraday's laws of electrolysis.

Problem 9 (Faraday's Law Calculation):

A constant current of 2.00 A is passed through an aqueous solution of copper(II) sulfate for 45.0 minutes. Calculate the mass of solid copper deposited at the cathode. (F = 96,485 C/mol, Molar mass Cu = 63.55 g/mol).

Step-by-step Solution:
1. Calculate total charge (Q): Q = I × t = 2.00 A × (45.0 × 60 s) = 5400 C.
2. Calculate moles of electrons transferred: Moles e- = Q / F = 5400 / 96,485 = 0.05597 mol.
3. Half-equation at cathode: Cu2+ + 2e- → Cu (2 moles of e- per 1 mole of Cu).
4. Moles of Cu deposited = 0.05597 / 2 = 0.02798 mol.
5. Mass of Cu = 0.02798 mol × 63.55 g/mol = 1.78 g.

Organic Synthesis & Reaction Mechanisms

Multi-step synthetic pathways, functional group transformations, and retrosynthetic analysis.

Problem 10 (Synthetic Pathway):

Outline a 3-step synthetic pathway to convert propene into 1-bromopropane.

Step-by-step Solution:
1. Step 1: Hydrohalogenation in the presence of peroxides (anti-Markovnikov addition). React propene with HBr in the presence of organic peroxides (ROOR) to yield 1-bromopropane.
*Note: If standard Markovnikov addition is used without peroxides, 2-bromopropane is formed instead.*
