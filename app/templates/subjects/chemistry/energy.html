1. What Is an Energy Change?

Chemical reactions involve atoms being rearranged. During this rearrangement, chemical bonds are broken and new bonds are formed. Because energy is involved in both processes, the total energy of the system can change.

The key idea: Breaking chemical bonds requires energy, while forming chemical bonds releases energy.

Energy Absorbed

Energy is transferred into the reacting system. This is associated with an endothermic process.

Energy Released

Energy is transferred from the reacting system to its surroundings. This is associated with an exothermic process.

2. Exothermic vs Endothermic

Exothermic Reactions

An exothermic reaction releases energy from the system into the surroundings. The surroundings therefore tend to become warmer.

  • Combustion of fuels
  • Respiration
  • Many neutralisation reactions
  • Many oxidation reactions
ΔH < 0 Negative enthalpy change

Endothermic Reactions

An endothermic reaction absorbs energy from the surroundings into the system. The surroundings may become cooler.

  • Thermal decomposition
  • Photosynthesis
  • Some dissolving processes
  • Some reactions involving salts and water
ΔH > 0 Positive enthalpy change
Feature Exothermic Endothermic
Energy transfer System → surroundings Surroundings → system
Surroundings Usually become warmer Usually become cooler
ΔH Negative Positive
Product enthalpy Lower than reactants Higher than reactants

What Do the Symbols Mean?

Eₐ — Activation Energy

The minimum energy particles need for a successful reaction to occur.

ΔH — Enthalpy Change

The difference in enthalpy between the products and the reactants.

4. Enthalpy Change

Enthalpy is a quantity used to describe the energy content of a chemical system. For a reaction carried out at constant pressure, the enthalpy change tells us how the system's enthalpy changes.

ΔH = Hproducts − Hreactants Usually measured in kJ mol⁻¹

ΔH is negative

Products have less enthalpy than reactants. The reaction is exothermic.

ΔH is positive

Products have more enthalpy than reactants. The reaction is endothermic.

5. Bond Breaking and Bond Formation

Breaking Bonds

Energy must be supplied to overcome the attraction holding bonded atoms together. Bond breaking is therefore endothermic.

Forming Bonds

When new bonds form, energy is released. Bond formation is therefore exothermic.

ΔH ≈ Energy used to break bonds − Energy released forming bonds
Worked Example — Bond Energies

Suppose a reaction requires 620 kJ mol⁻¹ to break its reactant bonds and releases 810 kJ mol⁻¹ when its product bonds form.

1
Write the energy change relationship.
ΔH ≈ 620 − 810
2
Calculate the difference.
ΔH ≈ −190 kJ mol⁻¹
The reaction is exothermic because ΔH is negative.

6. Calorimetry

Calorimetry is the measurement of heat transfer. In school chemistry, temperature changes are often used to determine the energy transferred.

Water / solution Thermometer
q = mcΔT q = heat energy, m = mass, c = specific heat capacity, ΔT = temperature change
ΔT = Final temperature − Initial temperature
Worked Example — Calorimetry

100 g of water is heated from 20 °C to 30 °C. Its specific heat capacity is 4.18 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹. Calculate the energy absorbed.

1
Calculate the temperature change: 30 − 20 = 10 °C.
2
Substitute into q = mcΔT.
q = 100 × 4.18 × 10
q = 4180 J
Answer: 4180 J of energy is absorbed by the water.

7. Activation Energy and Catalysts

Particles can collide without reacting. For a reaction to happen, particles must collide with enough energy and with a suitable orientation.

Ea = Energy of transition state − Energy of reactants

What Does a Catalyst Do?

A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy. This makes successful collisions more likely and increases the reaction rate.

Remember: A catalyst lowers Eₐ, but it does not change the overall ΔH of the reaction.

8. Hess's Law

Hess's Law states that the overall enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the route taken between the same starting and ending states.

Overall ΔH = Sum of the ΔH values of the individual steps
Reactants Intermediate Products ΔH₁ ↓ ΔH₂ ↓

Three Rules You Must Remember

  • Reverse an equation → reverse the sign of ΔH.
  • Multiply an equation → multiply ΔH by the same number.
  • Add equations → add their enthalpy changes.
Worked Example

A → B has ΔH = +50 kJ mol⁻¹. B → C has ΔH = −120 kJ mol⁻¹. Find the enthalpy change for A → C.

ΔH = +50 + (−120)
ΔH = −70 kJ mol⁻¹
The overall reaction is exothermic.

9. Important Standard Enthalpy Changes

Standard Enthalpy of Formation

The enthalpy change when one mole of a compound forms from its elements in their standard states.

Standard Enthalpy of Combustion

The enthalpy change when one mole of a substance undergoes complete combustion in oxygen under specified standard conditions.

Standard Enthalpy of Neutralisation

The enthalpy change when an acid and a base react to form one mole of water under standard conditions.

Enthalpy of Solution

The enthalpy change associated with dissolving a substance in a solvent under specified conditions.

10. Practice Questions

Question 1 — Classification

A reaction causes the temperature of the surrounding solution to increase. Is the reaction likely to be exothermic or endothermic?

Answer: Exothermic. Energy is being transferred from the reaction to the surroundings.
Question 2 — Calorimetry

A 200 g sample of water increases from 22 °C to 29 °C. Take c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹. Calculate the energy absorbed.

ΔT = 29 − 22 = 7 °C.

q = 200 × 4.18 × 7

q = 5852 J.
Question 3 — Catalyst

A student says, "A catalyst makes an exothermic reaction more exothermic." Is the statement correct?

No. A catalyst lowers activation energy. It does not change the overall ΔH of the reaction.
Question 4 — Bond Energies

A reaction uses 500 kJ mol⁻¹ to break its bonds and releases 640 kJ mol⁻¹ when new bonds form. Calculate the approximate enthalpy change.

ΔH ≈ 500 − 640 = −140 kJ mol⁻¹
The reaction is exothermic.
