Tests for Anions

Identify common anions using characteristic reactions, including acidification, gas evolution, precipitation, and the formation of silver and barium compounds.

Aim Principle Apparatus Procedure Anion Tests Equations Identify Precautions Questions
01

Aim of the Experiment

To identify common anions in unknown compounds by observing characteristic reactions with acids and selected reagents.

Key idea:
Different anions react in characteristic ways. Some produce gases when acidified, while others form insoluble precipitates with silver or barium ions.
02

Principle of Anion Testing

Qualitative analysis uses chemical reactions to identify ions by their observable properties. The presence of an anion may be indicated by gas evolution, a colour change, or the formation of an insoluble precipitate.

In these tests, dilute acids are commonly used to investigate carbonate and sulfite ions, while silver nitrate and barium salts are used to test for selected halides and sulfate ions.

Important:
A positive result should be interpreted together with the appropriate confirmatory test. A single observation may not always uniquely identify an unknown ion.

Main Types of Tests

  • Acid + anion: may produce a characteristic gas.
  • Silver nitrate: can produce precipitates with halide ions.
  • Barium solution: can produce a white precipitate with sulfate ions.
  • Aluminium reduction: can be used in a confirmatory test for nitrate ions.
03

Apparatus & Materials

  • Test tubes and test-tube rack
  • Dropping pipettes
  • Dilute hydrochloric acid
  • Silver nitrate solution
  • Barium nitrate or barium chloride solution
  • Dilute nitric acid
  • Sodium hydroxide solution
  • Aluminium foil or aluminium powder for nitrate testing
  • Limewater for carbon dioxide testing
  • Unknown salt samples
  • Safety goggles and appropriate laboratory equipment
Laboratory safety:
Handle acids and silver salts carefully. Avoid direct contact with reagents and wear appropriate eye protection throughout the experiment.
04

General Experimental Procedure

  1. Place a small amount of the unknown salt solution into a clean test tube.
  2. Add the appropriate reagent for the anion being tested.
  3. Observe carefully for gas evolution, colour changes, or formation of a precipitate.
  4. If a gas is produced, carry out the appropriate gas test to help identify it.
  5. Where required, acidify the solution before adding the confirmatory reagent.
  6. Record the observation and compare it with the expected result for each anion.
  7. Repeat the test using clean apparatus when testing another unknown sample.
Good practical technique:
Use clean test tubes and separate droppers where possible. Contamination from another ion can produce a misleading observation.
05

Tests for Common Anions

The table below summarizes the main observations used to identify the selected anions.

Anion Reagent / Test Observation Inference
Chloride (Cl−) Acidify with dilute nitric acid, then add silver nitrate solution. White precipitate. Chloride ions are present.
Bromide (Br−) Acidify with dilute nitric acid, then add silver nitrate solution. Cream precipitate. Bromide ions are present.
Iodide (I−) Acidify with dilute nitric acid, then add silver nitrate solution. Yellow precipitate. Iodide ions are present.
Sulfate (SO42−) Acidify the solution, then add a soluble barium salt solution. White precipitate. Sulfate ions are present.
Sulfite (SO32−) Add dilute hydrochloric acid. Colourless gas with a choking smell is evolved. Sulfur dioxide (SO2) is produced.
Carbonate (CO32−) Add dilute hydrochloric acid and pass the gas into limewater. Effervescence; limewater turns milky. Carbon dioxide is produced.
Nitrate (NO3−) Add sodium hydroxide and aluminium, then warm carefully. Ammonia gas is produced. Nitrate ions are present.
Halide comparison:
Silver nitrate gives different precipitate colours with chloride, bromide and iodide ions: white, cream and yellow respectively.

08

Precautions & Sources of Error

Precautions

  • Wear appropriate eye protection when handling acids and chemical reagents.
  • Use clean test tubes and droppers.
  • Add reagents carefully and use small quantities.
  • Acidify solutions only with the specified acid and amount required for the test.
  • Do not directly smell gases produced during reactions.
  • Perform gas tests carefully and in a well-ventilated laboratory.

Possible Sources of Error

  • Contamination of the sample or apparatus.
  • Adding an incorrect reagent.
  • Using excessive quantities of reagents.
  • Misinterpreting similar precipitate colours.
  • Failure to acidify a sample when the procedure requires it.
Safety reminder:
Never deliberately inhale gases produced during qualitative analysis. Gas observations should be made safely using the appropriate laboratory procedure.
09

Common Practical Questions

1. What reagent is used to test for halide ions?
Acidified silver nitrate solution.
2. What colour precipitate is produced by chloride ions?
White.
3. What colour precipitate is produced by bromide ions?
Cream.
4. What colour precipitate is produced by iodide ions?
Yellow.
5. How can sulfate ions be identified?
Acidify the solution and add a soluble barium salt. A white precipitate of barium sulfate indicates sulfate ions.
6. What gas is produced when a sulfite reacts with an acid?
Sulfur dioxide, SO2.
7. What gas is produced when a carbonate reacts with an acid?
Carbon dioxide, CO2.
8. What observation confirms carbon dioxide?
The gas turns limewater milky.
9. What gas is produced in the nitrate test?
Ammonia, which can be identified by its characteristic alkaline properties.
