Electrochemical Cells & Electrolysis

Trace charge transfers, identify electrode polarities, and analyze chemical products generated during aqueous electrolysis.

01

Aim of the Experiment

To investigate the electrolysis of aqueous ionic solutions, identify the specific chemical products formed at the anode and cathode, and trace the flow of electric charge through electrochemical setups.

Key idea:
Electrolysis involves forcing a non-spontaneous redox reaction to occur by passing an electric current through an electrolyte solution or molten salt.
02

Principle of Electrolysis

During electrolysis, charge transfer occurs across two electrodes connected to an external DC power source:

  • Anode (Positive Electrode): Connected to the positive terminal of the power supply. Oxidation occurs here as anions lose electrons.
  • Cathode (Negative Electrode): Connected to the negative terminal of the power supply. Reduction occurs here as cations gain electrons.
  • Aqueous Solutions: Water molecules can also undergo oxidation or reduction alongside dissolved ions, depending on standard electrode potentials (reduction potentials).
03

Electrode Processes & Selectivity

1. Anode Reactions (Oxidation)

Negatively charged ions (anions) migrate to the anode. If halide ions (like Cl- or Br-) are present in high concentrations, they are oxidized to halogen gas. In dilute solutions or with inert electrodes where no reactive halides are present, water is oxidized to oxygen gas (O2) and hydrogen ions: 2H2O(l) → O2(g) + 4H+(aq) + 4e-.

2. Cathode Reactions (Reduction)

Positively charged ions (cations) migrate to the cathode. Metal ions less reactive than hydrogen (such as Cu2+ or Ag+) are reduced to solid metal. If more reactive metal ions (like Na+ or K+) are in aqueous solution, water is reduced instead, producing hydrogen gas (H2) and hydroxide ions: 2H2O(l) + 2e- → H2(g) + 2OH-(aq).

04

Experimental Procedure

  1. Set up an electrolytic cell with inert electrodes (such as carbon or platinum) immersed in the chosen aqueous electrolyte (e.g., copper(II) sulfate or sodium chloride).
  2. Connect the electrodes to a DC power supply or battery pack and switch on the current.
  3. Observe and record any gas bubbles evolved at the electrodes, color changes in the solution, or solid deposits forming on the electrodes.
  4. Test any evolved gases using standard tests (e.g., glowing splint for oxygen, burning splint for hydrogen, damp litmus paper for chlorine).

07

Precautions & Safety

  • Ensure the DC power supply voltage is kept at safe levels to avoid electrical hazards.
  • Perform electrolysis of concentrated brines in a well-ventilated fume hood due to toxic chlorine gas evolution.
  • Avoid direct skin contact with heavy metal salt solutions like copper sulfate.
08

Common Practical Questions

1. Why is copper deposited at the cathode during copper sulfate electrolysis instead of hydrogen?
Copper ions (Cu2+) have a higher positive reduction potential than hydrogen ions, meaning they are reduced more readily at the cathode.
2. Which electrode is connected to the positive terminal of the DC power source, and what reaction occurs there?
The anode is connected to the positive terminal, and oxidation occurs there as anions lose electrons.
