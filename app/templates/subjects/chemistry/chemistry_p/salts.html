Preparation of Salts

Review standard laboratory techniques for isolating soluble salts through excess solid crystallization and insoluble salts through ionic precipitation.

Solubility Rules in Water

Before choosing a preparation method, you must determine whether the desired salt is soluble or insoluble in water.

Salt Type Soluble in Water? Exceptions
Sodium, Potassium, Ammonium salts Always Soluble None
Nitrates (NO3-) Always Soluble None
Chlorides (Cl-) Soluble Lead(II) chloride, Silver chloride
Sulfates (SO42-) Soluble Lead(II) sulfate, Barium sulfate, Calcium sulfate (spare)
Carbonates (CO32-) Insoluble Sodium, potassium, and ammonium carbonates
02

Preparing Soluble Salts (Excess Solid Method)

Soluble salts (such as copper(II) sulfate or zinc chloride) are typically prepared by reacting an acid with an insoluble reactant (metal, metal oxide, or metal carbonate).

1. Warm & React

Add the insoluble solid to warm acid until no more dissolves, ensuring all acid is fully neutralised (excess solid sits at the bottom).

2. Filter

Filter the mixture to remove the unreacted excess solid. The filtrate is the pure aqueous salt solution.

3. Crystallise

Heat the solution to evaporate water until crystals form, then leave to cool, filter, and dry between filter papers.

03

Preparing Insoluble Salts (Precipitation Method)

Insoluble salts (such as barium sulfate or silver chloride) are prepared by ionic precipitation (mixing two soluble salt solutions together).

Precipitation Principle: Mix a solution containing the required positive metal ion with a solution containing the required negative non-metal ion. The insoluble salt precipitates out instantly.

Example: Preparing Barium Sulfate

Barium Chloride (aq) + Sodium Sulfate (aq) → Barium Sulfate (s) + Sodium Chloride (aq)
The barium sulfate is filtered out, washed with distilled water, and dried in an oven.

Step-by-Step Exam Procedure

  1. Pour 25 cm3 of dilute sulfuric acid into a conical flask and warm gently using a Bunsen burner.
  2. Add copper(II) oxide powder in small portions while stirring with a glass rod until it is in excess (powder stops disappearing).
  3. Filter the hot mixture through filter paper and a funnel to collect the clear blue filtrate in an evaporating basin.
  4. Heat the filtrate using a water bath until crystals start to form around the edge (crystallisation point).
  5. Leave the basin in a warm place to allow large, well-formed blue crystals to grow slowly.
  6. Filter off the remaining liquid and gently dab the pure copper(II) sulfate crystals dry between two sheets of filter paper.
