Combustion Profiles

Evaluate the degree of unsaturation and carbon-to-hydrogen ratios by observing soot levels and flame characteristics in burner tests.

Aim Principle Soot Analysis Procedure Comparison Table Identify Precautions Questions
01

Aim of the Experiment

To evaluate the degree of unsaturation and carbon content of different organic compounds by observing their flame luminosity and soot production during combustion.

Key idea:
Hydrocarbons with higher carbon percentages or higher degrees of unsaturation burn with a smokier, sootier flame due to incomplete combustion in standard atmospheric oxygen.
02

Principle of Combustion Profiles

When hydrocarbons burn, they react with oxygen to produce carbon dioxide and water (complete combustion), or carbon monoxide, unburnt carbon particles (soot), and water (incomplete combustion).

  • Alkanes: Generally have lower carbon-to-hydrogen ratios (except for small chains) and tend to burn with cleaner, less luminous blue/yellow flames.
  • Alkenes & Alkynes: Have higher percentages of carbon by mass and multiple bonds, leading to a smoky, luminous yellow flame full of soot.
  • Aromatic Hydrocarbons: (e.g., benzene rings) have very high carbon-to-hydrogen ratios and burn with intensely smoky, soot-heavy flames.
03

Flame Characteristics & Soot Levels

The appearance of the flame gives immediate qualitative data about the molecular structure of the fuel:

  • Clean Blue Flame: Indicates efficient combustion, low molecular weight, or high hydrogen content.
  • Luminous Yellow Flame: Caused by glowing incandescent carbon particles heated inside the flame.
  • Heavy Soot / Smoke: Indicates an exceptionally high carbon-to-hydrogen ratio or high degree of unsaturation/aromaticity.
04

Experimental Procedure

  1. Place a small amount (3 to 5 drops) of the liquid hydrocarbon onto a clean evaporating dish or combustion spoon.
  2. Ignite the sample using a lighter or Bunsen burner flame.
  3. Observe the color of the flame and hold an inverted clean porcelain crucible lid or glass slide briefly over the flame to collect soot.
  4. Compare the amount of black carbon residue deposited across different samples (e.g., hexane vs. cyclohexene vs. toluene).

07

Precautions & Safety

  • Perform combustion tests using only small quantities (a few drops) to avoid uncontrolled flare-ups.
  • Always work inside a fume hood when burning aromatic or unknown volatile solvents.
  • Keep a fire blanket and safety shield nearby during ignition steps.
08

Common Practical Questions

1. Why do aromatic compounds burn with a sootier flame than alkanes?
Aromatic compounds have a much higher percentage of carbon by mass (high carbon-to-hydrogen ratio), making complete combustion difficult without a specialized oxygen-rich flame.
2. What does a luminous yellow flame typically indicate during a hydrocarbon burn test?
It indicates the presence of glowing incandescent carbon particles formed due to incomplete combustion.
