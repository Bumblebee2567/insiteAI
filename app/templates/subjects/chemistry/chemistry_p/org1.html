Organic Characterization

Distinguish between alkanes, alkenes, and terminal alkynes using halogenation and selective oxidizing agents.

Aim Principle Key Tests Procedure Summary Table Identify Precautions Questions
01

Aim of the Experiment

To chemically differentiate between saturated hydrocarbons (alkanes), unsaturated hydrocarbons (alkenes), and terminal alkynes using characteristic functional group tests.

Key idea:
Functional groups dictate chemical reactivity. While alkanes are generally unreactive due to strong single covalent sigma-bonds, alkenes and alkynes undergo rapid addition and oxidation reactions.
02

Principle of Characterization

Different classes of hydrocarbons react differently with specific chemical reagents:

  • Alkanes: Contain only C-C and C-H single bonds. They do not react with bromine water in the dark and resist mild oxidizing agents.
  • Alkenes: Contain a reactive C=C double bond (pi-bond). They rapidly decolorize bromine water via electrophilic addition.
  • Terminal Alkynes: Contain a C≡C triple bond with an acidic hydrogen atom at the end of the chain, allowing specific metal-ion complexation tests (e.g., Silver Nitrate or Tollens' reagent).
03

Key Reagents & Reactions

1. Bromine Water Test (Br2(aq))

Bromine water is an orange-brown solution. When added to an alkene, the pi-bond attacks the bromine molecule, forming a colorless dibromoalkane addition product. Alkanes show no reaction unless exposed to UV light (free-radical substitution, which is very slow).

2. Potassium Permanganate Test (KMnO4(aq) / Baeyer's Reagent)

Cold, dilute alkaline potassium permanganate oxidizes alkenes to diols (glycols), turning the purple solution into a brown manganese dioxide (MnO2) precipitate or a clear solution. Alkanes do not react.

3. Terminal Alkyne Test (Silver Nitrate / Tollens' Reagent)

Terminal alkynes have a relatively acidic terminal proton (≡C-H). When treated with ammoniacal silver nitrate, they form a white/off-white precipitate of silver acetylide. Internal alkynes do not react because they lack this acidic hydrogen.

04

Experimental Procedure

  1. Place 1 mL of each hydrocarbon sample (alkane, alkene, alkyne) into separate clean test tubes.
  2. Add 2 to 3 drops of bromine water to each test tube and gently shake. Record color changes.
  3. Repeat using a fresh set of samples with cold dilute potassium permanganate solution.
  4. Perform the silver nitrate test specifically to identify terminal alkynes by checking for precipitate formation.

07

Precautions & Safety

  • Handle liquid hydrocarbons in a well-ventilated fume hood as they are volatile and flammable.
  • Bromine water is toxic and causes skin irritation; wear gloves and safety goggles.
  • Dispose of silver residues properly to prevent explosive silver fulminate formation.
08

Common Practical Questions

1. Why do alkenes react with bromine water while alkanes do not?
Alkenes possess a reactive carbon-carbon double bond (pi-bond) that undergoes electrophilic addition, whereas alkanes only have stable sigma-bonds.
2. How can you distinguish between an alkene and a terminal alkyne?
Terminal alkynes react with ammoniacal silver nitrate to form a white precipitate due to their acidic terminal proton, whereas alkenes do not react with silver nitrate.
