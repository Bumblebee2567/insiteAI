Flame Emission Tests

Observe the characteristic flame colours produced by selected Group 1 and Group 2 metal ions and use the colours to identify unknown substances.

Aim Principle Apparatus Procedure Flame Colours Identify Conclusion Precautions Questions
01

Aim of the Experiment

To identify certain metal ions by observing the characteristic colours they produce when their compounds are introduced into a hot Bunsen burner flame.

Key idea:
Different metal ions emit different characteristic colours when heated strongly in a flame.
02

Principle of the Flame Test

When a metal salt is heated in a flame, the heat energy causes electrons in the metal atoms to move to higher energy levels. When these electrons return to lower energy levels, energy is released as light.

The wavelength of the emitted light determines the colour that we observe.

Why are the colours different?
Each element has a unique arrangement of electron energy levels. Therefore, different elements release different wavelengths of visible light when their excited electrons return to lower energy levels.

Emission of Light

Violet Blue Green Yellow Orange Red
03

Apparatus & Materials

  • Bunsen burner
  • Clean nichrome or platinum wire loop
  • Dilute hydrochloric acid for cleaning the wire
  • Samples of suitable metal salts
  • Safety goggles
  • Heat-resistant laboratory equipment
Why is hydrochloric acid used?
Hydrochloric acid can help remove residues from the wire. The wire must be cleaned thoroughly between tests to prevent contamination from one sample affecting another flame colour.
04

Experimental Procedure

  1. Light the Bunsen burner and adjust it to produce a hot, non-luminous flame.
  2. Clean the wire loop using dilute hydrochloric acid.
  3. Heat the wire in the flame until it produces no noticeable colour of its own.
  4. Dip the clean wire into a small amount of the metal salt sample.
  5. Place the sample-containing wire into the hottest part of the Bunsen flame.
  6. Observe and record the colour produced.
  7. Clean the wire thoroughly before testing the next sample.
Important:
Always clean the wire between different samples. Sodium contamination is particularly noticeable because its intense yellow emission can mask weaker flame colours.
05

Characteristic Flame Colours

The following are common flame-test observations for selected Group 1 and Group 2 metal ions.

Element Ion Group Flame Colour
Lithium Li+ 1 Crimson red
Sodium Na+ 1 Yellow
Potassium K+ 1 Lilac
Calcium Ca2+ 2 Brick red / orange-red
Strontium Sr2+ 2 Crimson / scarlet red
Barium Ba2+ 2 Apple green
Note:
Magnesium does not normally produce a strong characteristic visible flame colour in the usual flame test, so it is not included in the main identification table above.

07

Observation & Conclusion

Observation

Different metal salt samples produce different characteristic colours when heated in the Bunsen burner flame.

Conclusion

Flame tests can be used as a qualitative test to help identify certain metal ions because their excited atoms emit characteristic wavelengths of visible light.

Remember:
A flame test provides evidence for the presence of a particular metal ion, but it should not always be treated as the only test needed for complete identification of an unknown compound.
08

Precautions & Sources of Error

Precautions

  • Wear appropriate eye protection.
  • Handle the Bunsen burner carefully.
  • Use small amounts of the test substances.
  • Clean the wire thoroughly between tests.
  • Use a clean flame to avoid contamination.
  • Do not look directly into an excessively bright flame.

Possible Sources of Error

  • Contamination of the wire by another metal ion.
  • Using too much of the sample.
  • Failure to clean the wire properly.
  • Difficulty distinguishing similar flame colours.
  • Strong sodium contamination masking weaker colours.
09

Common Practical Questions

1. What colour does sodium produce in a flame test?
Yellow.
2. What colour is produced by potassium?
Lilac.
3. Why must the wire be cleaned between tests?
To prevent contamination from a previous sample from affecting the next flame colour.
4. What colour is produced by barium?
Apple green.
5. Why do different elements produce different flame colours?
Their electrons have different energy-level arrangements, producing different wavelengths of emitted light.
