Gas Evolution Tests

Identify common gases produced during chemical reactions using characteristic tests with limewater, test papers, glowing splints, lighted splints, and other simple laboratory methods.

Aim Principle Apparatus Procedure Gas Tests Observations Equations Identify Precautions Questions
01

Aim of the Experiment

To identify common gases produced during chemical reactions by observing their characteristic physical and chemical reactions with suitable test reagents.

Key idea:
Different gases have different chemical properties. These properties allow them to be identified using simple tests such as limewater, damp indicator paper, and splints.
02

Principle of Gas Testing

A gas produced in a chemical reaction can often be identified by a characteristic reaction. The gas may cause a colour change in a test paper, relight a glowing splint, extinguish a flame, or produce a distinctive precipitate.

The test chosen depends on the chemical properties of the gas being investigated.

Why are gas tests useful?
Many gases are colourless and may look identical. A chemical test provides observable evidence that can help distinguish one gas from another.

Main Identification Methods

Limewater Test

Carbon dioxide turns limewater milky because calcium carbonate is formed.

Splint Tests

Hydrogen, oxygen and other gases can be distinguished using carefully performed splint tests.

Test Papers

Ammonia changes damp red litmus paper blue, while chlorine bleaches damp coloured indicator paper.

Observation

Always record visible changes such as colour changes, effervescence, precipitates and flame behaviour.

03

Apparatus & Materials

  • Test tubes
  • Test-tube rack
  • Delivery tube where required
  • Rubber stopper
  • Limewater
  • Damp red and blue litmus paper
  • Suitable indicator paper
  • Wooden splints
  • Bunsen burner
  • Suitable gas-generating chemicals
  • Safety goggles
Safety:
Gas tests involving flames or potentially harmful gases must be performed carefully under appropriate laboratory supervision. Do not deliberately inhale an unknown gas.
04

General Experimental Procedure

  1. Place the appropriate reactants into a clean reaction vessel.
  2. Allow the reaction to produce the gas.
  3. Collect or direct the gas safely toward the appropriate test.
  4. Select the test appropriate for the suspected gas.
  5. Observe any colour change, flame behaviour, precipitate, or other characteristic result.
  6. Record the observation carefully.
  7. Compare the observation with the expected result for each gas.
Important:
Never assume that a gas is identified from its appearance alone. Most of the gases in this practical are colourless, so a chemical test is required.
05

Tests for Common Gases

The following tests are commonly used to identify carbon dioxide, chlorine, oxygen, hydrogen and ammonia.

Gas Test Observation Inference
Carbon dioxide
CO₂ 		Bubble the gas through limewater. Limewater turns milky/cloudy. Carbon dioxide is present.
Chlorine
Cl₂ 		Expose damp litmus or suitable damp indicator paper to the gas. Damp indicator paper is bleached. Chlorine is present.
Oxygen
O₂ 		Insert a glowing splint into the gas. The glowing splint relights. Oxygen is present.
Hydrogen
H₂ 		Apply a lighted splint to a small sample of the gas. A characteristic squeaky pop is heard. Hydrogen is present.
Ammonia
NH₃ 		Hold damp red litmus paper near the gas. Red litmus turns blue. Ammonia is present.
Remember:
Oxygen supports combustion but is not itself a fuel. Hydrogen is combustible, while carbon dioxide does not support combustion.
06

Test Observations in Detail

Carbon Dioxide

Carbon dioxide is passed through limewater. The limewater becomes milky because insoluble calcium carbonate forms.

Chlorine

Chlorine reacts with moisture on damp indicator paper and can bleach the paper. This bleaching property is a useful identifying feature.

Oxygen

A glowing splint placed into oxygen relights because oxygen supports combustion.

Hydrogen

A small sample of hydrogen tested with a lighted splint produces a characteristic pop as the hydrogen reacts with oxygen.

Ammonia

Ammonia is alkaline. It turns damp red litmus paper blue.

Exam tip:
Learn the test → observation → inference pattern. Practical questions often provide the observation and ask you to identify the gas.
07

Important Chemical Equations

Carbon Dioxide and Limewater

CO2(g) + Ca(OH)2(aq) → CaCO3(s) + H2O(l)

Hydrogen Combustion

2H2(g) + O2(g) → 2H2O(l)

Ammonia and Water

NH3(g) + H2O(l) ⇌ NH4+(aq) + OH−(aq)

Chlorine and Water

Cl2(g) + H2O(l) ⇌ HCl(aq) + HClO(aq)
Note:
Chlorine's bleaching action is associated with the formation of reactive chlorine-containing species in the presence of water.

09

Precautions & Sources of Error

Precautions

  • Wear appropriate eye protection.
  • Handle chemical reagents carefully.
  • Never deliberately inhale an unknown gas.
  • Use small quantities when performing flame-based gas tests.
  • Keep flammable gases away from unnecessary ignition sources.
  • Perform potentially hazardous gas tests under appropriate laboratory supervision and ventilation.

Possible Sources of Error

  • Contamination of the gas sample.
  • Using a damp or contaminated test apparatus.
  • Misreading a colour change on indicator paper.
  • Using too much or too little reagent.
  • Testing an impure gas mixture rather than a single gas.
Safety reminder:
Some gases used in qualitative analysis can be harmful, corrosive, toxic, or flammable. Follow your laboratory's safety procedures and teacher's instructions.
10

Common Practical Questions

1. How is carbon dioxide tested?
Pass the gas through limewater. The limewater turns milky.
2. What test identifies oxygen?
A glowing splint relights when placed in oxygen.
3. What test identifies hydrogen?
A lighted splint produces a characteristic squeaky pop with hydrogen.
4. How can ammonia be identified?
Ammonia turns damp red litmus paper blue.
5. What happens when chlorine contacts damp indicator paper?
Chlorine can bleach damp indicator paper.
6. Why does carbon dioxide turn limewater milky?
Carbon dioxide reacts with calcium hydroxide in limewater to form insoluble calcium carbonate.
7. Which gas relights a glowing splint?
Oxygen.
8. Which gas produces a squeaky pop with a lighted splint?
Hydrogen.
9. Which gas turns damp red litmus paper blue?
Ammonia.
10. Why should an unknown gas never be deliberately inhaled?
Some gases can be toxic, corrosive, or otherwise harmful. Identification should be performed using the appropriate chemical test instead.
