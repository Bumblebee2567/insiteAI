1. What Is the Periodic Table?

The periodic table is a systematic arrangement of chemical elements according to their atomic number, electron configuration and recurring chemical properties.

Every known chemical element is represented by a symbol. For example, hydrogen is H, oxygen is O, carbon is C, sodium is Na and chlorine is Cl.

Key idea: The modern periodic table is arranged in increasing atomic number. Atomic number means the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom.

Why Is It Called "Periodic"?

The word periodic means something that repeats at regular intervals.

When elements are arranged by increasing atomic number, similar chemical and physical properties appear again and again. This repeating pattern is called periodicity.

2. History of the Periodic Table

Early Attempts

Before the modern periodic table existed, scientists attempted to classify elements according to similarities in their properties.

Döbereiner's Triads

Johann Wolfgang Döbereiner noticed that some elements could be arranged in groups of three called triads.

In some triads, the atomic mass of the middle element was approximately the average of the other two.

Newlands' Law of Octaves

John Newlands proposed that when elements were arranged according to increasing atomic mass, similar properties appeared approximately every eighth element.

Mendeleev

Dmitri Mendeleev developed one of the most important early periodic tables.

He arranged elements mainly according to atomic mass but also considered their chemical properties.

One of Mendeleev's greatest achievements was leaving gaps for elements that had not yet been discovered.

Important: Mendeleev did not simply make a list of known elements. He used the patterns in the table to predict the existence and properties of undiscovered elements.

Moseley and Atomic Number

Henry Moseley demonstrated that atomic number was a more fundamental basis for arranging elements than atomic mass.

This helped establish the modern periodic law.

Modern Periodic Law: The physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers.

3. Structure of the Periodic Table

The periodic table contains rows called periods and columns called groups.

Periods

Horizontal rows in the periodic table.

Groups

Vertical columns containing elements with related valence-electron patterns.

Atomic Number

The number of protons in an atom.

Electron Configuration

The arrangement of electrons around the nucleus.

5. Groups of the Periodic Table

A group is a vertical column of the periodic table. Elements in the same group often have similar chemical properties because they have related valence-electron configurations.

Group 1 — Alkali Metals

Group 1 contains lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, caesium and francium.

They have one main valence electron and commonly form +1 ions.

Group 2 — Alkaline Earth Metals

These elements generally have two valence electrons and commonly form +2 ions.

Groups 3–12 — Transition Elements

These include many familiar metals such as iron, copper, zinc and nickel.

Transition elements often have variable oxidation states and can form coloured compounds.

Group 17 — Halogens

Halogens include fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine and astatine.

They have seven valence electrons and commonly gain one electron to form -1 ions.

Group 18 — Noble Gases

Noble gases include helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon.

They generally have very stable outer electron configurations and are comparatively unreactive.

6. Periods

A period is a horizontal row in the periodic table. There are seven periods.

Moving from left to right across a period generally increases atomic number by one.

Elements within the same period have electrons occupying the same principal energy level, although the sublevels being filled change across the period.

7. The s, p, d and f Blocks

The periodic table can also be divided according to the type of atomic orbital being filled by the differentiating electron.

s-block

The s-block contains Groups 1 and 2, together with helium by electron configuration.

p-block

The p-block contains Groups 13–18.

d-block

The d-block contains the transition elements.

f-block

The f-block contains the lanthanides and actinides.

8. Metals, Nonmetals and Metalloids

Metals

Metals generally conduct heat and electricity well, are malleable and ductile, and tend to lose electrons during chemical reactions.

Nonmetals

Nonmetals generally have poorer electrical conductivity and tend to gain or share electrons.

Metalloids

Metalloids have properties intermediate between metals and nonmetals.

Silicon is an important example because of its semiconductor properties.

9. Atomic Radius

Atomic radius describes the approximate size of an atom. Because an electron cloud does not have a sharp outer boundary, atomic radius is not simply the distance from the nucleus to a clearly defined surface.

How Is Atomic Radius Defined?

Different definitions are used depending on the situation. Common types include covalent radius, metallic radius and van der Waals radius.

Covalent Radius

The covalent radius is approximately half the distance between the nuclei of two identical atoms joined by a covalent bond.

Covalent radius ≈ ½ × internuclear distance

Trend Across a Period

Atomic radius generally decreases from left to right across a period.

The reason is that nuclear charge increases while the added electrons are being placed into the same principal energy level. The stronger effective nuclear attraction pulls the electron cloud closer to the nucleus.

Across a period: Atomic radius generally ↓

Trend Down a Group

Atomic radius generally increases down a group.

Each successive period introduces another occupied principal energy level. This places the outer electrons farther from the nucleus and increases shielding.

Down a group: Atomic radius generally ↑
Important: There is no single person who "discovered atomic radius." Atomic radius is a calculated or experimentally inferred property whose exact value depends on the definition and bonding environment being used.

10. Ionic Radius

Ionic radius is the approximate size of an ion.

Cations

A cation is a positively charged ion formed when an atom loses one or more electrons.

Cations are generally smaller than their corresponding neutral atoms.

This happens because losing electrons reduces electron repulsion and can remove an entire occupied energy level.

Anions

An anion is a negatively charged ion formed when an atom gains electrons.

Anions are generally larger than their corresponding neutral atoms because the additional electrons increase electron-electron repulsion.

Example: Na is larger than Na⁺, while Cl⁻ is larger than Cl.

Isoelectronic Ions

Isoelectronic species have the same number of electrons.

When comparing isoelectronic ions, the species with more protons generally has the smaller radius because the same number of electrons is attracted by a greater nuclear charge.

11. Ionisation Energy

Ionisation energy is the energy required to remove an electron from a gaseous atom or ion.

X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻

Across a Period

First ionisation energy generally increases from left to right because effective nuclear attraction generally increases.

Down a Group

Ionisation energy generally decreases down a group because the outer electron is farther from the nucleus and more strongly shielded.

Successive Ionisation Energies

Removing additional electrons requires additional ionisation energies.

A very large jump can indicate that all valence electrons have already been removed and the next electron would be removed from an inner shell.

12. Electron Affinity

Electron affinity describes the energy change associated with adding an electron to a gaseous atom.

X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g)

Electron affinity trends are more complicated than many other periodic trends because electron-electron repulsion, orbital structure and electron configuration affect the energy change.

Halogens generally have strong tendencies to gain electrons because gaining one electron gives them a filled outer shell.

13. Electronegativity

Electronegativity is the tendency of an atom in a chemical bond to attract the shared electrons toward itself.

Periodic Trend

Electronegativity generally increases from left to right across a period.

It generally decreases down a group.

Electronegativity: ↑ across a period
↓ down a group

Fluorine

Fluorine is commonly identified as the most electronegative element on the Pauling scale.

14. Periodic Trends Summary

Atomic Radius

Generally decreases across a period and increases down a group.

Ionisation Energy

Generally increases across a period and decreases down a group.

Electronegativity

Generally increases across a period and decreases down a group.

Ionic Radius

Depends strongly on charge, electron configuration and nuclear attraction.

Why Do These Trends Exist?

Most periodic trends can be understood using three major ideas:

  • Nuclear charge — the positive charge produced by protons in the nucleus.
  • Shielding — the reduction in attraction caused by electrons between the nucleus and an outer electron.
  • Distance — the separation between the nucleus and the outer electrons.
Effective nuclear charge: The outer electron does not experience the full attraction of all the protons equally because inner electrons shield it from part of the nuclear charge.

15. Valency and Valence Electrons

Valence electrons are electrons in the outermost occupied shell that are involved in many chemical reactions and bonding processes.

Why Are Valence Electrons Important?

The number and arrangement of valence electrons strongly influence how an element reacts.

For example, Group 1 elements generally have one valence electron and readily lose it.

Group 17 elements generally have seven valence electrons and tend to gain one.

16. Reactivity and the Periodic Table

Alkali Metals

Alkali-metal reactivity generally increases down the group. The outer electron becomes easier to remove because it is farther from the nucleus and more shielded.

Halogens

Halogen reactivity generally decreases down the group. Their ability to attract an additional electron generally becomes weaker as atomic size increases.

Noble Gases

Noble gases are generally unreactive because their electron configurations are particularly stable.

17. Why the Periodic Table Matters

The periodic table is more than a list of elements. It allows chemists to predict properties and understand relationships between elements.

  • Predicting whether an element is metallic or nonmetallic.
  • Predicting common ion charges.
  • Understanding chemical bonding.
  • Comparing atomic sizes.
  • Comparing ionisation energies.
  • Understanding electronegativity.
  • Predicting chemical reactivity.
  • Understanding electron configurations.
  • Predicting similarities between elements in the same group.
