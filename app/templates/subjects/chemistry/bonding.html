Chemical Bonding

1. What Is Chemical Bonding?

Chemical bonding is the attraction that holds atoms or ions together in a substance.

Atoms do not normally exist as completely independent particles. Many atoms combine with other atoms because doing so can produce a more stable arrangement of electrons.

The electrons involved most directly in bonding are called valence electrons. The way these electrons are transferred, shared, or delocalised determines the type of bond formed.

Main idea: Chemical bonding is fundamentally about the interactions between positively charged nuclei and negatively charged electrons. Different arrangements of these particles produce different types of bonding and therefore different physical and chemical properties.

2. Why Do Atoms Form Bonds?

Atoms form bonds because bonded arrangements can have lower potential energy and therefore greater stability than separated arrangements.

Energy and Stability

When two atoms approach one another, attractive forces can develop between the nucleus of one atom and electrons associated with the other atom. At the same time, repulsive forces also exist.

At a suitable distance, the attractive and repulsive interactions produce a stable arrangement known as the equilibrium bond length.

Lower potential energy → greater stability
A chemical bond is not simply an atom "wanting" electrons. Bond formation results from electrostatic interactions and the overall energy of the system.

3. Valence Electrons

Valence electrons are the electrons in the outermost occupied shell of an atom that are most involved in chemical bonding.

Why Are They Important?

The number of valence electrons strongly influences how an element reacts and what type of ions or bonds it can form.

Metals

Metals often lose valence electrons and form positively charged ions called cations.

Nonmetals

Nonmetals often gain or share electrons when forming chemical bonds.

Example: Sodium

Sodium has one valence electron.

It can lose that electron to form Na⁺.

Na → Na⁺ + e⁻

4. Ionic Bonding

An ionic bond is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.

Ionic compounds commonly form when electrons are transferred from a metal atom to a nonmetal atom.

Formation of Sodium Chloride

1

Sodium loses an electron

Sodium has one valence electron and can lose it.

Na → Na⁺ + e⁻
2

Chlorine gains the electron

Chlorine has seven valence electrons and can gain one electron to complete its outer shell.

Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻
3

The ions attract

Na⁺ and Cl⁻ have opposite charges, so they attract electrostatically.

Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl

Ionic Lattice

Ionic compounds do not usually consist of separate NaCl molecules. Instead, their ions form a repeating three-dimensional structure called an ionic lattice.

This is why writing NaCl represents the simplest ratio of ions rather than a single isolated NaCl molecule in the solid.

Properties of Ionic Compounds

  • Usually high melting and boiling points.
  • Often form crystalline solids.
  • Many are soluble in polar solvents such as water.
  • Conduct electricity when molten.
  • Conduct electricity when dissolved in water if the ions are free to move.
  • Usually do not conduct electricity effectively as solid crystals because their ions cannot move freely.

5. Covalent Bonding

A covalent bond forms when atoms share electron pairs.

Covalent bonding is particularly common between nonmetal atoms.

Single Covalent Bond

A single covalent bond contains one shared pair of electrons.

H — H

Double Covalent Bond

A double bond contains two shared electron pairs.

O = O

Triple Covalent Bond

A triple bond contains three shared electron pairs.

N ≡ N

Single Bond

One shared pair of electrons. Generally longer and weaker than corresponding multiple bonds.

Double Bond

Two shared electron pairs. Generally shorter and stronger than a single bond between the same atoms.

Triple Bond

Three shared electron pairs. Generally shorter and stronger than corresponding single and double bonds.

Coordinate Covalent Bond

A covalent bond in which both electrons in the shared pair are supplied by the same atom.

6. Metallic Bonding

Metallic bonding occurs between metal atoms. In a simplified model, metal atoms contribute valence electrons to a delocalised electron sea surrounding a lattice of positive metal ions.

Why Metals Conduct Electricity

The electrons in a metallic structure are able to move through the lattice. When a potential difference is applied, these mobile electrons can carry electrical charge.

Why Metals Are Malleable

Layers of metal ions can shift while remaining attracted to the delocalised electrons. This allows metals to bend and deform without immediately breaking their entire structure.

Electrical Conductivity

Delocalised electrons can move through the metallic lattice.

Malleability

Metal layers can move without completely destroying the metallic bonding.

7. Lewis Structures

Lewis structures are diagrams used to represent valence electrons and bonding in molecules and ions.

What Do the Dots Represent?

Each dot represents a valence electron. A pair of dots between atoms can represent a shared electron pair.

Example: Hydrogen Molecule

H : H

The two hydrogen atoms share one pair of electrons.

Steps for Drawing a Lewis Structure

1

Count valence electrons

Determine the total number of valence electrons contributed by all atoms.

2

Choose the central atom

Usually the least electronegative atom is placed in the centre, while hydrogen is almost always terminal.

3

Connect the atoms

Draw single bonds between the central atom and surrounding atoms.

4

Complete outer shells

Distribute remaining electrons around the surrounding atoms before placing remaining electrons on the central atom.

5

Check the structure

Check the electron count, formal charges, and whether multiple bonds are required.

8. The Octet Rule

The octet rule states that many atoms tend to achieve a stable arrangement involving eight electrons in their valence shell.

Important: The octet rule is a useful guideline, not an absolute law. There are important exceptions, including electron-deficient species, odd-electron species, and atoms that can accommodate expanded valence shells.

Common Ways Atoms Reach Stable Configurations

  • Loss of electrons.
  • Gain of electrons.
  • Sharing of electrons.
  • Delocalisation of electrons in metallic structures.

9. Electronegativity and Bond Polarity

Electronegativity describes the tendency of an atom in a chemical bond to attract the shared electrons toward itself.

Electronegativity Difference

When two bonded atoms have different electronegativities, the bonding electrons may be pulled more strongly toward one atom. This produces a polar bond.

ΔEN = |EN₁ − EN₂|

The exact classification boundaries depend on the scale and convention being used, so electronegativity difference should not be treated as a universal set of rigid cutoffs.

Nonpolar Covalent Bond

If the bonding electrons are shared relatively equally, the bond is described as nonpolar covalent.

Polar Covalent Bond

If the electrons are shared unequally, partial charges can develop.

δ⁺ — δ⁻
Remember: Bond polarity and molecular polarity are not always the same thing. The shape of the molecule determines whether individual bond dipoles cancel or reinforce one another.

10. Molecular Shapes

Molecular shape is strongly influenced by the repulsions between regions of electron density around a central atom.

VSEPR Theory

VSEPR stands for Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion.

The central idea is that electron domains arrange themselves as far apart as possible to reduce repulsion.

Linear

Bond angle approximately 180° in the ideal two-domain case.

Trigonal Planar

Three electron domains arranged around the central atom.

Tetrahedral

Four bonding electron domains with an ideal angle of about 109.5°.

Bent

Lone pairs on the central atom can produce a bent molecular shape.

Lone pairs generally repel more strongly than bonding pairs. Therefore, lone pairs can reduce bond angles from their ideal electron-domain geometry values.

11. Intermolecular Forces

Intermolecular forces are attractions between separate molecules. They are different from the covalent or ionic interactions that hold atoms or ions together within a substance.

London Dispersion Forces

Attractions caused by temporary fluctuations in electron distribution. They occur in all atoms and molecules.

Dipole-Dipole Forces

Attractions between permanent partial charges in polar molecules.

Hydrogen Bonding

A particularly strong type of intermolecular attraction involving hydrogen bonded to highly electronegative atoms, especially N, O, or F.

Stronger intermolecular attractions generally require more energy to separate molecules, which can contribute to higher boiling points and melting points.

12. Bond Properties

Bond Length

Bond length is the average distance between the nuclei of two bonded atoms.

Bond Energy

Bond energy is related to the energy required to break a bond. Stronger bonds generally require more energy to break.

Bond Order

Bond order describes the number of bonding pairs between two atoms in simple Lewis structures.

Single bond → Bond order = 1
Double bond → Bond order = 2
Triple bond → Bond order = 3

Higher bond order → generally shorter, stronger bond

Lower bond order → generally longer, weaker bond

13. Giant Structures

Some substances form enormous continuous structures rather than small independent molecules.

Giant Ionic Structures

Ionic solids contain a repeating lattice of positive and negative ions.

Giant Covalent Structures

In giant covalent structures, atoms are connected by an extensive network of covalent bonds.

Diamond

Each carbon atom is strongly covalently bonded to four neighbouring carbon atoms, producing a rigid three-dimensional network.

Graphite

Carbon atoms form layers. Each carbon is covalently bonded to three others, with delocalised electrons contributing to electrical conductivity.

15. Worked Examples

Example 1: What type of bond forms between sodium and chlorine?

Sodium is a metal and chlorine is a nonmetal.

Sodium can lose one electron while chlorine can gain one.

Na → Na⁺     Cl → Cl⁻

The resulting electrostatic attraction is an ionic bond.

Example 2: Why is O₂ covalently bonded?

Oxygen is a nonmetal. Two oxygen atoms can share electrons rather than one simply transferring electrons to the other.

O = O

The two oxygen atoms share two pairs of electrons, forming a double covalent bond.

Example 3: Why does MgCl₂ contain two chloride ions?

Magnesium forms Mg²⁺ by losing two electrons.

Each chlorine atom can gain one electron to form Cl⁻.

Mg²⁺ + 2Cl⁻ → MgCl₂

Two chloride ions are therefore required to balance the +2 charge of one magnesium ion.

16. Chemical Bonding Word Problems

Problem 1

An element has one valence electron and reacts with an element that has seven valence electrons. Predict the likely type of bonding.

Solution: An element with one valence electron is likely to lose that electron, while an element with seven valence electrons can gain one. This suggests electron transfer and therefore ionic bonding.

Problem 2

A substance conducts electricity when molten but does not conduct electricity effectively as a solid. What type of structure might it have?

Solution: This behaviour is characteristic of an ionic compound. In the solid, ions are fixed in the lattice. When molten, the ions become mobile and can carry charge.

Problem 3

Explain why a metal can conduct electricity in the solid state.

Solution: Metallic bonding contains delocalised electrons. These electrons can move through the metallic structure and carry electrical charge.
