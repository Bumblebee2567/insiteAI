Stoichiometry

A complete guide to the quantitative relationships between reactants and products in chemical reactions, including moles, molar mass, balanced equations, mole ratios, limiting reactants, empirical formulae, molecular formulae, percentage yield, concentration and chemical calculations.

1. What Is Stoichiometry?

Stoichiometry is the part of chemistry concerned with measuring the amounts of substances involved in chemical reactions.

In simple terms, stoichiometry answers questions such as: How much reactant is needed? How much product will be formed? Which reactant will run out first? How many moles are involved? How much mass is produced?

Central idea: A balanced chemical equation provides the quantitative relationship between the substances participating in a reaction.

The Stoichiometric Chain

Mass → Moles → Mole Ratio → Moles → Mass

This chain is one of the most important patterns in stoichiometry. When a problem gives mass and asks for another mass, you normally convert the first mass into moles, use the balanced equation to obtain the required mole ratio, and finally convert the resulting moles into mass.

2. Conservation of Mass

Stoichiometric calculations are based on the conservation of atoms. In an ordinary chemical reaction, atoms are rearranged rather than created or destroyed.

Therefore: The same number of atoms of each element must appear on both sides of a correctly balanced chemical equation.

Example

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

On the left there are four hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms. On the right there are also four hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms.

3. The Mole

A mole is a counting unit used by chemists to describe very large numbers of particles.

Nₐ = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹

This number is Avogadro's constant. One mole of a substance contains approximately 6.022 × 10²³ specified particles.

Mass and Moles

n = m / M

where:

  • n = amount in moles
  • m = mass in grams
  • M = molar mass in g mol⁻¹

Example

Calculate the number of moles in 18 g of water.

Molar mass of H₂O = 18 g mol⁻¹.

n = 18 / 18
Answer: 1 mol

4. Molar Mass

Molar mass is the mass of one mole of a substance. It is calculated by adding the relative atomic masses of all atoms represented in the chemical formula.

Example: H₂O

H₂O = (2 × 1) + 16

= 18 g mol⁻¹

Example: CaCO₃

CaCO₃ = 40 + 12 + (3 × 16)

= 100 g mol⁻¹
Tip: Always count every atom in the formula, including atoms inside brackets.

5. Moles and Number of Particles

The number of particles can be calculated from the amount in moles.

N = nNₐ

where N is the number of particles and Nₐ is Avogadro's constant.

Example

How many molecules are present in 2 mol of water?

N = 2 × 6.022 × 10²³
Answer: 1.2044 × 10²⁴ molecules

6. Balanced Chemical Equations

Before performing most stoichiometric calculations, the chemical equation must be balanced.

Example

Consider the formation of water:

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

The coefficients tell us the relative amounts of the substances involved in the reaction.

Important: The coefficients of a balanced equation represent mole ratios.

7. Stoichiometric Mole Ratios

The coefficients in a balanced equation provide the mole ratio between reactants and products.

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
Substance Coefficient Relationship
H₂ 2 2 mol
O₂ 1 1 mol
H₂O 2 2 mol
Mole ratio: H₂ : O₂ : H₂O = 2 : 1 : 2

8. Mass-to-Mass Stoichiometry

Mass-to-mass calculations require a conversion from mass to moles, a mole-ratio calculation, and a conversion back to mass.

1 Write and balance the equation.

Never begin a mass calculation from an unbalanced equation.

2 Convert the known mass to moles.

n = m / M

3 Apply the mole ratio.

4 Convert product moles to mass.

m = nM

Worked Example

Calcium carbonate decomposes according to:

CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂

Calculate the mass of carbon dioxide produced from 100 g of CaCO₃.

Molar masses:

  • CaCO₃ = 100 g mol⁻¹
  • CO₂ = 44 g mol⁻¹

First find moles of CaCO₃:

n = 100 / 100 = 1 mol

The mole ratio is 1 : 1.

1 mol CaCO₃ → 1 mol CO₂

Therefore:

m = 1 × 44
Answer: 44 g CO₂

9. Empirical Formula

The empirical formula shows the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound.

General Method

1 Convert each element's mass into moles.

2 Divide all mole values by the smallest mole value.

3 Convert the resulting values into the simplest whole-number ratio.

4 Write the empirical formula.

Example

Suppose a compound contains 24 g carbon and 4 g hydrogen.

Carbon:

24 / 12 = 2 mol

Hydrogen:

4 / 1 = 4 mol

Divide by the smallest value, 2:

C : H = 1 : 2
Empirical formula: CH₂

10. Molecular Formula

The molecular formula gives the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule.

n = Molecular mass / Empirical formula mass

The molecular formula is obtained by multiplying every subscript in the empirical formula by n.

Example

If the empirical formula is CH₂ and the molecular mass is 42 g mol⁻¹:

Empirical formula mass = 14

n = 42 / 14 = 3
Molecular formula: C₃H₆

11. Limiting Reactant

The limiting reactant is the reactant that is completely consumed first. It determines the maximum amount of product that can form.

Think of it like a recipe. If you have enough flour but run out of eggs, the eggs determine how many cakes you can make. The eggs are the limiting ingredient.

Example

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Suppose we have 3 mol H₂ and 2 mol O₂.

The equation requires:

2 mol H₂ for every 1 mol O₂

3 mol H₂ requires only 1.5 mol O₂. Since 2 mol O₂ is available, oxygen is in excess.

Limiting reactant: H₂

The maximum amount of water is therefore determined by the available hydrogen.

12. Excess Reactant

An excess reactant is present in a greater amount than is required to react completely with the limiting reactant.

How to Find Excess Remaining

1 Identify the limiting reactant.

2 Use the mole ratio to calculate how much excess reactant is consumed.

3 Subtract the amount consumed from the original amount.

Excess remaining = Initial amount − Amount consumed

13. Theoretical Yield and Percentage Yield

The theoretical yield is the maximum amount of product predicted by stoichiometric calculations.

The actual yield is the amount of product obtained experimentally.

Percentage yield = (Actual yield / Theoretical yield) × 100%

Example

A reaction has a theoretical yield of 20 g but produces 16 g experimentally.

Percentage yield = (16 / 20) × 100
Percentage yield = 80%

Why Is Actual Yield Often Lower?

  • Incomplete reactions
  • Side reactions
  • Loss during transfer or purification
  • Impure reactants
  • Experimental limitations

14. Concentration and Stoichiometry

Concentration relates the amount of dissolved substance to the volume of solution.

c = n / V

where c is concentration in mol dm⁻³, n is moles and V is volume in dm³.

Volume Conversion

1000 cm³ = 1 dm³

Example

A solution contains 0.50 mol of solute in 2.0 dm³.

c = 0.50 / 2.0
Concentration = 0.25 mol dm⁻³

15. Titration Stoichiometry

Titration is a laboratory technique used to determine an unknown concentration by reacting a measured amount of one solution with another solution of known concentration.

Basic Relationship

n = cV

When V is measured in dm³, concentration is commonly expressed in mol dm⁻³ and n is obtained in moles.

General Titration Steps

1 Write the balanced chemical equation.

2 Calculate moles of the known solution.

3 Use the mole ratio.

4 Calculate the unknown concentration.

Important: The concentrations cannot simply be compared directly unless the reaction has a 1:1 mole ratio.

16. Gas Stoichiometry

Gases can also participate in stoichiometric calculations. Depending on the conditions and the course being followed, gas quantities may be related using molar gas volume or the ideal gas equation.

Ideal Gas Equation

PV = nRT

where P is pressure, V is volume, n is amount in moles, R is the gas constant and T is absolute temperature.

Temperature

T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15
Remember: Temperature must be expressed in kelvin when using the ideal gas equation.

17. Percentage Composition

Percentage composition tells us what percentage of a compound's mass comes from a particular element.

Percentage of element = (Mass contributed by element / Molar mass of compound) × 100%

Example: Water

H₂O has molar mass 18.

Hydrogen contributes 2 units of mass.

(2 / 18) × 100
Hydrogen ≈ 11.1%

Oxygen therefore contributes approximately 88.9%.

18. Stoichiometry Word Problems

Stoichiometry word problems may combine several calculations. The safest approach is to identify exactly what information is given, determine what is required, and work through the chemical relationship step by step.

Word Problem 1 — Mass to Moles

A laboratory contains 49 g of sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄. Calculate the number of moles.

Molar mass:

H₂SO₄ = 2(1) + 32 + 4(16)

= 98 g mol⁻¹
n = 49 / 98
Answer: 0.50 mol

Word Problem 2 — Mass to Mass

Magnesium reacts with oxygen:

2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO

Calculate the mass of MgO produced from 24 g of magnesium.

Molar mass of Mg ≈ 24 g mol⁻¹.

Therefore:

n(Mg) = 24 / 24 = 1 mol

The ratio of Mg to MgO is 1:1.

n(MgO) = 1 mol

Molar mass of MgO ≈ 40 g mol⁻¹.

m = 1 × 40
Answer: 40 g MgO

Word Problem 3 — Limiting Reactant

Hydrogen reacts with oxygen according to:

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

If 5 mol H₂ reacts with 2 mol O₂, determine the limiting reactant.

2 mol H₂ require 1 mol O₂. Therefore 5 mol H₂ require:

5 × 1/2 = 2.5 mol O₂

Only 2 mol O₂ is available.

Limiting reactant: O₂

Word Problem 4 — Percentage Yield

A reaction should theoretically produce 25 g of product. An experiment produces 21 g. Calculate the percentage yield.

Percentage yield = (21 / 25) × 100
Answer: 84%

Word Problem 5 — Concentration

A solution contains 0.30 mol of NaOH in 600 cm³ of solution. Calculate its concentration in mol dm⁻³.

First convert the volume:

600 cm³ = 0.600 dm³

Then:

c = 0.30 / 0.600
Answer: 0.50 mol dm⁻³

Word Problem 6 — Multi-Step Stoichiometry

Calcium carbonate decomposes according to:

CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂

A sample contains 250 g of CaCO₃. Calculate the theoretical mass of CO₂ produced.

Relative formula masses:

  • CaCO₃ = 100 g mol⁻¹
  • CO₂ = 44 g mol⁻¹
n(CaCO₃) = 250 / 100 = 2.5 mol

The mole ratio is 1:1.

n(CO₂) = 2.5 mol
m(CO₂) = 2.5 × 44 = 110 g
Theoretical yield = 110 g CO₂
