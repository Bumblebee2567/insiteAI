Comprehensive Introduction to Relational Connectives

Language relies heavily on relational connectives to establish logical, spatial, and temporal bridges between words, phrases, and clauses. Without prepositions and conjunctions, sentences would disintegrate into isolated, disconnected fragments devoid of coherent structural relationships.

In West African examinations such as JAMB and WAEC, test items focusing on prepositions and conjunctions frequently exploit idiomatic collocations and fixed dependent pairings. Examiners deliberately construct trick questions where candidates are tempted to translate literal vernacular prepositions into formal English, resulting in unidiomatic structures.

Core Syntactic Principle:
A preposition invariably requires an object (a noun, pronoun, or noun phrase) in the objective case, establishing a directional or locational relationship. A conjunction, by contrast, serves to link grammatical equals (coordinating) or subordinate a dependent clause to a main clause (subordinating) without taking an object.
02

Prepositions of Time and Place (At, On, In)

The spatial and temporal prepositions at, on, and in form a descending pyramid of specificity that examiners test rigorously.

Temporal Prepositions

  • AT: Used for precise, exact clock times or specific clock hours (e.g., *at 4:00 PM, at noon, at midnight*).
  • ON: Used for specific days, dates, and recurring calendar events (e.g., *on Monday, on October 1st, on Christmas Day*).
  • IN: Used for extended periods such as months, years, centuries, decades, and seasons (e.g., *in July, in 2024, in the 21st century*).

Spatial Prepositions

  • AT: Indicates an exact point or specific location (e.g., *at the bus stop, at the entrance*).
  • ON: Indicates a physical surface or boundary (e.g., *on the wall, on the desk, on the floor*).
  • IN: Indicates containment within an enclosed three-dimensional space or geographical boundary (e.g., *in the room, in Lagos, in a box*).

Fixed Dependent Prepositions (JAMB Favorites)

Many English verbs, adjectives, and nouns demand specific prepositions by idiomatic convention. Memorizing these fixed pairings is essential for securing high scores in lexical and structure tests.

Base Word / Expression Required Dependent Preposition Example Usage in Context Common Exam Trap
Accuse of *The suspect was accused of embezzlement.* Avoid *accused for*.
Comply with *All citizens must comply with traffic laws.* Avoid *comply to*.
Different from *Your perspective is different from mine.* Avoid *different than* in formal exams.
Provoked / Abstain from *Candidates must abstain from exam malpractice.* Avoid *abstain in*.
Superior / Inferior to *This imported fabric is superior to local variants.* Avoid *superior than*.
Consist of (or in) *Matter consists of atoms.* / *Success consists in hard work.* Context determines 'of' vs 'in'.
04

Coordinating & Subordinating Conjunctions

Conjunctions are divided into categories based on the structural weight of the clauses they connect.

Coordinating Conjunctions (FANBOYS)

Coordinating conjunctions connect words, phrases, or independent clauses of equal grammatical rank. They are easily memorized via the acronym FANBOYS: *For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So*.

Subordinating Conjunctions

Subordinating conjunctions introduce dependent (subordinate) clauses, binding them to a main independent clause while establishing a logical relationship of time, cause, condition, or concession (e.g., *although, because, unless, whereas, provided that, since, while*).

05

Correlative Conjunctions & Parallelism

Correlative conjunctions operate in paired sets to join grammatically equivalent sentence elements (*either...or, neither...nor, not only...but also, both...and, whether...or*).

The Parallelism Rule:
When using correlative conjunctions, the grammatical structure following the first word must strictly parallel the structure following the second word.
❌ *Incorrect:* He not only passed the examination, but also won a scholarship. (Mixed verb phrases vs clauses).
✔️ *Correct:* He not only passed the examination but also won a scholarship.

Exhaustive Worked Exam Examples

Problem 1 (Dependent Preposition Correction):

Analyze and correct the error in this WAEC objective item:
"The accused fraudster was charged for stealing public funds before the federal high court."

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution & Grammatical Breakdown:
1. Identify Idiomatic Collocation: In legal and standard English grammar, the verb "charge" when referring to criminal offenses takes the preposition "with", not "for".
2. Contrast: You are charged *with* a crime, even though you may be arrested *for* a crime.
3. Fully Corrected Version:
"The accused fraudster was charged with stealing public funds before the federal high court."
Problem 2 (Correlative Conjunction Parallelism):

Explain why examiners marked the following sentence as ungrammatical:
"The school principal is responsible both for student discipline as well as academic excellence."

Detailed Step-by-Step Explanation:
1. Identify Correlative Pair: The sentence uses the correlative pair "both...and".
2. Diagnose Error: The sentence incorrectly mixes "both" with "as well as" instead of its required partner "and". Furthermore, the prepositional phrase "for" is misplaced.
3. Correction: Restructure the sentence to ensure absolute parallelism.
4. Fully Corrected Version:
"The school principal is responsible for both student discipline and academic excellence."
