Introduction to Verbs & Concord

Verbs are the core engine of any sentence, expressing action, occurrence, or state of being. In English examinations, questions on verbs heavily test Concord (subject-verb agreement), tense consistency, and correct modal usage.

Mastering concord ensures that your verbs match their subjects in number (singular or plural) and person, regardless of intervening prepositional phrases or deceptive modifiers.

Core Grammatical Principle:
A singular subject must always take a singular verb (marked by an '-s' or '-es' in the present tense), while a plural subject takes a plural verb.
02

Subject-Verb Agreement (Concord)

Examiners frequently test complex agreement structures where the subject is separated from the verb by interrupting phrases.

  • Indefinite Pronouns: Words like everyone, everybody, someone, nobody, each, either, and neither always take singular verbs.
    Example: Each of the students has a textbook. (Not *have*).
  • Compound Subjects Joined by "And": Usually take plural verbs (e.g., *John and Mary are coming*). However, if they form a single meal or concept, use a singular verb:
    Example: Rice and stew is my favorite meal.
  • Correlative Conjunctions (*Either...or*, *Neither...nor*): The verb agrees with the subject closest to it.
    Example: Neither the teacher nor the students were present.
Subject Structure Verb Agreement Example Sentence
Quantifiers (*A number of* vs *The number of*) Plural vs Singular *A number of students are here.* vs *The number of students is small.*
Expressions with fractions (*Half of / Two-thirds of*) Depends on the noun following *Half of the water was spilled.* / *Half of the mangoes were eaten.*
Plural-looking titles (*Mathematics, Physics*) Singular verb *Mathematics is a fascinating subject.*
03

Tenses & Sequence of Tenses

Tenses indicate the time of an action. In complex sentences, the tense in the main clause dictates the appropriate tense in the subordinate clause.

  • Present / Future Main Clause: Can be followed by any tense required by the context.
    Example: He knows that she will win the race.
  • Past Main Clause: Requires a past tense in the subordinate clause (sequence of tenses).
    Example: He knew that she would win the race.
  • Universal Truths: Always remain in the simple present tense, even after a past tense main verb.
    Example: Galileo proved that the Earth moves around the sun. (Not *moved*).
04

Auxiliary & Modal Verbs

Modal verbs (such as can, could, shall, should, will, would, may, might, must) express necessity, possibility, permission, or ability.

  • Grammatical Rule: Modals are always followed by the bare infinitive (the root verb without "to").
    ❌ *Incorrect:* She can to speaks fluent French.
    ✔️ *Correct:* She can speak fluent French.
  • No Agreement Suffixes: Modals do not take an "-s" in the third-person singular (e.g., *He must go*, not *he musts*).
05

Conditional Sentences

Conditional sentences express the result of a particular condition. JAMB and WAEC test these structures rigorously:

  • First Conditional (Real Present/Future): If + present simple, future simple (will + infinitive).
    Example: If he comes early, we will start the meeting.
  • Second Conditional (Hypothetical Present/Future): If + past simple, would + infinitive.
    Example: If I won the lottery, I would buy a mansion.
  • Third Conditional (Past Unreal): If + past perfect, would have + past participle.
    Example: If she had studied hard, she would have passed the exam.

Exhaustive Worked Exam Examples

Problem 1 (Subject-Verb Agreement with Intervening Phrases):

Identify the correct verb form in this sentence and explain why: *"The basket of mangoes (is / are) ripe."*

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
- Identify Subject and Prepositional Phrase: The prepositional phrase "of mangoes" comes between the subject and the verb. Many students mistakenly agree the verb with "mangoes".
- True Subject: The actual subject is the singular noun "basket".
- Correct Version: *"The basket of mangoes is ripe."*
Problem 2 (Conditional Sentence Correction):

Correct the grammatical error in this past exam sentence: *"If I had known about the traffic, I would take another route."*

Detailed Step-by-Step Explanation:
- Identify Conditional Type: The clause "If I had known" uses the past perfect tense, which indicates a **Third Conditional** (past unreal situation).
- Correct Structure: The main clause in a third conditional must use "would have + past participle".
- Correct Version: *"If I had known about the traffic, I would have taken another route."*
Problem 3 (Correlative Concord Rule):

Explain why the verb is singular in this sentence: *"Neither the manager nor his assistant (was / were) present at the meeting."*

Detailed Step-by-Step Explanation:
- Grammatical Rule: When subjects are joined by correlative conjunctions like *either...or* or *neither...nor*, the verb agrees with the subject **closest to it**.
- Application: The subject closest to the verb is "his assistant", which is singular. Therefore, the singular verb "was" is correct.
