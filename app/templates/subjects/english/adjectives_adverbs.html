01

Comprehensive Introduction to Adjectives & Adverbs

Language derives its precision, descriptive richness, and clarity from modifiers—specifically adjectives and adverbs. While nouns establish the core subjects and objects of discourse and verbs drive action, modifiers qualify, restrict, expand, or specify the precise attributes and conditions surrounding those nominal and verbal elements.

In West African and international examinations like JAMB and WAEC, test items focusing on adjectives and adverbs move far beyond basic identification. Examiners target subtle syntactic traps, including complex adjective sequencing (the OSASCOMP acronym), irregular comparative and superlative formations, ambiguous modifier placement, and the precise distinction between flat adverbs and standardly inflected adverbial forms.

Core Syntactic Principle:
An adjective modifies a noun or pronoun by answering questions such as *Which one?*, *What kind?*, or *How many?*. Conversely, an adverb modifies a verb, an adjective, or another adverb by answering questions such as *How?*, *When?*, *Where?*, *To what extent?*, or *In what manner?*.

Syntactic Functions of Modifiers

Understanding where modifiers sit relative to their targets determines sentence grammaticality:

  • Attributive Position: Adjectives placed immediately before the noun they modify (e.g., *A brilliant student*).
  • Predicative Position: Adjectives placed after a linking verb, modifying the subject (e.g., *The student is brilliant*).
  • Intensifying Adverbs: Adverbs that scale adjectives or other adverbs (e.g., *She performed exceptionally well*).
02

Specialized Categories of Modifiers

Modifiers encompass diverse subclasses that dictate their grammatical behavior in complex examinations.

Descriptive vs. Limiting Adjectives

Descriptive adjectives ascribe physical or qualitative traits (e.g., *tall, rusted, acidic*). Limiting adjectives restrict nouns rather than describing them, including demonstrative (*this, that*), possessive (*my, their*), and quantitative (*several, much*) determiners.

Flat Adverbs

A flat adverb (or plain adverb) has the exact same morphological form as its adjective counterpart without taking the standard "-ly" suffix. Examiners frequently test sentences where candidates mistakenly add "-ly" to words that should remain flat.

Exam Trap on Flat Adverbs:
❌ *Incorrect:* Drive slowly around the sharp bend.
✔️ *Standard Conversational & Idiomatic English:* Drive slow around the sharp bend.
Note: While *slowly* is grammatically permissible, standard exam items test fixed idiomatic adverbial imperatives like *drive slow, hold tight, fly high, and cost dear*.
03

The Strict Order of Adjectives (OSASCOMP)

When two or more descriptive adjectives precede a single noun in English, native speakers do not sequence them randomly. They follow an unyielding chronological hierarchy codified by the acronym OSASCOMP.

Acronym Letter Category Name Grammatical Function Illustrative Examples
O Opinion Subjective judgment or value *lovely, horrible, intelligent, tedious*
S Size Physical dimensions or scale *massive, tiny, tall, microscopic*
A Age Chronological timeframe *ancient, adolescent, brand-new, elderly*
S Shape Geometric outline or form *spherical, rectangular, curved, flat*
C Color Optical hue or shade *crimson, turquoise, obsidian, amber*
O Origin Geographical source or nationality *Nigerian, Scandinavian, lunar, domestic*
M Material Substance composition *metallic, synthetic, woolen, ceramic*
P Purpose Intended utilization (noun adjunct) *sleeping (bag), drilling (rig), wedding (ring)*
04

Degrees of Comparison & Superlative Traps

Adjectives and adverbs alter their morphological structure across three degrees of comparison: Positive, Comparative, and Superlative. JAMB and WAEC test exceptional irregular forms and structural redundancies.

Rules of Morphological Scaling

  • Short Modifiers: Take "-er" for comparative and "-est" for superlative (e.g., *fast, faster, fastest*).
  • Long Modifiers: Take "more" for comparative and "most" for superlative (e.g., *intelligent, more intelligent, most intelligent*).
  • Absolute Adjectives (Non-Gradable): Certain modifiers describe absolute states that cannot logically be compared (e.g., *dead, unique, universal, perfect, fatal, infinite*). Saying *"more perfect"* or *"most unique"* is heavily penalized in exams.
05

Adverbial Placement & Focus

Unlike adjectives which have relatively fixed attributive positions before nouns, adverbs are highly mobile within sentence architecture. However, shifting an adverb's position can dramatically alter the scope and focus of the statement.

The Restrictive Adverb Trap (*Only*):
The adverb "only" must be placed immediately before the specific word or phrase it modifies. Misplacing "only" completely alters legal meaning.
*Meaning A:* Only Chinedu scored an A in physics. (Nobody else scored an A).
*Meaning B:* Chinedu only scored an A in physics. (He scored an A, but did not study or do anything else).
06

Misplaced, Dangling, and Squinting Modifiers

A modifier is classified as misplaced when syntactic distance separates it from its intended target, creating illogical or humorous interpretations. A dangling modifier occurs when the introductory modifying phrase lacks any explicit noun target to modify within the main clause.

Exhaustive Worked Exam Examples

Problem 1 (Advanced OSASCOMP Sequencing Analysis):

Analyze and correct the adjective sequence in this WAEC structural item:
"The museum curator displayed an antique magnificent wooden large carved sculpture."

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution & Grammatical Breakdown:
1. Extract Modifiers: *antique (Age), magnificent (Opinion), wooden (Material), large (Size), carved (Purpose/Descriptor)*.
2. Apply OSASCOMP Hierarchy:
- **O**pinion: *magnificent*
- **S**ize: *large*
- **A**ge: *antique*
- **M**aterial: *wooden*
- **P**urpose/Descriptor: *carved*
3. Fully Corrected Version:
"The museum curator displayed a magnificent large antique wooden carved sculpture."
Problem 2 (Non-Gradable Absolute Adjectives):

Explain why the examiners marked the sentence *"Dr. Bello's medical hypothesis was far more unique than previous theories"* as structurally flawed.

Detailed Step-by-Step Explanation:
1. Analyze "Unique": The adjective "unique" means being the only one of its kind; it denotes an absolute, binary state (an object is either unique or it is not).
2. Identify Redundancy: Because uniqueness is absolute, it cannot take comparative scaling modifiers like *more* or *most*.
3. Correction: Remove the comparative modifier, or replace "unique" with a gradable alternative like *unusual* or *original*.
4. Fully Corrected Version:
"Dr. Bello's medical hypothesis was more original than previous theories."
