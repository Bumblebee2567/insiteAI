Introduction to Ecological Systems

Ecology is the scientific study of the interactions between organisms and their biotic (living) and abiotic (non-living) environments. The hierarchy of ecological organization ranges from individual organisms to populations, communities, ecosystems, landscapes, and ultimately the global biosphere.

Ecosystem stability relies on the continuous flow of energy originating from solar radiation and the cyclic exchange of chemical nutrients between living organisms and their physical environment.

Core Ecological Principle:
Energy flows through ecosystems in a one-way path (from producers to consumers and eventually lost as metabolic heat), whereas chemical nutrients are perpetually recycled within biogeochemical closed loops.
02

Energy Flow & Trophic Webs

Ecosystem energetics describes how energy enters, moves through, and dissipates out of biological communities:

  • Primary Producers (Autotrophs): Organisms such as plants, algae, and cyanobacteria that capture solar energy via photosynthesis (or chemical energy via chemosynthesis) to synthesize organic compounds.
  • Consumers (Heterotrophs): Herbivores (primary consumers eating producers), carnivores (secondary and tertiary consumers eating other animals), and omnivores.
  • Decomposers (Detritivores): Fungi and bacteria that break down dead organic matter and waste, returning inorganic nutrients to the soil.
  • The Ten Percent Rule: Due to metabolic heat loss, respiration, and unconsumed waste, only approximately 10 percent of energy is transferred from one trophic level to the next higher level.

03

Biogeochemical Nutrient Cycles

Nutrients circulate through ecosystems via complex biogeochemical cycles involving biotic reservoirs, geological formations, and atmospheric sinks:

  • The Water (Hydrologic) Cycle: Driven by solar evaporation of oceanic and terrestrial water, transpiration from plant leaves, condensation into clouds, and precipitation returning water to land and oceans.
  • The Carbon Cycle: Atmospheric carbon dioxide is absorbed by plants during photosynthesis, incorporated into organic molecules, passed through food webs, and returned to the atmosphere via cellular respiration, decomposition, and combustion of fossil fuels.
  • The Nitrogen Cycle: Atmospheric nitrogen gas (N2) is unreactive to most organisms and must be fixed into ammonia or nitrates by nitrogen-fixing bacteria in root nodules or soil. Nitrification converts ammonia to nitrites and nitrates, which plants absorb, while denitrification returns N2 gas back to the atmosphere.
  • The Phosphorus Cycle: A sedimentary cycle lacking an atmospheric phase. Weathering of rocks releases phosphate into soil and water, where it is absorbed by plants and enters food webs.
04

Population Ecology & Growth Models

Population ecology examines how group sizes of a single species change over time in response to environmental conditions:

  • Exponential Growth Model: Occurs in ideal environments with unlimited resources. Population size increases rapidly over time, described mathematically as dN/dt = rN (where N is population size, r is intrinsic per capita growth rate). Produces a J-shaped growth curve.
  • Logistic Growth Model: Accounts for environmental resistance and resource limitations. As population size approaches the carrying capacity (K), growth slows down, described by dN/dt = rN * ((K - N) / K). Produces an S-shaped (sigmoidal) growth curve.
  • Life History Strategies:
    • r-selected species: High reproductive rate, small body size, early maturity, minimal parental care (e.g., insects, bacteria).
    • K-selected species: Slow maturation, large body size, few offspring, extensive parental care, living near carrying capacity (e.g., elephants, humans).
05

Community Ecology & Interspecific Interactions

Communities consist of all populations of different species living close enough for potential interaction. Key interspecific interactions include:

  • Competition (-/-): Occurs when two species vie for the same limited resource. Competitive exclusion principle states that two species competing for the exact same limiting resources cannot coexist indefinitely.
  • Predation & Herbivory (+/-): One species (predator/herbivore) kills and consumes another (prey/plant), driving evolutionary adaptations like camouflage, mimicry, and defense toxins.
  • Symbiosis: Close, long-term biological interactions between two different species:
    • Mutualism (+/+): Both species benefit (e.g., mycorrhizal fungi and plant roots).
    • Commensalism (+/0): One species benefits while the other is unaffected (e.g., barnacles on whales).
    • Parasitism (+/-): Parasite derives nourishment at the host's expense.

Exhaustive Worked Exam Examples

Problem 1 (Trophic Energy Pyramids):

An ecosystem has 50,000 kg of plant biomass (primary producers). Based on the standard 10% ecological efficiency rule, calculate: (a) the biomass available to primary consumers, (b) the biomass available to secondary consumers, and (c) the biomass available to tertiary carnivores.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
(a) Primary Consumers (Herbivores):
- Receives 10% of producer biomass: 50,000 kg * 0.10 = 5,000 kg.

(b) Secondary Consumers:
- Receives 10% of primary consumer biomass: 5,000 kg * 0.10 = 500 kg.

(c) Tertiary Consumers:
- Receives 10% of secondary consumer biomass: 500 kg * 0.10 = 50 kg.

- Conclusion: Energy loss at each trophic step limits food chain length, which is why apex predators are rare in natural ecosystems.
Problem 2 (Logistic Population Growth Analysis):

A population of deer has an intrinsic growth rate (r) of 0.2 per individual per year in a forest with a carrying capacity (K) of 2,000 deer. Calculate the population growth rate (dN/dt) when the current population size (N) is 500 deer.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
- Logistic Growth Formula: dN/dt = r * N * ((K - N) / K)
- Substitute given values: r = 0.2, N = 500, K = 2,000.
- Calculate population headroom fraction: (2,000 - 500) / 2,000 = 1,500 / 2,000 = 0.75.
- Compute growth rate: dN/dt = 0.2 * 500 * 0.75 = 100 * 0.75 = 75 deer per year.
- Conclusion: The population will increase by 75 individuals in that year as it grows toward carrying capacity.
Problem 3 (Biogeochemical Nitrogen Fixation):

Explain why atmospheric nitrogen gas (N2), which makes up about 78% of Earth's atmosphere, cannot be used directly by plants and describe the biological mechanism that overcomes this limitation.

Detailed Step-by-Step Explanation:
- Chemical Inertness: Two nitrogen atoms in N2 are bound together by a strong triple covalent bond, making the molecule extremely stable and chemically unreactive to eukaryotic enzymes.
- Biological Nitrogen Fixation: Specialized prokaryotes (such as Rhizobium bacteria living in symbiotic root nodules of legumes, and free-living cyanobacteria) possess the nitrogenase enzyme complex.
- Conversion: These bacteria break the triple bond and reduce N2 gas into ammonia (NH3) and ammonium (NH4+), forms that plants can readily absorb to synthesize amino acids and nucleic acids.
