Introduction to Cellular Respiration

Cellular respiration is the set of metabolic reactions and processes that take place in the cells of organisms to convert biochemical energy from nutrients into adenosine triphosphate (ATP), and then release waste products.

The overall chemical equation for aerobic cellular respiration is: C6H12O6 + 6 O2 → 6 CO2 + 6 H2O + Energy (ATP + Heat). This process is divided into four major stages: glycolysis, pyruvate oxidation, the Krebs cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation.

Core Energetic Principle:
Cellular respiration is a controlled redox (oxidation-reduction) catabolic pathway where glucose is oxidized and oxygen is reduced, capturing free energy in the phosphate bonds of ATP.
02

Stage 1: Glycolysis (Cytoplasm)

Glycolysis is a universal metabolic pathway that occurs in the cytoplasm of all living cells, requiring zero oxygen (anaerobic).

  • Energy Investment Phase (Steps 1-5): The cell invests 2 ATP molecules to phosphorylate a 6-carbon glucose molecule, splitting it into two 3-carbon phosphorylated sugars (glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate).
  • Energy Payoff Phase (Steps 6-10): Each 3-carbon intermediate is oxidized, transferring electrons to NAD+ to form NADH, and producing 4 ATP molecules via substrate-level phosphorylation.
  • Net Yield per Glucose: 2 ATP, 2 NADH, and 2 pyruvate molecules.

03

Stages 2 & 3: Pyruvate Oxidation & The Krebs Cycle

In eukaryotic cells, if oxygen is present, pyruvates are transported across the mitochondrial double membrane into the matrix.

  • Pyruvate Oxidation (Link Reaction): Each 3-carbon pyruvate is decarboxylated (releasing CO2), oxidized to an acetyl group while reducing NAD+ to NADH, and attached to Coenzyme A to form Acetyl-CoA.
  • The Krebs (Citric Acid) Cycle: Acetyl-CoA combines with a 4-carbon oxaloacetate molecule to form 6-carbon citrate. Through an 8-step cyclical pathway, citrate is systematically oxidized back into oxaloacetate.
  • Products per Glucose (Two turns of the cycle): 4 CO2, 6 NADH, 2 FADH2, and 2 ATP (via substrate-level phosphorylation).
04

Stage 4: Oxidative Phosphorylation & Chemiosmosis

Oxidative phosphorylation accounts for the vast majority of ATP generated during aerobic respiration, consisting of two coupled processes: the Electron Transport Chain (ETC) and chemiosmosis via ATP synthase.

  • Electron Transport Chain (ETC): A series of protein complexes (I-IV) embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane. NADH and FADH2 donate high-energy electrons down the chain. As electrons pass through, complexes pump protons (H+) from the matrix into the intermembrane space.
  • Final Electron Acceptor: Oxygen (O2) acts as the terminal electron acceptor at Complex IV, combining with electrons and protons to form metabolic water (H2O).
  • Chemiosmosis & ATP Synthase: The accumulated proton gradient creates an electrochemical proton-motive force. Protons flow back into the mitochondrial matrix exclusively through the turbine-like channel of ATP synthase, driving the catalytic phosphorylation of ADP into ATP.
Proton-Motive Force:
Chemiosmosis is the process where energy stored in the form of a hydrogen ion gradient across a membrane is used to drive cellular work, specifically the synthesis of ATP.
05

Anaerobic Pathways: Fermentation

When oxygen is absent or in short supply, cells cannot run oxidative phosphorylation. To sustain glycolysis and maintain ATP production, cells rely on fermentation to regenerate NAD+ from NADH.

  • Lactic Acid Fermentation: Pyruvate is reduced directly by NADH to form lactate, regenerating NAD+. Occurs in human skeletal muscle during strenuous exercise and in certain bacteria/fungi (used to make yogurt and cheese).
  • Alcohol Fermentation: Pyruvate is converted into acetaldehyde (releasing CO2), which is then reduced by NADH to produce ethanol and regenerate NAD+. Occurs in yeast (used in baking and brewing).

Exhaustive Worked Exam Examples

Problem 1 (ATP Yield Accounting per Glucose Molecule):

Calculate the theoretical maximum net yield of ATP produced from the complete aerobic respiration of one glucose molecule, accounting for substrate-level phosphorylation and oxidative phosphorylation.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
1. Glycolysis: Net 2 ATP (substrate-level) + 2 NADH (yielding ~5 ATP via transport).
2. Pyruvate Oxidation: 2 NADH yielded (yielding ~5 ATP).
3. Krebs Cycle: 2 ATP (substrate-level) + 6 NADH (~15 ATP) + 2 FADH2 (~3 ATP).
- Total Net Yield: Approximately 30 to 32 ATP molecules generated per single glucose molecule oxidized completely to carbon dioxide and water.
Problem 2 (Role of Oxygen in the ETC):

Explain what happens to the electron transport chain and ATP synthesis if a poison blocks the final enzyme complex (Complex IV) or removes oxygen from the cellular environment.

Detailed Step-by-Step Explanation:
- Halt of Electron Flow: Without oxygen to accept electrons at the end of Complex IV, electrons become backed up along the entire electron transport chain.
- Loss of Proton Gradient: Because electrons stop moving, proton pumping across the inner mitochondrial membrane ceases, causing the proton-motive force to dissipate.
- Failure of ATP Synthase: ATP synthase cannot generate ATP without the proton gradient, leading to rapid cellular energy failure and cell death.
Problem 3 (Comparing Aerobic Respiration vs. Fermentation):

Compare the net ATP yield per glucose molecule between aerobic cellular respiration and anaerobic fermentation. Why is fermentation necessary for cells in the absence of oxygen?

Detailed Step-by-Step Explanation:
- Energy Efficiency: Aerobic respiration yields roughly 30-32 ATP per glucose, whereas anaerobic fermentation yields only 2 ATP (from glycolysis alone).
- Regeneration of NAD+: Glycolysis requires a continuous supply of NAD+ to accept electrons. In the absence of oxygen, the electron transport chain cannot oxidize NADH back to NAD+. Fermentation provides an alternative pathway to oxidize NADH back to NAD+, allowing glycolysis to continue producing vital emergency ATP.
