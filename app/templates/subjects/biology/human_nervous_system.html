Introduction to Neural Coordination

The nervous system provides rapid, electrochemical communication throughout the body, coordinating sensory inputs, central processing, and motor responses.

Core Physiological Principle:
Neural signaling relies on maintaining and rapidly reversing electrochemical concentration gradients across excitable neuron cell membranes.
02

Neuron Anatomy & Resting Potential

  • Dendrites: Branching extensions that receive chemical signals from other neurons.
  • Axon: Long nerve fiber that transmits electrical impulses away from the cell body.
  • Myelin Sheath: Insulating lipid layer formed by Schwann cells that dramatically increases impulse conduction speed via saltatory conduction.
  • Resting Potential: Typically -70 mV, maintained by the sodium-potassium pump (active transport of 3 Na+ out for every 2 K+ in).
03

Action Potentials & Depolarization

  • Depolarization: Voltage-gated sodium channels open, causing Na+ influx and a rapid spike in membrane potential.
  • Repolarization: Sodium channels close and potassium channels open, allowing K+ efflux to restore negative internal polarity.
  • Hyperpolarization: K+ channels close slowly, causing a temporary dip below resting potential before returning to baseline.
04

Synaptic Neurotransmission

When an action potential reaches the axon terminal, voltage-gated calcium channels trigger vesicle fusion and neurotransmitter release into the synaptic cleft.

Exhaustive Worked Exam Examples

Problem 1 (Saltatory Conduction):

Explain why myelinated axons transmit nerve impulses significantly faster than unmyelinated axons.

Detailed Step-by-Step Explanation:
- Myelin acts as an electrical insulator around the axon.
- Action potentials can only occur at uninsulated gaps called nodes of Ranvier.
- The impulse effectively "jumps" from node to node (saltatory conduction), greatly reducing the time required for ion exchange along the axon length.
