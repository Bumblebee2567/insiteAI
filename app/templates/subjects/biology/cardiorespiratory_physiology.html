Introduction to Cardiorespiratory Systems

The cardiovascular and respiratory systems function as an integrated super-system responsible for bulk transport of oxygen, carbon dioxide, nutrients, and metabolic wastes throughout the human body.

While the heart and blood vessels generate pressure gradients to circulate blood, the lungs provide a massive surface area for rapid gaseous diffusion across alveolar-capillary membranes.

Core Physiological Principle:
Bulk flow moves blood through the circulatory circuit, whereas respiratory gas exchange across alveolar tissues relies entirely on passive diffusion driven by partial pressure gradients.
02

Cardiac Cycle Mechanics & Electrical Conduction

The cardiac cycle describes the sequence of pressure and volume changes occurring in the heart during one complete heartbeat. Electrical impulses originating in the heart coordinate synchronous atrial and ventricular contractions:

  • Sinoatrial (SA) Node: The natural pacemaker located in the right atrium that spontaneously depolarizes to initiate electrical impulses.
  • Atrioventricular (AV) Node & Bundle of His: Delays the impulse slightly to allow complete atrial emptying before ventricular contraction, relaying signals down the bundle branches and Purkinje fibers.
  • Systole & Diastole: Ventricular systole refers to contraction and blood ejection; ventricular diastole refers to relaxation and ventricular filling.

03

Blood Vessel Hemodynamics

Blood flow dynamics through the vascular tree depend on vessel structure, pressure gradients, and peripheral resistance:

  • Arteries & Arterioles: Thick, muscular, and elastic walls designed to withstand high pulsatile pressures and regulate systemic blood pressure via vasoconstriction and vasodilation.
  • Capillaries: Extremely thin endothelial walls (one cell thick) that maximize the surface area for rapid diffusion of gases, nutrients, and waste products with interstitial fluids.
  • Veins & Venules: Low-pressure conduits possessing one-way valves that prevent backflow, aided by skeletal muscle contractions returning blood to the heart.
04

Alveolar Gas Exchange & Ventilation Mechanics

Ventilation ensures continuous renewal of air within the alveoli, maintaining steep partial pressure gradients for oxygen and carbon dioxide:

  • Inhalation: The diaphragm and external intercostal muscles contract, expanding thoracic cavity volume, lowering intra-alveolar pressure below atmospheric pressure, and drawing air inward.
  • Alveolar Diffusion: Oxygen diffuses down its partial pressure gradient from alveoli (high PO2) into pulmonary capillary blood (low PO2). Simultaneously, carbon dioxide diffuses from capillary blood into alveoli to be exhaled.
  • Surfactant Production: Specialized alveolar cells secrete pulmonary surfactant, reducing surface tension and preventing small alveoli from collapsing.
05

Oxygen Transport & The Bohr Effect

Because oxygen has low solubility in blood plasma, over 98% of oxygen is transported bound to hemoglobin inside red blood cells:

  • Hemoglobin Cooperativity: A tetrameric protein containing four iron-bound heme groups that binds oxygen cooperatively—binding one O2 molecule increases affinity for subsequent oxygen molecules.
  • The Bohr Effect: In active skeletal tissues, higher temperature, higher carbon dioxide concentrations, and lower pH (acidosis) reduce hemoglobin's affinity for oxygen, causing it to unload O2 efficiently where metabolic demand is highest.
Key Physiological Adaptation:
The sigmoidal shape of the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve reflects cooperative binding, ensuring maximal oxygen loading in the lungs and efficient unloading in metabolically active tissues.

Exhaustive Worked Exam Examples

Problem 1 (Cardiac Output Calculation):

Calculate the cardiac output (CO) of an individual whose heart rate (HR) is 75 beats per minute and whose stroke volume (SV) is 70 mL per beat. Express your answer in liters per minute.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
- Formula: Cardiac Output (CO) = Heart Rate (HR) × Stroke Volume (SV)
- Substitution: CO = 75 beats/min × 70 mL/beat = 5,250 mL/min.
- Conversion to Liters: 5,250 mL/min ÷ 1,000 = 5.25 L/min.
- Conclusion: The heart pumps approximately 5.25 liters of blood per minute at rest.
Problem 2 (Hemoglobin Oxygen Unloading & The Bohr Effect):

Explain how changes in local tissue pH and carbon dioxide concentration facilitate enhanced oxygen delivery to exercising skeletal muscles.

Detailed Step-by-Step Explanation:
- Metabolic Demand: Exercising muscle cells consume oxygen rapidly and produce large amounts of carbon dioxide and lactic acid, lowering tissue pH.
- The Bohr Effect: Increased hydrogen ions (lower pH) and elevated carbon dioxide bind to allosteric sites on hemoglobin, altering its conformation.
- Result: Hemoglobin's affinity for oxygen decreases, shifting the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve to the right and forcing hemoglobin to release its stored oxygen precisely where metabolic demand is greatest.
Problem 3 (Alveolar Partial Pressure Gradients):

Describe how partial pressure gradients govern the diffusion of oxygen and carbon dioxide across the alveolar-capillary membrane during external respiration.

Detailed Step-by-Step Explanation:
- Oxygen Gradient: Inhaled alveolar air has a high oxygen partial pressure (PO2 approx. 104 mmHg), whereas deoxygenated blood entering pulmonary capillaries has a low PO2 (approx. 40 mmHg). Oxygen diffuses rapidly down this steep gradient into the blood until equilibrium is reached.
- Carbon Dioxide Gradient: Deoxygenated blood has a high PCO2 (approx. 45 mmHg) compared to alveolar air (approx. 40 mmHg). Carbon dioxide diffuses down its gradient out of the blood into the alveoli to be eliminated through exhalation.
