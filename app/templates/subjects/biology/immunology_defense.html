Introduction to Classical Genetics

Classical genetics examines how heritable traits are transmitted across generations through discrete units of inheritance known as genes. Building upon Gregor Mendel's foundational pea plant hybridization experiments, modern genetics correlates inheritance patterns with the behavior of homologous chromosomes during meiosis.

Key terminology includes genotype (the specific genetic allele makeup) and phenotype (the observable physical or physiological expression of those genes).

Core Genetic Principle:
Alleles are alternative forms of a gene that account for variations in inherited characters, segregating independently during gamete formation.
02

Mendel's Laws of Heredity

Gregor Mendel established two fundamental principles of inheritance through statistical analysis of garden pea crosses:

  • Law of Segregation (First Law): Allele pairs separate during gamete formation (anaphase I/II of meiosis), so that each gamete carries only one allele for each gene.
  • Law of Independent Assortment (Second Law): Alleles of two or more different genes sort into gametes independently of one another, provided the genes are located on different non-homologous chromosomes or far apart on the same chromosome.

Monohybrid & Dihybrid Cross Calculations

Punnett squares are predictive grid diagrams used to determine the probability of offspring inheriting specific genotypes and phenotypes:

  • Monohybrid Crosses: Track the inheritance of a single gene locus. For two heterozygous carrier parents (Aa × Aa), the probability of an affected homozygous recessive offspring (aa) is 25%.
  • Dihybrid Crosses: Track two independent gene loci simultaneously (e.g., seed color and seed shape). Using the product rule, the probability of obtaining a double homozygous recessive offspring (yyrr) from heterozygous parents (YyRr × YyRr) is (1/4) × (1/4) = 1/16.
04

Pedigree Analysis in Human Genetics

A pedigree chart maps the inheritance of a particular trait across multiple generations of a family tree to determine whether the underlying gene is autosomal or sex-linked, dominant or recessive.

  • Autosomal Recessive: Typically skips generations; affected individuals can have unaffected carrier parents. Equal frequency in males and females.
  • Autosomal Dominant: Does not skip generations; every affected individual has at least one affected parent.
05

Sex-Linked Inheritance Patterns

Genes located on the sex chromosomes (X and Y) exhibit unique inheritance patterns due to unequal chromosome distribution between sexes:

  • X-Linked Recessive Traits: Because human males possess only one X chromosome (XY), a single recessive allele on the X chromosome is sufficient to express the trait (e.g., red-green color blindness, hemophilia, muscular dystrophy).
  • Maternal Transmission: Affected fathers pass their X chromosome to all of their daughters but none of their sons, making X-linked recessive disorders appear much more frequently in males.
Key Genetic Distinction:
Autosomal traits affect males and females equally, whereas X-linked recessive traits show marked male bias because males lack a second protective X chromosome.

Exhaustive Worked Exam Examples

Problem 1 (Dihybrid Cross Probability Calculation):

In pea plants, yellow seed color (Y) is dominant over green (y), and round seed shape (R) is dominant over wrinkled (r). If two plants heterozygous for both traits (YyRr) are crossed, calculate the probability of producing an offspring that is homozygous recessive for both traits (yyrr).

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
- Method 1: Probability Rule:
  • Probability of getting yy from Yy × Yy = 1/4.
  • Probability of getting rr from Rr × Rr = 1/4.
  • Multiply independent probabilities: (1/4) × (1/4) = 1/16.
- Conclusion: 1 out of 16 offspring in a standard dihybrid cross will display the homozygous recessive double trait (yyrr).
Problem 2 (X-Linked Recessive Inheritance):

A color-blind man (X^h Y) marries a woman who is a carrier for red-green color blindness (X^H X^h). Determine the expected phenotypic ratios among their sons and daughters.

Detailed Step-by-Step Explanation:
- Cross: X^h Y × X^H X^h
- Daughters:   • 50% chance of being carrier females (X^H X^h - normal vision).
  • 50% chance of being affected females (X^h X^h - color blind).
- Sons:   • 50% chance of having normal vision (X^H Y).
  • 50% chance of being color blind (X^h Y).
- Conclusion: Sons inherit their single X chromosome exclusively from their mother, explaining why X-linked disorders affect males more frequently.
Problem 3 (Codominance in ABO Blood Groups):

A man with blood type AB and a woman with blood type O have a child. What are the possible blood types of their offspring, and what genetic principle does this illustrate?

Detailed Step-by-Step Explanation:
- Genotypes: Man is I^A I^B; Woman is i i.
- Punnett Square Cross: Gametes from man are I^A and I^B; gametes from woman are all i.
- Offspring Genotypes: 50% I^A i (Blood type A) and 50% I^B i (Blood type B).
- Genetic Principle: This illustrates both codominance (when I^A and I^B are inherited together) and multiple alleles in the human population.
