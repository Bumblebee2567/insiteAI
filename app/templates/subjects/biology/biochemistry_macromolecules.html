Introduction to Biochemistry

Biochemistry explores the chemical substances and vital metabolic processes occurring within living organisms. At the heart of biochemistry are four major classes of organic macromolecules: carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids.

Almost all macromolecules are polymers constructed from repeating monomer subunits joined together through dehydration synthesis (condensation) reactions, and broken down into monomers via hydrolysis reactions.

Core Biochemical Principle:
Carbon's unique tetravalent bonding capacity allows it to form stable, complex branched and ringed carbon skeletons that serve as the backbone for all organic biomolecules.
02

Chemical Properties of Water

Water (H2O) is the biological medium on Earth, vital for all known life due to its unique chemical characteristics:

  • Polar Covalent Bonding: Oxygen attracts electrons more strongly than hydrogen, creating partial negative charges near oxygen and partial positive charges near hydrogen.
  • Hydrogen Bonding: The attraction between the partial positive charge of hydrogen and partial negative charges on neighboring molecules creates a cohesive network.
  • High Specific Heat Capacity: Water resists temperature fluctuations, stabilizing thermal conditions inside cells and aquatic biomes.
  • Universal Solvent: Water's polarity allows it to dissolve ionic compounds and polar molecules (hydrophilic), while repelling nonpolar molecules (hydrophobic).
03

Carbohydrates: Sugars and Polysaccharides

Carbohydrates include sugars and polymers of sugars, serving as primary energy sources and structural building blocks:

  • Monosaccharides: Simple sugars such as glucose, fructose, and galactose (formula C6H12O6), serving as immediate cellular fuel.
  • Disaccharides: Formed when two monosaccharides join via a glycosidic linkage through dehydration synthesis (e.g., sucrose, lactose, maltose).
  • Polysaccharides: Complex carbohydrate polymers:
    • Energy Storage: Starch in plants and glycogen in animals.
    • Structural Support: Cellulose in plant cell walls and chitin in arthropod exoskeletons and fungal walls.

04

Lipids: Fats, Phospholipids, and Steroids

Lipids are a diverse group of hydrophobic organic compounds that do not form true polymers:

  • Triglycerides (Fats): Composed of one glycerol molecule attached to three fatty acid tails via ester linkages.
    • Saturated Fats: Contain no double bonds between carbon atoms; solid at room temperature (e.g., animal fats).
    • Unsaturated Fats: Contain one or more double bonds causing kinks; liquid at room temperature (e.g., plant oils).
  • Phospholipids: Consist of a glycerol bound to two fatty acids and a phosphate group, forming the amphipathic foundation of cell membranes.
  • Steroids: Lipids characterized by a carbon skeleton consisting of four fused rings (e.g., cholesterol, testosterone, estrogen).
05

Proteins: Structure and Hierarchy

Proteins are polymers of amino acids linked by peptide bonds, executing nearly all cellular tasks:

  • Primary Structure: The unique linear sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide chain.
  • Secondary Structure: Local folding into alpha-helices and beta-pleated sheets stabilized by hydrogen bonds along the backbone.
  • Tertiary Structure: The overall three-dimensional conformation shaped by interactions among side chains (R-groups), including ionic bonds, hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and disulfide bridges.
  • Quaternary Structure: The association of multiple polypeptide subunits into a functional protein complex (e.g., hemoglobin).

Exhaustive Worked Exam Examples

Problem 1 (Dehydration Synthesis & Polymerization):

If a polypeptide chain is synthesized by joining 125 individual amino acid monomers together, calculate: (a) the number of peptide bonds formed, and (b) the number of water molecules released during this polymerization process.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
(a) Peptide Bonds Formed:
- To connect n monomers into a linear polymer, exactly n - 1 bonds are required.
- For 125 amino acids, number of peptide bonds = 125 - 1 = 124 peptide bonds.

(b) Water Molecules Released:
- Each time a peptide bond is formed via dehydration synthesis, one molecule of water (H2O) is removed.
- Therefore, releasing 1 water molecule per bond yields 124 water molecules.
Problem 2 (Protein Denaturation Analysis):

An enzyme is exposed to extreme heat and a high-pH chemical solution, causing it to lose its catalytic activity. Explain which levels of protein structure are disrupted and which level remains intact.

Detailed Step-by-Step Explanation:
- Disrupted Levels: Extreme heat and pH break hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, and disulfide bridges. This disrupts the secondary, tertiary, and quaternary structures, causing the protein to unfold (denature) and lose its functional shape.
- Intact Level: The covalent peptide bonds holding the linear amino acid sequence together are remarkably strong and typically remain unbroken during denaturation. Therefore, the primary structure remains completely intact.
Problem 3 (Saturated vs. Unsaturated Fats):

Compare the chemical structure and physical room-temperature state of saturated fatty acids versus unsaturated fatty acids.

Detailed Step-by-Step Explanation:
- Saturated Fatty Acids: Contain only single covalent bonds between carbon atoms, meaning they are "saturated" with the maximum possible number of hydrogen atoms. Their straight hydrocarbon tails pack closely together, making them solid at room temperature (e.g., butter, lard).
- Unsaturated Fatty Acids: Contain one or more double bonds between carbon atoms, creating rigid kinks or bends in the hydrocarbon chain. These kinks prevent tight packing, making them liquid at room temperature (e.g., olive oil, vegetable oil).
