Introduction to Molecular Genetics

Molecular genetics explores the chemical structure and physiological function of genes at a molecular level, investigating how hereditary information is stored, replicated, expressed, and transmitted across generations.

By decoding the nucleotide sequences of genomes, scientists can understand how proteins are synthesized to build cellular machinery and regulate every metabolic process in living organisms.

Core Molecular Principle:
The informational continuity of life relies on nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) storing precise sequences of nitrogenous bases that dictate the primary amino acid sequence of functional proteins.
02

The Structure of DNA and RNA

Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) is a polymer composed of repeating nucleotide subunits. Its iconic double helix model was elucidated by James Watson and Francis Crick, building on Rosalind Franklin's critical X-ray diffraction data.

  • Nucleotide Components: Each nucleotide consists of a 5-carbon pentose sugar (deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA), a negatively charged phosphate group, and a nitrogenous base.
  • Nitrogenous Bases: Purines (Adenine [A] and Guanine [G]) pair exclusively with pyrimidines (Thymine [T] and Cytosine [C]) via hydrogen bonding: A pairs with T (2 hydrogen bonds), and C pairs with G (3 hydrogen bonds).
  • Antiparallel Strands: The two polynucleotide strands run in opposite directions—one oriented $5'$ to $3'$ and the complementary strand $3'$ to $5'$—which is critical for enzyme machinery during replication and transcription.
Feature DNA (Deoxyribonucleic Acid) RNA (Ribonucleic Acid)
Sugar Deoxyribose (lacks one oxygen atom at 2' carbon) Ribose (has a hydroxyl group at 2' carbon)

03

Semi-Conservative DNA Replication

Prior to cellular division, a cell must duplicate its entire genome so that daughter cells receive identical genetic blueprints. Replication proceeds via a semi-conservative model, where each new double helix retains one original template strand and one newly synthesized strand.

  • Initiation & Helicase: The enzyme DNA helicase unwinds and unzips the double helix by breaking hydrogen bonds, creating a replication fork. Single-strand binding proteins stabilize the exposed templates.
  • Primer Synthesis: RNA primase synthesizes a short RNA primer to provide a free $3'$-OH group required by DNA polymerase.
  • Elongation: DNA polymerase III adds complementary free deoxynucleoside triphosphates (dNTPs) in the $5'$ to $3'$ direction.
    • Leading Strand: Synthesized continuously toward the replication fork.
    • Lagging Strand: Synthesized discontinuously away from the replication fork in short segments called Okazaki fragments.
  • Ligation: DNA ligase seals nicked phosphodiester backbones, joining Okazaki fragments into a continuous strand.
04

The Central Dogma of Molecular Biology

Formulated by Francis Crick, the Central Dogma describes the directional flow of genetic information within a biological system: DNA → RNA → Protein.

  • Step 1: Transcription: Occurs in the nucleus. RNA polymerase binds to a promoter region on DNA, unwinds the duplex, and synthesizes a complementary single-stranded messenger RNA (mRNA) transcript using the template strand.
  • Step 2: mRNA Processing (Eukaryotes): Pre-mRNA undergoes capping (adding a $5'$ methyl-G cap), polyadenylation (adding a $3'$ poly-A tail), and splicing, where non-coding introns are excised and coding exons are joined together.
  • Step 3: Translation: Occurs at ribosomes in the cytoplasm. Transfer RNA (tRNA) molecules carry specific amino acids to the ribosome, matching their anticodon to mRNA codons to assemble a polypeptide chain.
05

Gene Mutations & Phenotypic Effects

A mutation is any permanent, heritable change in the nucleotide sequence of an organism's DNA. Mutations can arise spontaneously during replication errors or be induced by mutagenic agents (ionizing radiation, UV light, chemical mutagens).

  • Point Mutations (Base Substitutions): A single nucleotide base is swapped for another.
    • Silent Mutation: The altered codon codes for the exact same amino acid due to genetic code degeneracy (no phenotypic effect).
    • Missense Mutation: The codon codes for a different amino acid, which may alter protein folding and function.
    • Nonsense Mutation: The substitution generates a premature stop codon, resulting in a truncated, non-functional protein.
  • Frameshift Mutations: The insertion or deletion of nucleotides not in multiples of three, shifting the reading frame and drastically altering every downstream amino acid in the polypeptide sequence.

Exhaustive Worked Exam Examples

Problem 1 (Transcription & Translation Sequence Analysis):

Given the non-template (coding) DNA strand: 5'-ATG GCT CAA TGA-3', determine: (a) the corresponding template DNA strand, (b) the resulting mRNA transcript sequence, and (c) the exact amino acid sequence produced (using a standard genetic code table).

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
(a) Template DNA Strand: Complementary and antiparallel to the coding strand: 3'-TAC CGA GTT ACT-5'.
(b) mRNA Transcript: Synthesized complementary to the template strand in the $5'$ to $3'$ direction (matches coding strand with U replacing T): 5'-AUG GCU CAA UGA-3'.
(c) Amino Acid Sequence: Translate codons:
  • AUG → Methionine (Start)
  • GCU → Alanine
  • CAA → Glutamine
  • UGA → Stop Codon
Final Polypeptide: Met - Ala - Gln (terminating at UGA).
Problem 2 (Chargaff's Base Composition Rules):

A double-stranded DNA molecule is analyzed and found to contain 28% Adenine bases. Calculate the exact percentages of Thymine, Cytosine, and Guanine present in this DNA sample.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
- According to Chargaff's Rules, in double-stranded DNA, the amount of Adenine equals Thymine ([A] = [T]) and the amount of Cytosine equals Guanine ([C] = [G]).
- Since [A] = 28%, then [T] = 28%.
- Combined Adenine and Thymine percentage = 28% + 28% = 56%.
- The remaining percentage for Cytosine and Guanine is 100% - 56% = 44%.
- Dividing equally between C and G: [C] = 44% / 2 = 22% and [G] = 22%.
- Summary: Adenine = 28%, Thymine = 28%, Cytosine = 22%, Guanine = 22%.
Problem 3 (Frameshift Mutation Consequences):

An mRNA sequence reads 5'-AUG GUA CCG UAA-3'. If a single deletion mutation removes the first nucleotide 'G' of the second codon ('GUA'), explain the resulting change in the reading frame and amino acid sequence.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
- Original Sequence & Translation:
  AUG (Met) • GUA (Val) • CCG (Pro) • UAA (Stop).
- Mutated Sequence (Deletion of 'G'):
  Removing 'G' shifts the downstream reading frame by one nucleotide: 5'-AUG UAC CGU AA...-3'.
- New Codons & Translation:
  1st codon: AUG → Methionine
  2nd codon: UAC → Tyrosine
  3rd codon: CGU → Arginine
- Biological Consequence: This is a frameshift mutation. It alters every amino acid following the mutation site and can disrupt the normal stop codon placement, drastically modifying protein structure and function.
