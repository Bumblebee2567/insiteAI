Introduction to Evolutionary Biology

Evolution is the foundational unifying principle of all biological sciences. It describes the process by which populations of organisms change over successive generations through genetic variations, natural selection, and environmental adaptation.

While microevolution examines changes in allele frequencies within a single population over a short time scale, macroevolution investigates large-scale evolutionary patterns and the emergence of entirely new species over geological time.

Core Evolutionary Principle:
Individuals do not evolve; populations evolve. Evolution operates on the heritable genetic traits within a gene pool over generations in response to ecological pressures.
02

Darwin, Wallace, and Natural Selection

Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace independently formulated the theory of evolution by natural selection after extensive biogeographical expeditions across the globe.

  • Observation 1: Populations tend to exhibit exponential reproductive potential, yet population sizes typically remain relatively stable over time due to limited environmental resources.
  • Observation 2: Organisms within any species display heritable phenotypic variations in traits such as coloration, size, and metabolic efficiency.
  • Inference 1 (Differential Survival & Reproduction): Individuals possessing heritable traits better suited to their local environment (adaptations) are more likely to survive and reproduce ("survival of the fittest").
  • Inference 2 (Adaptation): Over generations, advantageous alleles become more common within the population gene pool, driving adaptive evolution.
03

Mechanisms of Microevolution

Microevolution is driven by four primary forces that alter allele and genotype frequencies within a population gene pool:

  • Natural Selection: The only mechanism that consistently leads to adaptive evolution by favoring beneficial traits.
  • Genetic Drift: Random fluctuations in allele frequencies due to chance events, having a particularly drastic effect in small populations. Includes the bottleneck effect (sudden environmental reduction in population size) and the founder effect (a few individuals establish a new isolated colony).
  • Gene Flow: The transfer of alleles into or out of a population due to the migration of fertile individuals or gametes, reducing genetic differences between populations.
  • Mutation: The ultimate source of all genetic novelty, introducing entirely new alleles into a population's gene pool.
04

Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium

The Hardy-Weinberg principle states that allele and genotype frequencies in a population will remain constant from generation to generation in the absence of evolutionary influences (acting as a mathematical null hypothesis).

  • Allele Frequency Equation: p + q = 1 (where p is the frequency of the dominant allele and q is the recessive allele).
  • Genotype Frequency Equation: p² + 2pq + q² = 1
    • p² = frequency of homozygous dominant genotype (AA)
    • 2pq = frequency of heterozygous genotype (Aa)
    • q² = frequency of homozygous recessive genotype (aa)
  • Five Conditions for Equilibrium: 1) No mutations, 2) Random mating, 3) No natural selection, 4) Extremely large population size (no genetic drift), 5) No gene flow (migration).
05

Speciation & Macroevolutionary Patterns

Speciation is the evolutionary process where ancestral populations split into two or more distinct, reproductively isolated species.

  • Allopatric Speciation: Occurs when a population is divided by a physical geographic barrier (e.g., mountain range, river), leading to reproductive isolation and independent evolutionary divergence.
  • Sympatric Speciation: Occurs without geographic separation, often through polyploidy (common in plants), habitat differentiation, or sexual selection within the same geographic area.
  • Reproductive Isolation Barriers:
    • Prezygotic Barriers: Prevent mating or fertilization (habitat, temporal, behavioral, mechanical, or gametic isolation).
    • Postzygotic Barriers: Prevent hybrid zygotes from developing into viable, fertile offspring (reduced hybrid viability, hybrid sterility, or hybrid breakdown).

Exhaustive Worked Exam Examples

Problem 1 (Hardy-Weinberg Population Calculation):

In a population of 1,000 mice, 360 exhibit a white coat color (recessive trait, aa). Assuming the population is in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, calculate: (a) the frequency of the recessive allele (q) and dominant allele (p), and (b) the number of heterozygous mice in the population.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
(a) Find Allele Frequencies (q and p):
- Frequency of homozygous recessive phenotype (q²) = 360 / 1,000 = 0.36.
- Frequency of recessive allele (q) = square root of 0.36 = 0.60.
- Since p + q = 1, the frequency of the dominant allele (p) = 1 - 0.60 = 0.40.

(b) Calculate Number of Heterozygotes (2pq):
- Frequency of heterozygotes (2pq) = 2 × 0.40 × 0.60 = 0.48.
- Number of heterozygous mice = 0.48 × 1,000 = 480 mice.
Problem 2 (Genetic Drift vs. Natural Selection):

Compare and contrast the evolutionary effects of genetic drift and natural selection on a small population of endangered wildflowers following a catastrophic wildfire.

Detailed Step-by-Step Explanation:
- The Bottleneck Effect (Genetic Drift): The wildfire drastically reduces population size at random, killing plants regardless of their genetic traits. Surviving allele frequencies may differ drastically purely by chance.
- Loss of Genetic Diversity: Rare alleles may be entirely lost from the gene pool, increasing vulnerability to inbreeding depression.
- Natural Selection: Unlike drift, natural selection is non-random. If certain surviving plants possess traits that help them tolerate ash-rich soil or rapid post-fire germination, those beneficial alleles will be favored and increase in frequency over subsequent generations.
Problem 3 (Allopatric vs. Sympatric Speciation):

A population of freshwater snails is split when a tectonic shift creates a land barrier dividing a once-continuous lake into two isolated ponds. Over 50,000 years, the two populations accumulate genetic differences. Explain whether this represents allopatric or sympatric speciation and identify the likely isolating mechanisms.

Detailed Step-by-Step Explanation:
- Type of Speciation: This is a textbook example of allopatric speciation because a physical geographic barrier separated the original population into isolated sub-populations.
- Divergence Mechanism: Independent mutations, genetic drift, and differing selection pressures in each pond lead to genetic divergence.
- Reproductive Isolation: If the land barrier were eventually removed, prezygotic barriers (such as behavioral mating differences) or postzygotic barriers (such as hybrid sterility) would prevent successful interbreeding, confirming that two distinct species have formed.
