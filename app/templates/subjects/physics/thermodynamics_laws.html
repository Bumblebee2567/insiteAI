Introduction to Thermodynamics

Thermodynamics is the branch of physics that studies the relationships between heat, work, temperature, and energy. While mechanics focuses on forces and motion, thermodynamics governs the macroscopic transformations of energy, explaining why perpetual motion machines are impossible and why natural physical processes have a definite arrow of time.

Core Conceptual Principle:
Thermodynamic systems are defined by macroscopic state variables (Pressure, Volume, Temperature, and Internal Energy) that depend only on the current state of the system, independent of the path taken to reach that state.
02

The Zeroth and First Laws of Thermodynamics

The foundations of thermal equilibrium and energy conservation are established by the first two foundational principles:

  • Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics: Establishes temperature as a fundamental measurable property. If two bodies are each in thermal equilibrium with a third body, they are in thermal equilibrium with each other.
  • First Law of Thermodynamics (Conservation of Energy): The change in internal energy of a closed thermodynamic system is equal to the heat added to the system minus the work done by the system:
    Change in Internal Energy = Heat Added - Work Done (Delta U = Q - W).
  • Sign Conventions:
    • Q is positive (+) if heat is added to the system, negative (-) if heat leaves.
    • W is positive (+) if work is done by the system (expansion), negative (-) if work is done on the system (compression).
03

Thermodynamic Processes & Work

Work in thermodynamics is performed when a gas expands or compresses against an external pressure. On a Pressure-Volume (P-V) diagram, the work done during any thermodynamic process is equal to the area under the process curve.

Process Type Constant Variable Work Done (W) First Law Application
Isothermal Constant Temperature (Delta T = 0) W = n * R * T * ln(Vf / Vi) Delta U = 0, so Heat Added = Work Done (Q = W)
Adiabatic No Heat Transfer (Q = 0) W = - Delta U = - (n * Cv * Delta T) No heat exchange, so Delta U = -W
Isobaric Constant Pressure (Delta P = 0) W = Pressure × Change in Volume (P × Delta V) Heat changes both internal energy and does work: Q = Delta U + P × Delta V
Isochoric (Isovolumetric) Constant Volume (Delta V = 0) W = 0 (No volume change) No work done, so Heat Added = Change in Internal Energy (Q = Delta U)
04

The Second Law of Thermodynamics & Entropy

While the First Law permits any energy conversion as long as total energy is conserved, the Second Law of Thermodynamics dictates the natural direction of physical processes and establishes that certain energy transfers are strictly irreversible.

  • Kelvin-Planck Statement: It is impossible to construct a heat engine operating in a cycle that extracts heat from a single thermal reservoir and converts it completely into mechanical work without rejecting some waste heat to a colder reservoir.
  • Clausius Statement: Heat cannot spontaneously flow from a colder body to a warmer body without the input of external work (this is how refrigerators and heat pumps operate).
  • Entropy (S): Entropy is a quantitative measure of microscopic disorder or the number of available microstates in a system. The Second Law states that the total entropy of an isolated system always increases or remains constant for reversible processes:
    Change in Entropy = Heat Exchanged / Absolute Temperature (Delta S greater than or equal to 0 for isolated systems).
05

Heat Engines and Refrigerators

A heat engine is a cyclic device that absorbs thermal energy from a high-temperature reservoir, converts a portion of it into useful mechanical work, and exhausts the remaining waste heat into a low-temperature sink.

  • Energy Conservation in an Engine: Heat Input from Hot Reservoir = Useful Work Output + Waste Heat Exhausted (QH = W + QC).
  • Thermal Efficiency (eta): The ratio of net useful work output to the total heat input:
    Efficiency = Useful Work Output / Heat Input = 1 - (Waste Heat / Heat Input) (eta = W / QH = 1 - (QC / QH)).
  • Refrigerators & Heat Pumps: Devices that consume mechanical work to transfer heat from a cold reservoir to a hot reservoir. Their performance is measured by the Coefficient of Performance (COP) rather than efficiency:
    COPrefrig = Heat Removed from Cold Reservoir / Work Input (COP = QC / W).
06

The Carnot Cycle & Maximum Efficiency

Sadi Carnot established that the most efficient possible heat engine operating between two constant-temperature thermal reservoirs is an idealized reversible engine known as a Carnot Engine, consisting of two isothermal and two adiabatic processes.

Carnot Efficiency Formula:
Maximum Efficiency = 1 - (Temperature of Cold Reservoir / Temperature of Hot Reservoir)
(etamax = 1 - (TC / TH), where temperatures must be measured strictly in Kelvin).
No real engine operating between two temperatures can ever exceed the Carnot efficiency limit.
07

The Third Law of Thermodynamics

The Third Law of Thermodynamics addresses the behavior of systems as temperature approaches absolute zero (T = 0 K).

  • Statement: As the temperature of a pure crystalline substance approaches absolute zero, its entropy approaches a constant minimum value (typically zero). In mathematical terms: as T approaches 0 K, Delta S approaches 0.
  • Physical Implication: It is fundamentally impossible for any experimental procedure or thermodynamic process to cool a system to absolute zero in a finite number of steps.

Exhaustive Worked Exam Examples

Problem 1 (First Law of Thermodynamics):

During a thermodynamic expansion process, a gas absorbs 500 Joules of heat from its surroundings while performing 200 Joules of mechanical work on a piston. Calculate the exact change in internal energy (Delta U) of the gas system.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
- First Law of Thermodynamics: Delta U = Q - W.
- Heat added Q = +500 J (positive because heat enters the system).
- Work done by system W = +200 J (positive because gas expands).
- Substitute values: Delta U = 500 J - 200 J = +300 Joules.
- Conclusion: The internal energy of the gas increases by 300 Joules.
Problem 2 (Heat Engine Efficiency and Work Output):

A coal-fired power plant heat engine absorbs 1.20 × 109 Joules of heat from its high-temperature boiler furnace each hour and exhausts 8.00 × 108 Joules of waste heat into a nearby river. Calculate: (a) the net mechanical work output per hour, and (b) the actual thermal efficiency of the power plant.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
(a) Net Work Output (W):
- Conservation of Energy: QH = W + QC implies W = QH - QC.
- W = (1.20 × 109 J) - (8.00 × 108 J) = 1,200,000,000 - 800,000,000 = 4.00 × 108 Joules (400 MJ).

(b) Thermal Efficiency (eta):
- Efficiency = W / QH = (4.00 × 108 J) / (1.20 × 109 J) = 4 / 12 = 1 / 3 approximately equal to 33.3 percent (0.333).
Problem 3 (Carnot Efficiency Limit):

An engineer designs a futuristic heat engine to operate between a high-temperature geothermal source at 800 degrees Celsius and an ambient atmospheric sink at 20.0 degrees Celsius. Calculate the maximum theoretical efficiency this engine can achieve.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
- Convert Celsius temperatures to Kelvin:
  • TH = 800 degrees Celsius + 273.15 = 1073.15 K.
  • TC = 20.0 degrees Celsius + 273.15 = 293.15 K.
- Carnot Efficiency formula: Efficiencymax = 1 - (TC / TH).
- Efficiencymax = 1 - (293.15 / 1073.15) = 1 - 0.2732 = 0.727 or 72.7 percent.
