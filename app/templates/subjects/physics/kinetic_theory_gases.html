01

Introduction to Kinetic Theory

The kinetic theory of gases bridges the microscopic world of atomic motion with the macroscopic properties of matter such as pressure, temperature, and volume. Instead of treating a gas as a continuous fluid, kinetic theory models a gas as a vast collection of tiny, identical point-like particles (molecules or atoms) in constant, random, rapid motion, colliding elastically with one another and with the walls of their container.

Core Assumptions of an Ideal Gas:
1. The gas consists of a very large number of identical molecules moving in random directions with a distribution of speeds.
2. The total volume of the gas molecules is negligible compared to the total volume of the container.
3. Intermolecular forces are completely negligible except during instantaneous elastic collisions.
4. All collisions between molecules and with container walls are perfectly elastic.
02

The Ideal Gas Equation of State

Macroscopically, gases under low pressures and high temperatures obey the Ideal Gas Law, which relates pressure (P), volume (V), temperature (T), and quantity of gas:

Macroscopic Form:
Pressure × Volume = Number of Moles × Universal Gas Constant × Absolute Temperature
(PV = nRT, where R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) and temperature T is measured strictly in Kelvin).
  • Microscopic Form: Using the Boltzmann constant (kB = R / NA = 1.38 × 10-23 J/K) and the total number of molecules N:
    Pressure × Volume = Number of Molecules × Boltzmann Constant × Absolute Temperature (PV = N kB T).
  • Gas Laws Combined: Encapsulates Boyle's Law (P proportional to 1/V at constant T), Charles's Law (V proportional to T at constant P), and Gay-Lussac's Law (P proportional to T at constant V).
03

Microscopic Derivation of Gas Pressure

Pressure inside a gas container is not a static force, but rather the cumulative macroscopic result of billions of microscopic molecular momentum impulses striking the container walls every second.

  • Derivation Outline: When a molecule of mass m moving with speed vx hits a wall perpendicularly and rebounds elastically, its change in momentum is 2m vx. Dividing this impulse by the time interval between successive wall impacts and summing over all N molecules in a volume V yields:
    Pressure = (1/3) × (Mass Density) × (Root-Mean-Square Speed squared)
    or in terms of total molecules: Pressure = (1/3) × (Number Density) × (Mass of Molecule) × (Average Speed squared).
04

Root-Mean-Square Molecular Speed

Because gas molecules travel at a wide range of speeds, we use the root-mean-square (RMS) speed (vrms) as the statistical representative speed for calculating kinetic energy.

Equations for RMS Speed:
vrms = square root of ((3 × Universal Gas Constant × Absolute Temperature) / Molar Mass)
  or in terms of Boltzmann constant and single molecule mass (m):
vrms = square root of ((3 × Boltzmann Constant × Absolute Temperature) / Mass of Single Molecule)
  • Temperature Dependence: RMS speed is directly proportional to the square root of the absolute temperature in Kelvin and inversely proportional to the square root of the molar mass. Lighter gas molecules (like hydrogen or helium) move much faster than heavier molecules (like carbon dioxide or nitrogen) at the same temperature.
05

Equipartition of Energy & Internal Energy

The theorem of equipartition of energy states that in thermal equilibrium, every independent degree of freedom (ways a molecule can store energy, such as translational, rotational, or vibrational motion) shares an average thermal energy of (1/2) × Boltzmann Constant × Absolute Temperature per molecule.

  • Degrees of Freedom (f):
    • Monoatomic gases (He, Ne): 3 translational degrees of freedom (f = 3).
    • Diatomic gases (O2, N2 at room temperature): 3 translational + 2 rotational = 5 degrees of freedom (f = 5).
    • Polyatomic gases: 6 or more degrees of freedom.
  • Total Internal Energy (U): Internal Energy = (Number of Moles) × (Degrees of Freedom / 2) × Universal Gas Constant × Absolute Temperature (U = (f/2) nRT).
06

Maxwell-Boltzmann Speed Distribution

While the RMS speed gives an average statistical benchmark, the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution function describes the exact probability distribution of speeds among molecules in a gas at a given temperature.

  • Key Speed Markers on the Distribution Curve:
    • Most Probable Speed (vmp): The peak of the distribution curve where the maximum number of molecules travel:
    vmp = square root of ((2 × Universal Gas Constant × Temperature) / Molar Mass).
    • Average (Mean) Speed (vavg): The mathematical average speed of all molecules:
    vavg = square root of ((8 × Universal Gas Constant × Temperature) / (pi × Molar Mass)).
    • Root-Mean-Square Speed (vrms):
    vrms = square root of ((3 × Universal Gas Constant × Temperature) / Molar Mass).
    (Always ordered as: vmp < vavg < vrms).
  • Effect of Temperature: As temperature increases, the distribution curve flattens and shifts to the right, meaning a greater proportion of molecules attain higher speeds.

Exhaustive Worked Exam Examples

Problem 1 (RMS Speed Calculation):

Calculate the root-mean-square (RMS) speed of oxygen molecules (O2) at a room temperature of 20.0 degrees Celsius (293.15 K). Given that the molar mass of oxygen gas is M = 0.0320 kg/mol. (Use R = 8.314 J/(mol·K)).

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
- Formula: vrms = square root of ((3 × R × T) / M).
- Convert temperature to Kelvin: T = 20.0 + 273.15 = 293.15 K.
- Substitute values:
vrms = square root of (((3 × 8.314 J/(mol·K)) × 293.15 K) / (0.0320 kg/mol)).
- Numerator: 3 × 8.314 × 293.15 = 7,309.58 J/mol.
- Division: 7,309.58 / 0.0320 = 228,424.4 m2/s2.
- Take square root: vrms = square root of (228,424.4) approximately equal to 477.9 m/s.
Problem 2 (Ideal Gas Law & Number Density):

A rigid steel container of volume 0.500 m3 holds helium gas (a monoatomic ideal gas) at a pressure of 2.00 × 105 Pa and a temperature of 300.0 K. Calculate: (a) the total number of moles of helium gas, and (b) the total internal energy of the gas. (Use R = 8.314 J/(mol·K)).

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
(a) Number of Moles (n):
- Ideal Gas Law: P × V = n × R × T implies n = (P × V) / (R × T).
- n = ((2.00 × 105 Pa) × (0.500 m3)) / ((8.314 J/(mol·K)) × (300.0 K)).
- n = 100,000 / 2,494.2 approximately equal to 40.1 moles.

(b) Total Internal Energy (U):
- For a monoatomic ideal gas, degrees of freedom f = 3.
- Formula: U = (f/2) × n × R × T = (3/2) × P × V.
- U = 1.5 × (2.00 × 105 Pa) × (0.500 m3) = 1.5 × 100,000 = 150,000 Joules (150 kJ).
Problem 3 (Comparing Molecular Speeds):

Compare the RMS speed of helium atoms (molar mass = 0.0040 kg/mol) with that of sulfur hexafluoride molecules (molar mass = 0.1460 kg/mol) when both gases are maintained at the exact same temperature of 300 K. Calculate the ratio of their RMS speeds (vrms, He / vrms, SF6).

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
- Formula: vrms = square root of ((3 × R × T) / M).
- Since R, T, and factor 3 are identical for both gases, vrms is inversely proportional to the square root of molar mass (M):
vrms, He / vrms, SF6 = square root of (MSF6 / MHe).
- Substitute molar masses: square root of (0.1460 / 0.0040) = square root of (36.5) approximately equal to 6.04.
- Conclusion: Helium atoms travel more than 6 times faster than sulfur hexafluoride molecules at the same temperature!
