01

Introduction to Wave Motion

A mechanical wave is a disturbance that travels through a physical medium (such as a solid, liquid, or gas), transporting energy and momentum from one region of space to another without causing any permanent bulk displacement of the medium itself. As the wave passes, individual particles of the medium oscillate about their stable equilibrium positions.

Core Conceptual Principle:
Waves transport energy, not matter. The speed at which a wave disturbance propagates through a medium depends strictly on the elastic and inertial properties of that medium.
02

Transverse and Longitudinal Waves

Mechanical waves are classified into two primary categories based on the direction of particle oscillation relative to the direction of wave propagation:

  • Transverse Waves: Waves in which the oscillations of the medium particles are perpendicular (at right angles) to the direction of wave travel. Examples include ripples on the surface of water and waves traveling down a stretched guitar string.
  • Longitudinal Waves: Waves in which the oscillations of the medium particles are parallel to the direction of wave travel, consisting of alternating regions of compression (high pressure and density) and rarefaction (low pressure and density). Examples include sound waves propagating through air.
03

Mathematical Description of Traveling Waves

A sinusoidal wave traveling along the positive x-axis can be described mathematically by a wave function representing displacement y as a position x and time t:

  • Wave Equation:
    Displacement = Amplitude × sine(Angular Frequency × Time - Wave Number × Position)
    y(x, t) = A × sin(omega * t - k * x).
  • Key Wave Parameters:
    • Amplitude (A): The maximum displacement of a particle from its equilibrium position.
    • Wavelength (lambda): The spatial distance between two successive identical points on a wave (e.g., crest to crest). Measured in meters.
    • Wave Number (k): Spatial frequency defined as k = (2 * pi) / lambda (measured in rad/m).
    • Angular Frequency (omega): Temporal frequency defined as omega = 2 * pi * frequency (measured in rad/s).
    • Wave Propagation Speed (v): The speed at which wave crests move through space, related by Speed = Frequency × Wavelength (v = frequency * lambda = omega / k).
04

Speed of Waves in Media

The propagation speed of a mechanical wave is determined entirely by the mechanical properties of the medium through which it travels:

  • Waves on a Stretched String: The wave speed depends directly on the tension force (FT) in the string and inversely on the linear mass density (mu = mass / length):
    Speed = square root of (Tension / Linear Mass Density)
  • Sound Waves in Fluids: The speed of sound depends on the bulk modulus (B) and the volumetric mass density (rho):
    Speed of Sound = square root of (Bulk Modulus / Density)
  • Sound Waves in Ideal Gases:
    Speed of Sound = square root of ((Adiabatic Index × Universal Gas Constant × Kelvin Temperature) / Molar Mass)
05

Principle of Superposition and Interference

The principle of superposition states that when two or more mechanical waves overlap in space, the net displacement of the medium at any point and at any instant is equal to the algebraic sum of the individual wave displacements.

  • Constructive Interference: When two overlapping waves arrive in phase (crest meets crest, trough meets trough), their amplitudes reinforce each other, producing a resultant wave with a larger amplitude.
  • Destructive Interference: When two overlapping waves arrive completely out of phase (crest meets trough), their displacements cancel each other out, producing a resultant wave with a smaller or zero amplitude.
  • Beats: When two sound waves of slightly different frequencies (f1 and f2) interfere, they produce a periodic waxing and waning of sound volume known as beats, with a beat frequency equal to the absolute difference: Beat Frequency = Absolute Value of (Frequency 1 - Frequency 2).
06

Standing Waves and Boundary Conditions

When two identical sinusoidal waves travel in opposite directions along the same medium (such as a reflected wave meeting an incoming wave), they interfere to produce a standing wave. Standing waves do not travel horizontally; instead, they remain stationary in space.

  • Nodes and Antinodes:
    • Nodes: Points of zero displacement where destructive interference is permanent.
    • Antinodes: Points of maximum displacement where constructive interference is permanent.
  • Strings Fixed at Both Ends: Standing waves occur only at specific resonant frequencies (harmonics):
    Frequency of Harmonic n = (Harmonic Number × Wave Speed) / (2 × Length of String) (fn = n * v / (2 * L)).
  • Air Columns in Pipes:
    • Open-Open Pipes: Support all harmonics (n = 1, 2, 3, ...), with antinodes at both ends: fn = n * v / (2 * L).
    • Open-Closed Pipes: Support only odd harmonics (n = 1, 3, 5, ...), with a displacement node at the closed end and an antinode at the open end: fn = n * v / (4 * L).
07

Sound Waves, Intensity & Decibel Scale

Sound waves are longitudinal pressure waves traveling through elastic media. The human ear perceives sound intensity logarithmically, spanning a vast dynamic range.

  • Sound Intensity (I): The rate of sound energy flow per unit area perpendicular to the direction of wave propagation, measured in Watts per square meter (W/m2): Intensity = Power / Area. Intensity also obeys an inverse-square law from a point source: Intensity is proportional to 1 / (Distance squared).
  • Decibel Scale (Sound Level beta): Defined logarithmically relative to the human hearing threshold of intensity (I0 = 10-12 W/m2):
    Sound Level in Decibels = 10 × logarithm base 10 of (Intensity / Reference Intensity) (beta = 10 * log10(I / I0)).
08

The Doppler Effect

The Doppler effect is the apparent change in the frequency of a wave received by an observer whenever there is relative motion between the wave source and the observer.

General Doppler Frequency Equation:
Observed Frequency = Source Frequency × ((Speed of Sound ± Speed of Observer) / (Speed of Sound ∓ Speed of Source))
where upper signs are used when source/observer move toward each other, and lower signs when moving apart. (Remember: "Toward means higher frequency, away means lower frequency").

Exhaustive Worked Exam Examples

Problem 1 (Wave Speed on a Stretched String):

A steel guitar string has a length of 0.800 m and a total mass of 3.20 grams. If the string is subjected to a tension force of 200 N, calculate: (a) the linear mass density of the string, and (b) the propagation speed of transverse waves along the string.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
(a) Linear Mass Density (mu):
- mu = Total Mass / Length = (3.20 × 10-3 kg) / (0.800 m) = 0.0040 kg/m (4.00 g/m).

(b) Wave Speed (v):
- Formula: v = square root of (Tension / Linear Mass Density).
- v = square root of (200 N / 0.0040 kg/m) = square root of (50,000) approximately equal to 223.6 m/s.
Problem 2 (Standing Waves in an Open Pipe):

An organ pipe that is open at both ends has a length of 1.70 m. Given that the speed of sound in air is 340 m/s, calculate: (a) the fundamental frequency (first harmonic), and (b) the frequency of the third harmonic (n = 3).

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
(a) Fundamental Frequency (f1):
- Formula for open-open pipe: fn = (n × v) / (2 × L).
- f1 = (1 × 340 m/s) / (2 × 1.70 m) = 340 / 3.40 = 100 Hz.

(b) Third Harmonic Frequency (f3):
- f3 = 3 × f1 = 3 × 100 Hz = 300 Hz.
Problem 3 (The Doppler Effect):

An ambulance traveling at a speed of 30.0 m/s emits a siren tone of frequency 600 Hz while driving straight toward a stationary pedestrian. Given that the speed of sound in air is 340 m/s, calculate the frequency heard by the pedestrian.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
- Doppler Formula for source moving toward stationary observer: fobs = fsource × (v / (v - vsource)).
- Substitute values:
fobs = (600 Hz) × (340 m/s / (340 m/s - 30.0 m/s)).
- fobs = 600 × (340 / 310) = 600 × 1.0968 approximately equal to 658 Hz.
