Introduction to Kinematics

Mechanics is the branch of physics concerned with the study of motion and forces. It is subdivided into kinematics—the description of motion without regard to its causes (forces)—and dynamics, which investigates the forces that cause or modify motion.

To describe motion mathematically in one, two, or three dimensions, we must establish a coordinate reference frame consisting of an origin point and mutually orthogonal spatial axes (typically Cartesian x, y, and z coordinates) paired with a standard clock to measure time intervals.

Core Conceptual Principle:
All kinematic descriptions are strictly relative to the chosen reference frame. An observer standing stationary on the Earth measures different velocities for a moving train compared to an observer riding aboard another train.
02

Scalars, Vectors, and Tensor Foundations

Physical quantities are classified based on their directional properties and transformation laws under coordinate rotations:

  • Scalar Quantities: Quantities completely specified by a single real number with appropriate physical units, possessing no directional attribute. Examples include mass (m), time (t), temperature (T), speed (v), work (W), energy (E), and electric charge (q). They obey the standard laws of algebraic addition.
  • Vector Quantities: Quantities possessing both a magnitude (a positive real number with units) and a specific spatial direction. Examples include displacement (x or s), velocity (v), acceleration (a), force (F), momentum (p), and electric field (E). Vectors combine according to geometric rules such as the triangle or parallelogram law of addition.
  • Geometric Representation: A vector is graphically depicted as a directed line segment (an arrow) where the length of the segment is proportional to the vector's magnitude, and the arrowhead indicates its direction in space.

Vector Algebra & Operations

Let vector A and vector B be two vectors in three-dimensional space, and let scalar c be a scalar:

  • Scalar Multiplication: Multiplying a vector A by a scalar c scales its magnitude by |c| and reverses its direction if c is less than 0.
  • Vector Addition: Resultant vector R = vector A + vector B. Geometrically performed by placing the tail of vector B at the head of vector A (head-to-tail method).
  • Vector Subtraction: vector A - vector B = vector A + (-vector B), where -vector B has the same magnitude as vector B but points in the exact opposite direction.
  • Unit Vectors: A unit vector (u-hat) is a dimensionless vector of magnitude 1 pointing in the direction of vector A, defined as u-hat = vector A / magnitude of vector A. The standard Cartesian unit vectors are i-hat, j-hat, and k-hat along the x, y, and z axes.
03

One-Dimensional Kinematics: Position, Velocity & Acceleration

Motion along a straight line (1D) requires defining a position coordinate x(t) as a continuous function of time t:

  • Distance (s): A scalar quantity representing the total accumulated length of the path traveled by an object, regardless of direction. Always greater than or equal to 0.
  • Displacement (delta x): A vector quantity defined as the change in position: delta x = xf - xi. It depends solely on the initial and final positions, independent of the path taken.
  • Average Speed (vavg): Total distance traveled divided by the total time elapsed: vavg = Total Distance / delta t.
  • Average Velocity (vav): Total displacement divided by the total time elapsed: vav = delta x / delta t = (x2 - x1) / (t2 - t1).
  • Instantaneous Velocity (v): The limiting value of average velocity as the time interval approaches zero. Mathematically, the first derivative of position with respect to time: v(t) = limit as delta t approaches 0 of (delta x / delta t) = dx / dt.
  • Average Acceleration (aav): The change in velocity divided by the time interval over which the change occurs: aav = delta v / delta t = (v2 - v1) / (t2 - t1).
  • Instantaneous Acceleration (a): The time derivative of instantaneous velocity, or the second time derivative of position: a(t) = dv / dt = d2x / dt2.
04

Graphical Analysis & Calculus Foundations

Kinematics relies heavily on graphical interpretations and calculus relationships between position, velocity, and acceleration:

1. Position vs. Time (x-t) Graphs

  • The slope at any point on an x-t curve represents the instantaneous velocity (v = dx / dt).
  • A straight slanted line indicates constant velocity; a curved line indicates changing velocity (acceleration).
  • The inflection point of an x-t curve corresponds to zero acceleration.

2. Velocity vs. Time (v-t) Graphs

  • The slope at any point on a v-t curve represents the instantaneous acceleration (a = dv / dt).
  • The area under the curve (AUC) between two times t1 and t2 represents the total displacement: delta x = integral from t1 to t2 of v(t) dt.
  • A horizontal line on a v-t graph indicates uniform (constant) velocity and zero acceleration.

3. Acceleration vs. Time (a-t) Graphs

  • The area under the a-t curve between t1 and t2 represents the change in velocity: delta v = integral from t1 to t2 of a(t) dt.
Integral Calculus Bridge:
If acceleration is known as a function of time a(t), velocity is found via integration: v(t) = v0 + integral from 0 to t of a(t) dt. Position is subsequently found by integrating velocity: x(t) = x0 + integral from 0 to t of v(t) dt.

Kinematic Equations for Constant Acceleration

When acceleration a is constant (a = constant), the calculus integrations simplify into the standard kinematic equations. Let initial position x0 = 0, initial time t0 = 0, initial velocity be v0, and final velocity be v:

Equation Name / Purpose Missing Variable
v = v0 + at Velocity-Time Relation Displacement (delta x)
delta x = v0t + (1/2)at2 Displacement-Time Relation Final Velocity (v)
v2 = v02 + 2a(delta x) Velocity-Displacement Relation Time (t)
delta x = ((v + v0) / 2)t Average Velocity Relation Acceleration (a)
delta x = vt - (1/2)at2 Displacement with Final Velocity Initial Velocity (v0)

Free Fall Motion under Gravity

A special case of 1D motion with constant acceleration occurs for objects in free fall near the surface of the Earth, where air resistance is neglected. The acceleration is directed vertically downward with magnitude g approximately equal to 9.81 m/s2 (or 9.80 m/s2 depending on curriculum convention). Setting the upward vertical direction as positive (+y), we substitute a = -g into the kinematic equations:

  • v = v0 - gt
  • y = y0 + v0t - (1/2)gt2
  • v2 = v02 - 2g(y - y0)

Two-Dimensional Vector Analysis & Components

Extending motion to two dimensions (the x-y plane) requires treating vectors in component form. Any vector A can be resolved into orthogonal components along the cartesian axes:

  • Cartesian Expansion: vector A = Ax(i-hat) + Ay(j-hat)
  • Magnitude Calculation: Magnitude of vector A = square root of (Ax2 + Ay2)
  • Direction Angle: theta = arctan(Ay / Ax) measured counter-clockwise from the positive x-axis.
  • Component Trigonometry: Ax = A cos(theta) and Ay = A sin(theta).
  • Dot Product (Scalar Product): vector A dot vector B = AxBx + AyBy = AB cos(phi), where phi is the angle between the two vectors.
  • Cross Product (Vector Product in 2D/3D): vector A cross vector B = (AxBy - AyBx)(k-hat) = AB sin(phi) (n-hat).

Projectile Motion in Two Dimensions

Projectile motion describes the curved trajectory of an object launched into the air subject only to the uniform force of gravity (neglecting air resistance). The foundational principle of projectile motion is that horizontal and vertical motions are completely independent of one another.

Governing Equations

Let an object be launched from the origin (0,0) with initial speed v0 at an angle theta above the horizontal:

  • Initial Velocity Components: v0x = v0 cos(theta) and v0y = v0 sin(theta).
  • Horizontal Motion (ax = 0): vx(t) = v0 cos(theta) = constant
    x(t) = (v0 cos(theta))t
  • Vertical Motion (ay = -g): vy(t) = v0 sin(theta) - gt
    y(t) = (v0 sin(theta))t - (1/2)gt2
    vy2 = (v0 sin(theta))2 - 2gy

Key Derived Formulas (Symmetric Landing on Level Ground)

  • Time of Flight (tflight): Total time the projectile remains in the air before returning to y = 0: tflight = (2v0 sin(theta)) / g.
  • Maximum Height Reached (H): Occurs when vertical velocity vy = 0 at t = tflight / 2: H = (v02 sin2(theta)) / (2g).
  • Horizontal Range (R): Total horizontal displacement when the projectile lands (y = 0): R = x(tflight) = (v02 sin(2*theta)) / g.
    Notice that maximum range is achieved when sin(2*theta) = 1, which occurs at theta = 45 degrees.
  • Equation of Trajectory: Eliminating time t yields the parabolic path equation: y = x tan(theta) - (g / (2v02 cos2(theta))) x2.
08

Relative Velocity & Reference Frames

The velocity of an object depends fundamentally upon the reference frame of the observer measuring it. Let frame A be a stationary ground frame, and frame B be a moving reference frame traveling with velocity vector vBA relative to frame A.

  • Galilean Velocity Transformation: The velocity of an object P relative to frame A (vector vPA) is equal to the vector sum of its velocity relative to frame B (vector vPB) and the velocity of frame B relative to frame A (vector vBA):
    vector vPA = vector vPB + vector vBA
  • Inversion Property: vector vAB = -vector vBA.
  • Classic Applications: River-crossing problems (swimmer/boat heading against or across a flowing current) and wind-drift navigation for aircraft.

Exhaustive Worked Exam Examples

Problem 1 (1D Multi-Stage Kinematics):

A dragster starts from rest at the origin and accelerates along a straight track with a constant acceleration of 4.50 m/s2 for 6.00 s. It then maintains that achieved velocity for another 4.00 s, after which a parachute deploys, causing a constant deceleration of 3.00 m/s2 until the dragster comes to a complete rest. Calculate: (a) the maximum velocity reached, (b) the total distance traveled, and (c) the total time of travel.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
Phase 1 (Acceleration):
- Initial velocity v0 = 0, a1 = 4.50 m/s2, t1 = 6.00 s.
- Final velocity v1 = v0 + a1t1 = 0 + (4.50)(6.00) = 27.0 m/s.
- Distance d1 = v0t1 + (1/2)a1t12 = 0 + (1/2)(4.50)(6.00)2 = 81.0 m.

Phase 2 (Constant Velocity):
- Velocity v2 = 27.0 m/s, t2 = 4.00 s.
- Distance d2 = v2t2 = (27.0)(4.00) = 108.0 m.

Phase 3 (Deceleration to Rest):
- Initial velocity v2 = 27.0 m/s, final velocity v3 = 0, deceleration a3 = -3.00 m/s2.
- Time taken t3: v3 = v2 + a3t3 implies 0 = 27.0 - 3.00t3 implies t3 = 9.00 s.
- Distance d3: v32 = v22 + 2a3d3 implies 0 = (27.0)2 + 2(-3.00)d3 implies 6.00d3 = 729 implies d3 = 121.5 m.

Summary Answers:
(a) Maximum Velocity = 27.0 m/s.
(b) Total Distance = d1 + d2 + d3 = 81.0 + 108.0 + 121.5 = 310.5 meters.
(c) Total Time = t1 + t2 + t3 = 6.00 + 4.00 + 9.00 = 19.0 seconds.
Problem 2 (Calculus-Based Kinematics):

The position of a particle moving along the x-axis is given by the time-dependent equation x(t) = 2.0t3 - 9.0t2 + 12.0t - 5.0, where x is in meters and t is in seconds. Determine: (a) expressions for velocity v(t) and acceleration a(t), (b) the times at which the particle is momentarily at rest, and (c) the total distance traveled by the particle during the time interval from t = 0 to t = 3.0 s.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
(a) Velocity and Acceleration Derivatives:
- Velocity v(t) = dx / dt = d/dt(2.0t3 - 9.0t2 + 12.0t - 5.0) = 6.0t2 - 18.0t + 12.0 m/s.
- Acceleration a(t) = dv / dt = d/dt(6.0t2 - 18.0t + 12.0) = 12.0t - 18.0 m/s2.

(b) Times when Particle is at Rest (v(t) = 0):
- Set 6.0t2 - 18.0t + 12.0 = 0 implies t2 - 3.0t + 2.0 = 0.
- Factoring yields (t - 1.0)(t - 2.0) = 0.
- Therefore, the particle is at rest at t = 1.0 s and t = 2.0 s.

(c) Total Distance Traveled (t = 0 to t = 3.0 s):
- Calculate positions at turning points and interval endpoints:
  • At t = 0: x(0) = -5.0 m.
  • At t = 1.0 s (turning point 1): x(1) = 2.0(1)3 - 9.0(1)2 + 12.0(1) - 5.0 = 0.0 m.
  • At t = 2.0 s (turning point 2): x(2) = 2.0(8) - 9.0(4) + 12.0(2) - 5.0 = 16 - 36 + 24 - 5 = -1.0 m.
  • At t = 3.0 s: x(3) = 2.0(27) - 9.0(9) + 12.0(3) - 5.0 = 54 - 81 + 36 - 5 = 4.0 m.
- Segment 1 (t = 0 to 1.0): absolute value of (0.0 - (-5.0)) = 5.0 m.
- Segment 2 (t = 1.0 to 2.0): absolute value of (-1.0 - 0.0) = 1.0 m.
- Segment 3 (t = 2.0 to 3.0): absolute value of (4.0 - (-1.0)) = 5.0 m.
- Total Distance = 5.0 + 1.0 + 5.0 = 11.0 meters.
Problem 3 (Advanced Projectile Motion):

A rescue plane flying horizontally at a constant altitude of 1200 m with a speed of 80.0 m/s drops an emergency survival package to stranded hikers below. (a) How long does it take for the package to hit the ground? (b) What is the horizontal distance traveled by the package during its fall? (c) What are the horizontal and vertical components of its impact velocity? (Assume g = 9.81 m/s2).

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
(a) Time of Fall:
- Vertical motion: y(t) = y0 + v0yt - (1/2)gt2. Setting initial height y0 = 1200 m, ground y = 0, and initial vertical velocity v0y = 0:
- 0 = 1200 - (1/2)(9.81)t2 implies 4.905 t2 = 1200 implies t2 = 244.65 implies t = 15.64 seconds.

(b) Horizontal Range (R):
- Horizontal velocity v0x = 80.0 m/s (constant throughout flight).
- R = v0xt = (80.0 m/s)(15.64 s) = 1251.2 meters.

(c) Impact Velocity Components:
- Horizontal component: vx = v0x = 80.0 m/s.
- Vertical component: vy = v0y - gt = 0 - (9.81)(15.64) = -153.42 m/s (downward).
- Magnitude of Impact Velocity: v = square root of (80.02 + (-153.42)2) = square root of (6400 + 23537.7) = square root of (29937.7) approximately equal to 173.0 m/s.
