01

Introduction to Energy Concepts

While Newton's laws of motion provide a complete vector framework for analyzing physical systems, solving complex dynamics problems involving varying forces or curved paths can become mathematically cumbersome. The energy approach provides an alternative scalar formulation based on the principle of conservation of energy.

Core Conceptual Principle:
Energy is a scalar quantity representing the capacity of a physical system to perform work or transfer heat. The law of conservation of energy states that energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be transformed from one form to another or transferred between systems.
02

Work Done by a Constant and Variable Force

In physics, work (W) is done when a force acts upon an object and causes a displacement along the line of action of the force.

  • Constant Force: For a constant force vector F acting on an object undergoing a displacement vector delta x, work is defined as the scalar dot product:
    W = vector F dot vector delta x = F * delta x * cos(theta)
    where theta is the angle between the force vector and the displacement vector. Measured in Joules (J), where 1 J = 1 N·m = 1 kg·m2/s2.
  • Sign of Work:
    - If 0 degrees ≤ theta < 90 degrees, cos(theta) > 0, so work is positive (energy is transferred into the system).
    - If theta = 90 degrees, cos(90 degrees) = 0, so work is zero (e.g., centripetal force or carrying a bucket horizontally).
    - If 90 degrees < theta ≤ 180 degrees, cos(theta) < 0, so work is negative (energy is removed from the system, e.g., kinetic friction).
  • Variable Force (Calculus): When force varies with position along a 1D path from xi to xf, work is calculated via definite integration:
    W = integral from xi to xf of F(x) dx (equal to the area under the Force vs. Position curve).
03

The Work-Energy Theorem

The work-energy theorem establishes the direct bridge between mechanical work and changes in an object's speed:

Statement:
The net work (Wnet) done by all external forces acting on a particle is equal to the exact change in the particle's kinetic energy (delta K):
Wnet = Kf - Ki = delta K

This powerful theorem implies that if positive net work is done on an object, its kinetic energy increases (it speeds up). If negative net work is done, its kinetic energy decreases (it slows down).

04

Kinetic Energy

Kinetic energy (K) is the energy associated with an object's state of motion:

  • Translational Kinetic Energy: For an object of mass m moving with speed v:
    K = (1/2) * m * v2
  • Derivation via Newton's Second Law: Starting from W = integral of F dx where F = m(dv/dt) and dx = v dt:
    W = integral from vi to vf of m v dv = (1/2)m vf2 - (1/2)m vi2 = Kf - Ki.
05

Potential Energy and Conservative Systems

Potential energy (U) is stored energy associated with the configuration or relative position of a system subject to conservative forces.

  • Gravitational Potential Energy (Near Earth Surface): For an object of mass m at a vertical height y above a chosen reference datum:
    Ug = m * g * y
  • Elastic Potential Energy (Springs): For an ideal spring obeying Hooke's Law (F = -kx) stretched or compressed by a displacement x from its unstretched equilibrium position:
    Us = (1/2) * k * x2
    where k is the spring constant (stiffness in N/m) and x is the displacement from equilibrium.
06

Conservative vs. Non-Conservative Forces

Forces in physics are classified into two broad categories based on path dependence and energy dissipation:

  • Conservative Forces: Forces for which the work done in moving an object between two points is completely independent of the path taken. Equivalently, the net work done by a conservative force around any closed loop is exactly zero. Examples include gravitational force, elastic spring force, and electrostatic Coulomb force.
  • Non-Conservative Forces: Forces for which the work done depends explicitly on the path taken, and mechanical energy is dissipated into thermal or internal energy. Examples include kinetic friction, air resistance (drag), and applied tension from human muscles.
07

Conservation of Mechanical Energy

Total mechanical energy (E) of a system is defined as the sum of its kinetic energy and potential energy: E = K + U.

  • Principle of Conservation: When only conservative forces act within an isolated system, the total mechanical energy remains strictly constant:
    Ei = Ef → Ki + Ui = Kf + Uf
  • Extension with Non-Conservative Forces (like Friction): When non-conservative forces such as friction do work (Wnc), total mechanical energy changes by that exact amount:
    Wnc = delta E = Ef - Ei = (Kf + Uf) - (Ki + Ui)
08

Power Output and Efficiency

Power (P) measures the rate at which work is performed or at which energy is transferred or converted over time.

  • Average Power: Pavg = W / delta t = delta E / delta t. Measured in Watts (W), where 1 W = 1 J/s.
  • Instantaneous Power: P = dW / dt = F * (dx / dt) = F * v (scalar product of force and instantaneous velocity).
  • Mechanical Efficiency (eta): The ratio of useful power output to total power input: eta = (Pout / Pin) * 100 percent.

Exhaustive Worked Exam Examples

Problem 1 (Conservation of Energy on a Roller Coaster):

A cart of mass m = 500 kg starts from rest at the top of a hill of height h1 = 40.0 m. It rolls down the track, passing through a valley and then ascending a secondary hill of height h2 = 15.0 m. (a) Calculate the speed of the cart at the top of the second hill. (b) Calculate the maximum speed achieved at the bottom of the valley (height y = 0). (Assume g = 9.81 m/s2 and neglect friction).

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
(a) Speed at Top of Second Hill ($h_2 = 15.0\text{ m}$):
- Conservation of Mechanical Energy: $E_i = E_f \implies K_i + U_i = K_f + U_f$.
- Since initial speed $v_i = 0$, $K_i = 0$. Thus: $m g h_1 = \frac{1}{2}m v_f^2 + m g h_2$.
- Mass $m$ cancels out: $g h_1 = \frac{1}{2}v_f^2 + g h_2 \implies v_f^2 = 2g(h_1 - h_2)$.
- $v_f^2 = 2(9.81)(40.0 - 15.0) = 19.62(25.0) = 490.5$.
- $v_f = \sqrt{490.5} =$ 22.15 m/s.

(b) Maximum Speed at Valley Bottom ($y = 0$):
- $m g h_1 = \frac{1}{2}m v_{max}^2 \implies v_{max} = \sqrt{2g h_1}$.
- $v_{max} = \sqrt{2(9.81)(40.0)} = \sqrt{784.8} =$ 28.01 m/s.
Problem 2 (Work Done by Friction & Non-Conservative Forces):

A 2.00 kg block slides across a horizontal table with an initial speed of 6.00 m/s. Due to kinetic friction, the block travels a distance of 4.00 m before coming to a complete stop. (a) Calculate the work done by friction on the block. (b) Determine the coefficient of kinetic friction ($\mu_k$) between the block and the table. (Assume $g = 9.81\text{ m/s}^2$).

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
(a) Work Done by Friction:
- Using the Work-Energy Theorem ($W_{net} = \Delta K$):
- $W_{friction} = K_f - K_i = 0 - \frac{1}{2}m v_i^2$.
- $W_{friction} = -\frac{1}{2}(2.00\text{ kg})(6.00\text{ m/s})^2 = -1(36.0) =$ $-36.0\text{ Joules}$.

(b) Coefficient of Kinetic Friction ($\mu_k$):
- Work by a constant friction force is also defined as $W = -f_k \cdot d = -\mu_k m g d$.
- $-\mu_k m g d = -36.0 \implies \mu_k (2.00)(9.81)(4.00) = 36.0$.
- $\mu_k (78.48) = 36.0 \implies \mu_k = \frac{36.0}{78.48} \approx$ 0.459.
Problem 3 (Power Output of an Elevator):

An electric elevator motor lifts a total load of 1200 kg at a constant upward speed of 3.00 m/s. (a) What is the instantaneous mechanical power output required from the motor? (b) If the motor has an operational efficiency of 75.0 percent, what is the total electrical power input required from the mains supply? (Assume $g = 9.81\text{ m/s}^2$).

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
(a) Mechanical Power Output ($P_{out}$):
- Since speed is constant, the upward force exerted by the motor equals the weight of the load: $F = mg = (1200\text{ kg})(9.81\text{ m/s}^2) = 11,772\text{ N}$.
- Power $P = F \cdot v = (11,772\text{ N})(3.00\text{ m/s}) = 35,316\text{ W} =$ $35.3\text{ kW}$.

(b) Total Electrical Power Input ($P_{in}$):
- Efficiency $\eta = \frac{P_{out}}{P_{in}} \times 100\% \implies P_{in} = \frac{P_{out}}{\eta} = \frac{35,316\text{ W}}{0.750} = 47,088\text{ W} =$ $47.1\text{ kW}$.
