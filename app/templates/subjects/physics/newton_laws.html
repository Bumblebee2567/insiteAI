01

Introduction to Dynamics

While kinematics describes how objects move in terms of position, velocity, and acceleration, dynamics explores why objects move the way they do by investigating forces. A force is a vector push or pull exerted on an object that has the capacity to change its state of motion or deform its structure.

Forces are categorized fundamentally into contact forces (where physical touch is required, such as normal force, friction, tension, and applied pushing/pulling forces) and non-contact (field) forces (acting across empty space through force fields, such as gravitational force, electrostatic force, and magnetic force).

Core Conceptual Principle:
Force is a vector quantity measured in Newtons (N), where 1 Newton is defined as the force required to accelerate a 1 kg mass at a rate of 1 m/s2 (1 N = 1 kg·m/s2).
02

Newton's First Law and Inertia

Often called the Law of Inertia, Newton's First Law states:

Statement:
An object at rest will remain at rest, and an object in motion will continue moving at a constant velocity in a straight line, unless acted upon by a net external force.
  • Inertia: The intrinsic property of any mass to resist changes in its state of motion. Mass is the quantitative measure of inertia.
  • Translational Equilibrium: When the vector sum of all forces acting on an object is zero (sum of vector F = 0), the object is in equilibrium and maintains constant velocity (which includes remaining completely stationary).
  • Inertial Reference Frames: Newton's First Law defines inertial reference frames—frames of reference that are not accelerating, in which Newton's laws hold true without fictitious forces.
03

Newton's Second Law of Motion

Newton's Second Law establishes the quantitative relationship between net force, mass, and acceleration:

Equation:
The net force (sum of vector F) acting on an object is directly proportional to the product of its mass (m) and its resulting acceleration (vector a):
sum of vector F = m * vector a
  • Component Form: In a Cartesian coordinate system, the vector equation resolves into three scalar equations: sum of Fx = m * ax, sum of Fy = m * ay, and sum of Fz = m * az.
  • Gravitational Force (Weight): Near the surface of the Earth, the downward gravitational force acting on a mass m is given by W = mg, where g is the acceleration due to gravity (approximately 9.81 m/s2).
04

Newton's Third Law and Action-Reaction Pairs

Newton's Third Law governs the mutual interaction between two distinct bodies:

Statement:
Whenever one body exerts a force on a second body, the second body simultaneously exerts a force equal in magnitude and opposite in direction on the first body.
vector FAB = -vector FBA
  • Crucial Distinction: Action-reaction pairs never act on the same object. They always act on two different interacting bodies, which is why forces within a system do not cancel each other out when calculating net force on a single body.
05

Free-Body Diagrams (FBD)

A free-body diagram is the single most essential tool in solving mechanics problems. It is a schematic sketch representing an object as a point particle isolated from its surroundings, with all external forces acting upon it drawn as vectors pointing away from the center.

  • Step 1: Identify and isolate the specific body of interest.
  • Step 2: Draw all contact and field forces acting directly on that body (Gravity W pointing down, Normal force N perpendicular to contact surfaces, Tension T pulling away along ropes, Friction f opposing potential motion).
  • Step 3: Establish a convenient coordinate system (often aligning one axis with the direction of motion or acceleration).
  • Step 4: Resolve any diagonal forces into orthogonal x and y components.
  • Step 5: Apply Newton's Second Law ($\sum F_x = m a_x$ and $\sum F_y = m a_y$) to solve for unknowns.
06

Friction Mechanics & Surface Interaction

Friction is a resistive contact force that opposes the relative motion (or tendency of motion) between two surfaces in contact. It arises from microscopic interlocking surface asperities and molecular adhesion.

  • Static Friction (fs): Acts when surfaces are not sliding relative to each other. It matches the applied parallel force up to a maximum threshold: fs, max = mus * N, where mus is the coefficient of static friction and N is the normal force.
  • Kinetic Friction (fk): Acts when surfaces are actively sliding past one another. It remains relatively constant: fk = muk * N, where muk is the coefficient of kinetic friction (typically muk < mus).
07

Motion on Inclined Planes

When analyzing an object resting or moving on a ramp inclined at an angle $\theta$ above the horizontal, it is standard practice to tilt the coordinate axes: set the x-axis parallel to the incline surface (pointing down or up the slope) and the y-axis perpendicular to the incline surface.

  • Gravitational Components:
    - Component perpendicular to the incline: $W_y = mg \cos\theta$.
    - Component parallel down the incline: $W_x = mg \sin\theta$.
  • Normal Force: In the absence of vertical acceleration perpendicular to the ramp: $N = mg \cos\theta$.
  • Acceleration Down a Frictionless Incline: $a = g \sin\theta$ (independent of mass $m$).
  • Acceleration Down a Rough Incline (with kinetic friction $f_k$): $a = g(\sin\theta - \mu_k \cos\theta)$.
08

Tension in Ropes, Strings, and Pulleys

Tension ($T$) is the pulling force transmitted axially through a stretched rope, string, cable, or chain.

  • Ideal Strings: Unless otherwise specified, strings are assumed to be massless (meaning tension is uniform throughout the entire length of the string) and inextensible (maintaining constant acceleration constraints between connected bodies).
  • Ideal Pulleys: Frictionless and massless pulleys redirect the line of action of tension without changing its magnitude.
  • Atwood Machine: Two masses ($m_1$ and $m_2$) connected by a string passing over a massless, frictionless pulley. The acceleration of the system is given by $a = \frac{|m_2 - m_1|}{m_1 + m_2}g$, and the tension is $T = \frac{2m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}g$.

Exhaustive Worked Exam Examples

Problem 1 (Multi-Body Connected System on a Table):

A block of mass m1 = 4.00 kg rests on a rough horizontal table where the coefficient of kinetic friction is muk = 0.250. A light string attached to m1 runs horizontally over a frictionless pulley and hangs vertically down, connected to a suspended block of mass m2 = 6.00 kg. Calculate: (a) the acceleration of the system, and (b) the tension in the connecting string. (Assume g = 9.81 m/s2).

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
(a) System Acceleration:
- Forces on m1 (horizontal): Tension T pulling forward, kinetic friction fk = mukN pulling back.
- Normal force on m1: N = m1g = (4.00 kg)(9.81 m/s2) = 39.24 N.
- Friction force: fk = 0.250 * 39.24 = 9.81 N.
- Equation of motion for m1: T - fk = m1a.
- Forces on m2 (vertical downward motion): Weight m2g pulling down, tension T pulling up.
- Equation of motion for m2: m2g - T = m2a.
- Adding both equations to eliminate T: m2g - fk = (m1 + m2)a.
- (6.00 * 9.81) - 9.81 = (4.00 + 6.00)a
- 58.86 - 9.81 = 10.00 a
- 49.05 = 10.00 a implies a = 4.91 m/s2.

(b) Tension in the String:
- Substitute acceleration into m2 equation: T = m2g - m2a = 58.86 - (6.00 * 4.91) = 58.86 - 29.46 = 29.4 N.
Problem 2 (Block on Rough Inclined Plane):

A 5.00 kg wooden block is placed on a ramp inclined at 30.0 degrees above the horizontal. The coefficient of static friction between the block and the ramp is 0.400, and the coefficient of kinetic friction is 0.300. (a) Will the block slide down the ramp on its own if released from rest? (b) If it slides, calculate its acceleration down the ramp. (Assume g = 9.81 m/s2).

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
(a) Check for Motion (Static Friction Test):
- Component of weight pulling down ramp: Wparallel = mg sin(30 degrees) = (5.00)(9.81)(0.500) = 24.525 N.
- Maximum static friction force: fs,max = mus N = mus mg cos(30 degrees) = 0.400 * (5.00)(9.81)(0.866) = 0.400 * 42.47 = 16.99 N.
- Since Wparallel (24.525 N) > fs,max (16.99 N), the static friction cannot hold the block. Yes, the block will slide.

(b) Calculate Acceleration using Kinetic Friction:
- Kinetic friction force: fk = muk N = 0.300 * 42.47 = 12.74 N.
- Net force down the incline: Fnet = Wparallel - fk = 24.525 - 12.74 = 11.785 N.
- Acceleration a = Fnet / m = 11.785 / 5.00 = 2.36 m/s2.
