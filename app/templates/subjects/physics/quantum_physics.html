Introduction to Quantum Mechanics

At the turn of the 20th century, classical physics encountered insurmountable failures when attempting to explain microscopic phenomena such as atomic emission spectra, blackbody radiation, and the photoelectric effect. This gave birth to quantum mechanics, a revolutionary framework revealing that energy, momentum, and other physical quantities on atomic and subatomic scales are restricted to discrete, quantized values rather than continuous spectra.

Core Conceptual Principle:
Energy is quantized. Light, traditionally modeled strictly as continuous electromagnetic waves, exhibits particle-like properties (photons), while matter particles (such as electrons) exhibit wave-like properties (matter waves).
02

Blackbody Radiation & Planck's Hypothesis

A blackbody is an idealized physical object that absorbs all incident electromagnetic radiation falling on it and, when heated, emits thermal radiation across all frequencies. Classical physics predicted that a blackbody would emit infinite energy at ultraviolet frequencies (the "ultraviolet catastrophe"), which contradicted experimental reality.

  • Planck's Quantum Hypothesis: Max Planck resolved the crisis in 1900 by proposing that atoms in a blackbody oscillator can only absorb or emit energy in discrete packets called quanta:
    Energy of a Quantum = Planck's Constant × Frequency (E = h * f),
    where Planck's constant h ≈ 6.626 × 10-34 Joule·seconds.
03

The Photoelectric Effect & Einstein's Photons

The photoelectric effect occurs when light shines onto a clean metal surface, causing electrons to be emitted from the metal. Classical wave theory failed to explain why electron emission depended entirely on the frequency of the light rather than its intensity.

  • Einstein's Photon Explanation: Albert Einstein proposed that light consists of discrete particle packets called photons, each carrying energy E = h * f. When a single photon strikes an electron in the metal, it transfers its entire energy bundle in a 1-to-1 interaction.
  • Photoelectric Equation:
    Maximum Kinetic Energy of Emitted Electron = Photon Energy - Work Function
    (Kmax = h * f - phi),
    where phi (work function) is the minimum energy required to liberate an electron from the metal surface.
  • Threshold Frequency (f0): The minimum light frequency required to eject electrons, given by h * f0 = phi. Light frequencies below f0 produce zero photoelectrons regardless of how bright (intense) the light source is.
04

Compton Scattering

Arthur Compton demonstrated that when high-energy X-ray photons collide with stationary electrons, the scattered X-ray photons emerge with a longer wavelength (lower energy and frequency). This scattering process proves that photons possess momentum as well as energy, obeying relativistic conservation of energy and momentum during particle-like collisions.

  • Compton Wavelength Shift Formula:
    Wavelength Shift = (Planck's Constant / (Electron Mass × Speed of Light)) × (1 - cosine of Scattering Angle)
    (lambda' - lambda = (h / (me * c)) × (1 - cos(theta))).
05

de Broglie Matter Waves & Wave-Particle Duality

In 1924, Louis de Broglie proposed a bold symmetry in nature: if light waves can exhibit particle-like momentum, then all material particles (such as electrons, protons, and atoms) must also exhibit wave-like characteristics.

de Broglie Wavelength Equation:
Wavelength = Planck's Constant / Momentum
lambda = h / p = h / (m * v),
where m is the mass of the particle and v is its velocity. Later confirmed experimentally via electron diffraction experiments (Davisson-Germer experiment).

Exhaustive Worked Exam Examples

Problem 1 (Photon Energy Calculation):

Calculate the energy of a single photon of ultraviolet light having a frequency of f = 1.50 × 1015 Hz. Express your answer in both Joules and electron-volts (eV). (Given h = 6.626 × 10-34 J·s, 1 eV = 1.602 × 10-19 J).

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
- Formula: E = h * f.
- Substitute values:
E = (6.626 × 10-34 J·s) × (1.50 × 1015 Hz).
- Multiplication: 6.626 × 1.50 = 9.939.
- Exponent: 10-34 × 1015 = 10-19.
- E = 9.94 × 10-19 Joules.

- Convert to electron-volts (eV):
E = (9.939 × 10-19 J) / (1.602 × 10-19 J/eV) approximately equal to 6.20 eV.
Problem 2 (Photoelectric Effect Kinetic Energy):

Light of frequency f = 8.00 × 1014 Hz is shone onto a metal surface having a work function of phi = 2.00 eV. Calculate: (a) the energy of the incident photons in eV, and (b) the maximum kinetic energy (Kmax) of the emitted photoelectrons in eV. (Planck constant h ≈ 4.136 × 10-15 eV·s).

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
(a) Photon Energy (E):
- E = h * f = (4.136 × 10-15 eV·s) × (8.00 × 1014 Hz) = 3.309 eV ≈ 3.31 eV.

(b) Maximum Kinetic Energy (Kmax):
- Einstein's equation: Kmax = E - phi.
- Kmax = 3.31 eV - 2.00 eV = 1.31 eV.
Problem 3 (de Broglie Wavelength of an Electron):

Calculate the de Broglie wavelength of an electron traveling at a speed of v = 2.00 × 106 m/s. (Given electron mass m = 9.109 × 10-31 kg, h = 6.626 × 10-34 J·s).

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
- Formula: lambda = h / (m * v).
- Denominator (momentum p): p = (9.109 × 10-31 kg) × (2.00 × 106 m/s) = 1.8218 × 10-24 kg·m/s.
- Substitute values into de Broglie formula:
lambda = (6.626 × 10-34 J·s) / (1.8218 × 10-24 kg·m/s).
- Division: 6.626 / 1.8218 = 3.637.
- Exponent: 10-34 / 10-24 = 10-10 meters.
- lambda = 3.64 × 10-10 meters = 0.364 nanometers (3.64 Angstroms).
