Introduction to Electromagnetic Induction

While Oersted discovered that electric currents produce magnetic fields, Michael Faraday and Joseph Henry independently discovered the inverse phenomenon: changing magnetic fields can generate electric currents in closed conducting loops. Electromagnetic induction is the foundational principle behind electric power generators, transformers, electric motors, and wireless power transfer.

Core Conceptual Principle:
An electromotive force (EMF) is induced in a closed circuit whenever there is a time-rate of change of magnetic flux passing through the interior area of the loop.
02

Faraday's Law of Induction & Lenz's Law

Magnetic flux (PhiB) measures the total magnetic field passing through a given surface area: Magnetic Flux = Magnetic Field × Area × cosine of Angle (PhiB = B × A × cos(theta)), measured in Weber (Wb or T·m2).

  • Faraday's Law of Induction: The magnitude of the induced electromotive force (EMF) in a coil of N turns is directly proportional to the time rate of change of magnetic flux:
    Induced EMF = Number of Turns × (Change in Magnetic Flux / Time Interval) (EMF = N × (Delta PhiB / Delta t)).
  • Lenz's Law: The negative sign in Faraday's Law (EMF = - N × (Delta PhiB / Delta t)) represents Lenz's Law. It states that the direction of the induced current is always such that its own induced magnetic field opposes the original change in magnetic flux that produced it, ensuring strict conservation of energy.
03

Motional Electromotive Force

When a solid conducting rod moves through a static magnetic field, the magnetic forces acting on the free charge carriers inside the metal cause charges to accumulate at opposite ends of the rod, inducing a potential difference known as motional EMF.

  • Motional EMF Equation: For a straight conductor of length L moving at right angles with speed v through a uniform magnetic field B:
    Induced EMF = Magnetic Field × Length × Speed (EMF = B * L * v).
04

Inductance and Inductors

An inductor is a passive circuit component (typically a coil of wire) that stores electrical energy in a magnetic field when electric current flows through it.

  • Self-Inductance (L): Defined as the ratio of magnetic flux linkage to the current producing it:
    Inductance = (Number of Turns × Magnetic Flux) / Current (L = (N * PhiB) / I), measured in Henrys (H).
  • Self-Induced Back EMF: When current changes in an inductor, a back-EMF is induced that opposes the current change:
    Induced EMF = - Inductance × (Rate of Change of Current) (EMF = - L * (Delta I / Delta t)).
  • Energy Stored in an Inductor:
    Energy Stored = (1/2) × Inductance × Current squared (U = (1/2) × L × I2).
05

Transformers and Alternating Voltage Transformation

A transformer is a static electromagnetic device that transfers electrical energy between two or more coil windings through electromagnetic induction, operating strictly on alternating current (AC).

  • Transformer Turns Ratio Equation:
    Secondary Voltage / Primary Voltage = Secondary Turns / Primary Turns (Vs / Vp = Ns / Np).
  • Step-Up vs. Step-Down:
    • Step-Up Transformer: Ns > Np, increasing voltage while decreasing current.
    • Step-Down Transformer: Ns < Np, decreasing voltage while increasing current.
06

Alternating Current (AC) Circuits & Impedance

Alternating current (AC) circuits feature sinusoidal voltages and currents that reverse direction periodically.

  • RMS (Root-Mean-Square) Values: Because AC voltage varies sinusoidally, we use RMS values as effective equivalents to DC values for power calculations:
    RMS Voltage = Peak Voltage / square root of 2 (Vrms = Vpeak / sqrt(2)).
  • Inductive and Capacitive Reactance (XL and XC):
    • Inductive reactance: XL = 2 * pi * frequency * Inductance.
    • Capacitive reactance: XC = 1 / (2 * pi * frequency * Capacitance).
  • Circuit Impedance (Z): Total opposition to AC current flow in an RLC series circuit:
    Impedance = square root of (Resistance squared + (Inductive Reactance - Capacitive Reactance) squared) (Z = sqrt(R2 + (XL - XC)2)).

Exhaustive Worked Exam Examples

Problem 1 (Faraday's Law of Induction):

A circular wire coil with 50 turns and a cross-sectional area of 0.0500 m2 is placed inside a uniform magnetic field directed perpendicularly to the coil face. If the magnetic field strength increases uniformly from 0.100 T to 0.600 T over a time interval of 0.250 seconds, calculate the magnitude of the average induced EMF in the coil.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
- Initial magnetic flux: PhiB,i = Bi * A = 0.100 T * 0.0500 m2 = 0.0050 Wb.
- Final magnetic flux: PhiB,f = Bf * A = 0.600 T * 0.0500 m2 = 0.0300 Wb.
- Change in flux: Delta PhiB = 0.0300 - 0.0050 = 0.0250 Wb.
- Faraday's Law formula: EMF = N * (Delta PhiB / Delta t).
- EMF = 50 * (0.0250 Wb / 0.250 s) = 50 * 0.100 = 5.00 Volts.
Problem 2 (Motional EMF Calculation):

A metal conducting rod of length L = 0.800 m moves at a constant speed of v = 5.00 m/s perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field of strength B = 1.20 Tesla. Calculate the magnitude of the motional EMF induced across the ends of the rod.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
- Formula: EMF = B * L * v.
- Substitute values:
EMF = (1.20 T) × (0.800 m) × (5.00 m/s).
- Multiplication: 1.20 × 0.800 = 0.960.
- 0.960 × 5.00 = 4.80 Volts.
Problem 3 (AC Circuit RMS Voltage):

An alternating electrical wall outlet provides a sinusoidal AC voltage with a peak voltage of Vpeak = 170 Volts. Calculate the root-mean-square (RMS) voltage of this AC supply.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
- Formula: Vrms = Vpeak / sqrt(2).
- sqrt(2) approximately equal to 1.414.
- Vrms = 170 V / 1.414 approximately equal to 120 Volts.
