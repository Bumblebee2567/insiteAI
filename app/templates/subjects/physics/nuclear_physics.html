Introduction to Nuclear Physics

Nuclear physics is the branch of physics that studies the constituents, structure, and interactions of atomic nuclei. An atomic nucleus consists of protons (positively charged) and neutrons (electrically neutral), collectively referred to as nucleons. The nucleus is held together by the strong nuclear force, one of the four fundamental forces of nature, which overcomes the mutual electrostatic repulsion between protons at extremely short distances.

Core Conceptual Principle:
Mass and energy are interchangeable via Einstein's mass-energy equivalence equation (E = mc2). The mass of a stable atomic nucleus is always slightly less than the sum of the individual rest masses of its constituent unbound protons and neutrons.
02

Mass Defect and Nuclear Binding Energy

The difference between the total mass of the separate constituent nucleons and the actual measured mass of the unified atomic nucleus is known as the mass defect (Δm).

  • Mass Defect Equation:
    Mass Defect = (Number of Protons × Proton Mass + Number of Neutrons × Neutron Mass) - Nucleus Mass
    (Δm = Z × mp + N × mn - mnucleus).
  • Nuclear Binding Energy (Eb): The total mechanical work or energy required to completely dismantle a nucleus into its constituent unbound protons and neutrons:
    Binding Energy = Mass Defect × Speed of Light squared (Eb = Δm × c2).
  • Binding Energy per Nucleon: The total binding energy divided by the mass number (A). Higher binding energy per nucleon indicates greater nuclear stability. Iron-56 (56Fe) possesses one of the highest binding energies per nucleon in nature.
03

Radioactive Decay Laws and Half-Life

Unstable atomic nuclei spontaneously transform into more stable configurations by emitting radiation, a statistical process known as radioactive decay.

  • Exponential Decay Law: The number of undecayed radioactive nuclei (N) remaining at any time t decreases exponentially:
    Remaining Nuclei = Initial Nuclei × e-(decay constant × time) (N(t) = N0 × e-λt),
    where λ is the radioactive decay constant.
  • Half-Life (T1/2): The time required for exactly half of a given quantity of radioactive nuclei to decay:
    Half-Life = Natural Logarithm of 2 / Decay Constant (T1/2 = ln(2) / λ ≈ 0.693 / λ).
  • Activity (A): The rate of radioactive decay measured in Becquerels (Bq, decays per second):
    Activity = Decay Constant × Number of Undecayed Nuclei (A = λ × N).
04

Types of Radioactive Decay

Natural radioactivity primarily manifests in three distinct forms of radiation:

  • Alpha (α) Decay: Emission of an alpha particle (a helium-4 nucleus, 42He). The atomic number decreases by 2 and the mass number decreases by 4 (AZX → A-4Z-2Y + 42He).
  • Beta (β) Decay:
    • Beta-Minus (β-): A neutron transforms into a proton, emitting an energetic electron and an electron antineutrino (AZX → AZ+1Y + e- + v̄e).
    • Beta-Plus (β+): A proton transforms into a neutron, emitting a positron and an electron neutrino (AZX → AZ-1Y + e+ + ve).
  • Gamma (γ) Decay: Emission of high-frequency electromagnetic photons from an excited nucleus transitioning to a lower energy state, leaving atomic number (Z) and mass number (A) unchanged.
05

Nuclear Fission and Nuclear Fusion

Nuclear reactions release massive amounts of energy by transforming mass into energy:

  • Nuclear Fission: The splitting of a heavy, unstable nucleus (such as Uranium-235) into two smaller, lighter nuclei upon absorbing a neutron, releasing several free neutrons and immense thermal energy. This powers nuclear power reactors and atomic weapons.
  • Nuclear Fusion: The combining of two light atomic nuclei (such as hydrogen isotopes deuterium and tritium) to form a heavier nucleus (helium), releasing even greater energy per nucleon. Nuclear fusion is the primary energy source powering the Sun and stars.

Exhaustive Worked Exam Examples

Problem 1 (Radioactive Decay and Half-Life):

A radioactive sample contains 1.00 × 1012 active nuclei of an isotope with a half-life of 5.00 hours. Calculate: (a) the decay constant (λ) of the isotope in s-1, and (b) the number of undecayed nuclei remaining after 15.0 hours.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
(a) Decay Constant (λ):
- Convert half-life to seconds: T1/2 = 5.00 hours × 3600 s/hour = 18,000 seconds.
- Formula: λ = 0.693 / T1/2 = 0.693 / 18,000 s ≈ 3.85 × 10-5 s-1.

(b) Remaining Nuclei after 15.0 Hours:
- 15.0 hours represents exactly 3 half-lives (15.0 / 5.00 = 3).
- Formula: N(t) = N0 × (1 / 2)n, where n = 3.
- N = (1.00 × 1012) × (1 / 2)3 = (1.00 × 1012) × 0.125 = 1.25 × 1011 nuclei.
Problem 2 (Mass Defect and Binding Energy):

Calculate the total nuclear binding energy of a Helium-4 nucleus (42He), given that its nuclear mass is 4.00150 u. (Mass of proton mp = 1.00728 u, mass of neutron mn = 1.00866 u, 1 u = 931.5 MeV/c2).

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
- Helium-4 has 2 protons (Z = 2) and 2 neutrons (N = 2).
- Total mass of unbonded nucleons: Δmtotal = (2 × 1.00728 u) + (2 × 1.00866 u) = 2.01456 + 2.01732 = 4.03188 u.
- Mass defect (Δm): Δm = 4.03188 u - 4.00150 u = 0.03038 u.
- Convert mass defect to binding energy: Eb = 0.03038 u × 931.5 MeV/u ≈ 28.30 MeV.
Problem 3 (Alpha Decay Reaction Balance):

Uranium-238 (23892U) undergoes alpha decay to form Thorium (Th). Write the complete nuclear decay equation and determine the atomic number (Z) and mass number (A) of the resulting daughter nucleus.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
- Alpha decay equation: 23892U → AZTh + 42He.
- Conservation of mass number (A): 238 = A + 4 &implies; A = 234.
- Conservation of atomic number (Z): 92 = Z + 2 &implies; Z = 90.
- Complete Balanced Equation:
23892U → 23490Th + 42He (Thorium-234 daughter nucleus formed).
