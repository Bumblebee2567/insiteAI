Introduction to Periodic and Oscillatory Motion

Oscillatory motion is motion that repeats itself regularly over a fixed time interval. Examples span across all domains of physics: the swinging of a grandfather clock pendulum, vibrating guitar strings, alternating electrical currents, and atomic vibrations in crystal lattices. Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM) is the most fundamental and mathematically tractable type of oscillation, characterized by a linear restoring force directed toward a stable equilibrium position.

Core Conceptual Principle:
For any system undergoing simple harmonic motion, the net restoring force acting on the object is directly proportional to its displacement from equilibrium and is always directed opposite to that displacement (Hooke's Law form).
02

Kinematics of Simple Harmonic Motion

The position x of an object undergoing simple harmonic motion as a function of time t can be modeled using sinusoidal functions:

  • Displacement Equation:
    Position = Amplitude × cosine(Angular Frequency × Time + Phase Constant)
    x(t) = A × cos(omega * t + phi), where A is the maximum displacement (amplitude).
  • Velocity Equation: Obtained by taking the first time derivative of displacement:
    Velocity = - Amplitude × Angular Frequency × sine(Angular Frequency × Time + Phase Constant)
    v(t) = -A * omega * sin(omega * t + phi). Maximum speed occurs as the object passes through equilibrium (vmax = A * omega).
  • Acceleration Equation: Obtained by taking the second time derivative of displacement:
    Acceleration = - Amplitude × (Angular Frequency squared) × cosine(Angular Frequency × Time + Phase Constant)
    a(t) = -A * omega2 * cos(omega * t + phi) = -omega2 * x(t). Maximum acceleration occurs at maximum displacement (amax = A * omega2).
  • Frequency and Period:
    • Angular frequency: omega = 2 × pi × frequency (measured in rad/s).
    • Period (T): The duration of one complete oscillation cycle, T = 1 / frequency = (2 × pi) / omega.
03

Restoring Forces and Differential Equations of SHM

Applying Newton's Second Law to a system experiencing a linear restoring force (F = -k * x) yields the defining differential equation of simple harmonic motion:

Defining Differential Equation:
Mass × (Second Time Derivative of Position) + Stiffness Constant × Position = 0
m * (d2x / dt2) + kx = 0 → Rewrite as (d2x / dt2) + (k / m) * x = 0.
This proves that angular frequency for any linear harmonic oscillator is fundamentally determined by the ratio of the restoring stiffness to the inertia: omega = square root of (Stiffness / Inertia).

Common Oscillatory Systems

Different physical systems achieve simple harmonic motion through different restoring mechanisms. Below are the primary configurations studied in classical mechanics:

System Type Restoring Mechanism Angular Frequency (omega) Period Formula (T)
Mass-Spring System Elastic spring force (F = -kx) square root of (k / m) 2 × pi × square root of (m / k)
Simple Pendulum Component of gravity along arc (mg sin theta ≈ mg theta) square root of (g / L) 2 × pi × square root of (L / g)
Physical Pendulum Gravitational torque about pivot axis square root of (m × g × d / I) 2 × pi × square root of (I / (m × g × d))
Torsion Pendulum Restoring torque from twisted wire or fiber (tau = -kappa * theta) square root of (kappa / I) 2 × pi × square root of (I / kappa)
05

Energy Conservation in Simple Harmonic Motion

In an ideal conservative harmonic oscillator without friction or air resistance, mechanical energy is continuously and seamlessly converted back and forth between kinetic energy and potential energy, while the total mechanical energy remains strictly constant.

  • Kinetic Energy (K): Energy associated with the moving mass:
    Kinetic Energy = (1/2) × Mass × Velocity squared = (1/2) * m * vmax2 * sin2(omega * t + phi).
  • Potential Energy (U): Energy stored in the elastic spring or gravitational field:
    Potential Energy = (1/2) × Spring Constant × Position squared = (1/2) * k * A2 * cos2(omega * t + phi).
  • Total Mechanical Energy (E): At any arbitrary point in the oscillation cycle, total energy equals the sum of kinetic and potential energy, which is also equal to the maximum potential energy at the turning points (Amplitude A):
    Total Mechanical Energy = (1/2) × Spring Constant × Amplitude squared = (1/2) * k * A2.
06

Damped, Driven Oscillations & Resonance

Real-world oscillators experience dissipative non-conservative forces (such as friction or air resistance) that remove mechanical energy over time.

  • Damped Oscillations: When a damping force proportional to velocity (Fdamp = -b * v) acts on a system, its mechanical energy and oscillation amplitude decay exponentially over time: x(t) = A × e-(b / 2m)t × cos(omega' * t + phi), where omega' is the reduced damped angular frequency.
  • Driven Oscillations & Resonance: If a periodic external driving force (F0 × cos(omegad * t)) is applied to an oscillator, the system eventually oscillates at the driving frequency omegad. When the driving frequency matches the natural resonant frequency of the system (omegad = omega0), the amplitude of oscillation reaches a dramatic maximum, a phenomenon known as resonance.

Exhaustive Worked Exam Examples

Problem 1 (Mass-Spring System Mechanics):

A block of mass m = 0.500 kg is attached to a horizontal ideal spring with a spring constant k = 200 N/m. The block is pulled to a maximum displacement amplitude of A = 0.100 m from equilibrium and released from rest. Calculate: (a) the maximum speed of the block, (b) the maximum magnitude of acceleration, and (c) the total mechanical energy of the system.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
(a) Maximum Speed (vmax):
- Angular frequency omega = square root of (k / m) = square root of (200 / 0.500) = square root of (400) = 20.0 rad/s.
- vmax = A × omega = (0.100 m) × (20.0 rad/s) = 2.00 m/s.

(b) Maximum Acceleration (amax):
- amax = A × omega2 = (0.100 m) × (20.0 rad/s)2 = (0.100)(400) = 40.0 m/s2.

(c) Total Mechanical Energy (E):
- E = (1/2) × k × A2 = (1/2) × (200 N/m) × (0.100 m)2 = 100 × 0.0100 = 1.00 Joule.
Problem 2 (Simple Pendulum Frequency & Period):

A simple pendulum has a length L = 1.50 m and is located in a laboratory where the gravitational field strength is g = 9.81 m/s2. Calculate: (a) the period of oscillation, and (b) the frequency of oscillation in Hertz.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
(a) Period (T):
- Formula: T = 2 * pi * square root of (L / g).
- T = 2 * pi * square root of (1.50 / 9.81) = 2 × pi × square root of (0.1529) = 2 × pi × 0.3910 ≈ 2.46 seconds.

(b) Frequency (f):
- Frequency = 1 / T = 1 / 2.46 s ≈ 0.407 Hz.
Problem 3 (Energy Conservation in SHM):

For the mass-spring system in Problem 1 (m = 0.500 kg, k = 200 N/m, A = 0.100 m), calculate the kinetic energy and potential energy of the system when the block is at a displacement of x = 0.060 m from equilibrium.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:
- Potential Energy (U) at x = 0.060 m:
U = (1/2) × k × x2 = (1/2) × (200 N/m) × (0.060 m)2 = 100 × 0.0036 = 0.360 Joules.

- Kinetic Energy (K) at x = 0.060 m:
- By conservation of mechanical energy: E = K + U implies K = E - U.
- Total mechanical energy E was calculated as 1.00 Joule.
- K = 1.00 J - 0.360 J = 0.640 Joules.
